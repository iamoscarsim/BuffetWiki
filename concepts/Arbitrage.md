# Arbitrage

"Arbitrage" in Buffett's usage means **risk arbitrage** — buying the stock of a company
that is the subject of a publicly announced deal (merger, tender, liquidation,
spin-off) to capture the spread between the market price and the deal's expected
payout. Berkshire uses it as a high-returning alternative to cash and short-term
bonds — "a parking place for money" — when it cannot find equities or businesses to buy.

In the [[1987]] letter Buffett notes Berkshire has "practiced arbitrage on an
opportunistic basis for decades" and estimates it has **averaged at least 25% pre-tax
annual returns** from it — but cautions that "a really bad experience or two… could
change the figures dramatically." His discipline: only a few commitments a year, only
**large, publicly announced** deals, and never participating where greenmailers are
putting a company "in play" or "betting on the come." (His one $50M-plus arbitrage
position at year-end 1987 was Allegis.)

The [[1988]] letter contains the fuller essay, including Buffett's four questions for
any arbitrage situation — (1) How likely is the promised event to occur? (2) How long
will your money be tied up? (3) What chance is there that something better surfaces (a
competing bid)? (4) What happens if the event does *not* occur? — and the long-run
historical record (Berkshire and predecessors averaging ~25% before tax). Arbitrage is
the practical complement to [[Financial Strength]]: a fortress balance sheet lets
Berkshire commit to deals others can't, and to wait out the ones that wobble.

## Evolution
- [1987] Stated as a decades-long practice averaging ~25% pre-tax; disciplined rules
  (few, large, announced deals; no greenmail plays). Allegis the year-end position.
- [1988] **The full essay.** A record year — **$78M pre-tax on ~$147M** average funds.
  The **four questions** for any arbitrage: (1) How likely is the announced event to
  happen? (2) How long will money be tied up? (3) Could something better transpire (a
  competing bid)? (4) What happens if the event does *not* occur? Two illustrative
  stories: the 1954 **Rockwood** cocoa-bean tender (Buffett at 24 at Graham-Newman;
  architect Jay Pritzker; "sometimes there is more to stock valuation than P/E ratios")
  and the **Arcata/redwood** saga (a base 15% return plus a large windfall when the
  disputed government timber claim settled). The big position was **RJR Nabisco**
  ($281.8M → $64M gain as KKR won), constrained because Salomon's bidding involvement
  walled off Buffett and Munger. Berkshire's edge: few/large/announced deals only,
  "we just read the newspapers." For 1989 Buffett expects to sit out — takeover
  "excesses" (Herb Stein: "If something can't go on forever, it will end"). The record
  refutes [[Efficient Market Theory]].
- [1989] **Sat out**, as predicted. Even holding cash, Buffett declined to arbitrage the
  era's takeover deals because they "made no economic sense" — "arbitraging such deals
  comes too close to playing the greater-fool game" (Ray DeVoe: "Fools rush in where
  angels fear to trade"). Berkshire will still arbitrage "from time to time — sometimes on
  a large scale — but only when we like the odds."
- [1992] **Arbitrage converts to investment.** Buffett began buying **[[General Dynamics]]**
  purely as a Dutch-tender arbitrage — "expecting to tender our holdings for a small profit,"
  one of "perhaps a half-dozen" such commitments "in the last few years." But studying CEO
  [[Bill Anders]]' capital-return strategy, "I dumped my arbitrage thoughts and decided that
  Berkshire should become a long-term investor." The tender's swollen trading volume let
  Berkshire accumulate 14% of the post-tender shares in a month — a rare case of an arbitrage
  parking-place becoming a permanent holding.
