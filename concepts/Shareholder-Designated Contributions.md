# Shareholder-Designated Contributions

A distinctive Berkshire institution, launched in [[1981]] and conceived by
[[Charlie Munger]]: rather than have management or directors direct the company's
charitable giving, Berkshire let *each shareholder* designate the recipients of a
per-share amount of corporate contributions. It is a direct expression of
[[Owner Orientation and Candor]] — treating owners' money as genuinely theirs.

The 1981 debut drew "extraordinary enthusiasm": of 932,206 eligible shares, **95.6%
participated**, directing **$1.78M to about 675 charities**. Buffett needles the
"father-knows-best school of corporate governance," noting not one shareholder asked
management to decide for them, nor to match directors' personal pet causes (a
"popular, proliferating and non-publicized policy at many large corporations"). The
program ran for years until Berkshire ended it in 2003 after controversy and
boycotts directed at some subsidiaries over designated recipients — a later
evolution this page will record when that letter is reached.

## Evolution
- [1981] Program launched (Munger's idea); 95.6% of eligible shares participated;
  $1.78M to ~675 charities. Only suspended in years when Berkshire's tax position
  wouldn't yield a full deduction.
- [1982] 95.8% participation, at $1/share (down from $2). A consolidated tax position
  from the pending Blue Chip merger expected to enlarge the contribution base.
- [1986] ~97% of eligible shares participated; $4M to 1,934 charities.
- [1987] **97.2%** participation; **$4.9M to 2,050 charities**. Buffett contrasts the
  program with the then-common corporate practice of matching *directors'* gifts: "When
  A takes money from B to give to C and A is a legislator, the process is called
  taxation. But when A is an officer or director of a corporation, it is called
  philanthropy" — giving should reflect *owners'* preferences, not management's.
- [1989] **96.9%** participation; **$5.9M** to 2,550 charities.
- [1990] **97.3%** participation; **$5.8M** to 2,600 charities. Buffett adds that operating
  managers separately give ~$1.5M/yr to *local* charities (with commensurate business
  benefit), but neither managers nor parent officers use Berkshire funds for "broad national
  programs or charitable activities of special personal interest to them" — "if your
  employees, including your CEO, wish to give to their alma maters… they should use their
  own money, not yours."
- [1991] **97.7%** participation; **$6.8M to 2,630 charities**. Buffett repeats the
  registration caveat (shares must be in the actual owner's name, not a broker/bank nominee,
  by August 31 to be eligible) and reiterates that operating managers separately give ~$1.5M/yr
  (including merchandise) to local charities, while Berkshire funds are never used for
  managers' or officers' personal causes.
- [1993] **97%** participation; **$9.4M to 3,110 charities**. After polling owners (a few
  opposed the whole idea; most praised its tax efficiency and value in teaching children to
  give), Buffett **raised the per-share amount from $8 to $10**. He published the category
  breakdown — churches/synagogues, colleges, K-12 schools, arts, social services, hospitals,
  health — as a window into "what people choose to give money to when… free of pressure," noting
  corporate programs "almost never allow gifts to churches and synagogues," and that owners gave
  on **both sides of the abortion debate** (130 gifts to pro-availability groups, 30 to opposed).
  The program is cheap to run — one borrowed National Indemnity employee for two months — and
  Berkshire's "entire corporate overhead is less than half the size of our charitable
  contributions" (Charlie insists on noting $1.4M of the $4.9M overhead is the jet).
- [1994] **95.7%** participation; **$10.4M to 3,300 charities**. Buffett reiterates the strict
  registration/deadline rules ("we don't make exceptions when requirements aren't met").
- [1995] **96.3%** participation; **$11.6M to 3,600 charities**. Two friends with substantial
  holdings missed the deadline and were denied — "we can't make exceptions for some shareholders
  while refusing to make them for others." Note: the new **[[Class B Shares]]** will *not* be
  eligible for the program (only Class A is).
- [1996] **97.2%** participation; **$13.3M to 3,910 charities**. Buffett notes (in the
  intrinsic-value table) that designated contributions are charged against operating earnings
  "though we view them as a shareholder benefit rather than as an expense." The registration/
  deadline caveat is repeated ("distressing to Charlie and me" to reject late replies).
- [1997] **97.7%** participation; **$15.4M to 3,830 charities** — and a **17-year cumulative total
  of $113.1M** directed by shareholders. Separately, Berkshire's *subsidiaries* (which keep their
  pre-acquisition philanthropic patterns) gave **$8.1M** (incl. $4.4M in-kind). The same
  own-name/August-31 registration rules apply.
- [1998] **97.5%** participation; **$16.9M** designated; **18-year cumulative $130M**. Subsidiaries
  separately gave **$12.5M** (incl. $2.0M in-kind). 6,106 shareholders designated 3,880 charities.
  Standard registration/deadline caveat repeated ("Designations received after the due date will not
  be honored").
- [1999] **97.3%** participation; **$17.2M** designated; **19-year cumulative $147M**. Subsidiaries
  separately gave **$13.8M** (incl. $2.5M in-kind). Same registration/deadline rules.
- [2000] **97%** participation; **$16.9M** designated to 3,660 charities; **20-year cumulative
  $164M**. Subsidiaries separately gave **$18.3M** (incl. $3M in-kind). *(The program would be
  terminated in 2003 after subsidiary boycotts over some designated recipients — a later letter
  records the end.)*
- [2001] **97%** participation; **$16.7M** designated to 3,550 charities; cumulative **$181M**.
  Subsidiaries separately gave $19.2M. Buffett addresses the recurring **Planned Parenthood**
  complaints head-on: "It's not Berkshire, but rather its owners who are making charitable
  decisions" — and Berkshire follows them, "whether they designate Planned Parenthood or Metro Right
  to Life," so long as the recipient has 501(c)(3) status. *(The boycott pressure that ends the
  program two years later is already visible here.)*
- [2003] **The program is terminated.** Over 1981–2002 it disbursed **$197M** ("we were the only
  major public company that offered such a program… Charlie and I were proud of it"). The end came
  "**reluctantly**… because of controversy over the abortion issue": boycotts of subsidiary products
  had begun to hit the **independent associates of [[The Pampered Chef]]** — people who "trusted
  us… but… had [no] voice in Berkshire decision-making" and "suffered serious losses of income."
  Buffett refused to limit owners' giving choices, so he ended the program instead: "We see nothing
  charitable in harming decent, hard-working people just so we… can gain some minor tax
  efficiencies." Berkshire now makes **no parent-level contributions** (subsidiaries keep their own
  pre-acquisition philanthropy, funded personally by former owners for their own past pet causes).
