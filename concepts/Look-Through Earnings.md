# Look-Through Earnings

The idea that a shareholder's true economic earnings include not just dividends
received but their proportional share of an investee's *retained* earnings — the
portion the business reinvests on the owner's behalf. Reported accounting earnings
capture only dividends; "look-through" earnings add the undistributed remainder.

The concept is born in the [[1978]] letter through [[SAFECO]]: Berkshire received
only ~18% of SAFECO's earnings as dividends, but Buffett insists the retained 82%
is "just as real in terms of eventual benefit to us as the amount distributed,"
and at a company that reinvests well "may well eventually have a value to
shareholders greater than 100 cents on the dollar." He links it to a symmetry
principle: Berkshire is happy for its *wholly-owned* businesses to retain earnings
when they can be reinvested at attractive rates, so it should feel the same about
partly-owned companies with even better reinvestment prospects.

Buffett later formalizes this into an explicit "look-through earnings" figure he
reports and argues should be the real measure of Berkshire's progress. This page
tracks that development.

## Evolution
- [1978] Concept introduced (without the later label) via SAFECO: investees'
  retained earnings are real value to the owner, sometimes worth >100¢ on the
  dollar.
- [1979] Sharpened into the **dollar test**: "We have faith that the managements of
  these companies will... translate a dollar retained by them into a dollar or more
  of subsequent market value for us." Retained earnings attributable to Berkshire's
  equity stakes "now come to a very substantial number."
- [1980] The fullest statement yet ("Non-Controlled Ownership Earnings"). Walks
  through the three accounting regimes; notes that undistributed earnings of
  Berkshire's <20% holdings **exceeded total reported operating earnings** — only
  "less than half of our earnings 'iceberg'" shows above the surface. Value depends
  on the *use* of retained earnings, not the ownership %: **"if a tree grows in a
  forest partially owned by us... we still own part of the tree."** Notes selling
  the equities for tax-free bonds would lift reported earnings by $30M+ — a swap
  that "tempts us not at all."
- [1981] Reaffirmed: undistributed "ownership earnings" now exceed reported
  operating earnings and will continue to. Four holdings alone (GEICO, General Foods,
  R.J. Reynolds, Washington Post) projected to contribute well over $35M of unrecorded
  earnings in 1982. Buffett notes the accounting blind spot "diminish[es] the utility
  of our annual return on equity calculation." Also: lack of control is "often... an
  economic plus" since marketable-security mistakes can be exited faster than
  controlled-subsidiary mistakes.
- [1982] Reframed as **"economic" vs. "accounting" earnings**. GEICO: $3.5M dividends
  counted, $23M of retained share ignored — "if GEICO had earned an additional $100
  million — and retained it all — our reported earnings would have been unchanged."
  Unrecorded earnings of four holdings topped $40M (> total reported earnings).
  "Accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation." Credits
  "St. Offset": over-achieving holdings (a retained $1 → $2+ of market value) more
  than cover laggards. This is why Buffett retires the [[Return on Equity]] yardstick
  this year.
- [1987] The **"accounting schizophrenia"** stated plainly via the three permanent
  holdings ([[Capital Cities]]/ABC, [[GEICO]], [[Washington Post]]): worth >$2B but
  delivering only **$11M of reported (dividend) earnings** vs. **>$100M** of Berkshire's
  share of their actual earnings. GAAP shows these holdings' up-to-date market *value*
  on the balance sheet but not their *earnings* in the income account; for controlled
  companies it does the opposite (full earnings, frozen asset values). Buffett's
  response: "ignore GAAP figures and focus solely on the future earning power of both
  our controlled and non-controlled businesses." Vivid per-share proof of compounding:
  WaPo bought 1973 at $5.63 now earns $10.30/sh after tax; GEICO bought at ~$6.67 earns
  $9.01/sh — "Mr. Market has proven to be a mighty good friend."
- [1989] **"Look-through earnings" formally named** as the best measure of Berkshire's
  fundamental earning power — "our share of the operating earnings retained by our
  investees… append[ed] to our own reported operating earnings, excluding capital gains
  in both instances." The 1989 numbers: ~$45M of dividends received vs. ~**$212M** share
  of investees' *retained* earnings (had it been distributed, operating earnings would
  have been ~$500M, not the ~$300M reported). Buffett argues the retained portion may be
  *more* valuable — "a-bird-in-the-bush-may-be-worth-two-in-the-hand" — because
  owner-oriented managers like Goizueta redeploy it well. **The goal stated**: for
  intrinsic value to compound ~15%/yr, look-through earnings must grow at about the same
  pace.
- [1990] The calculation shown step by step: ~$250M of investees' retained earnings,
  less ~$30M hypothetical dividend tax, plus $371M reported operating earnings = **~$590M
  of look-through earnings**. The extreme illustration: Berkshire's 17% of **[[Capital Cities]]**
  earned $83M, of which GAAP recorded only ~$530K. "We care not whether the auditors hear
  a tree fall in the forest; we do care who owns the tree and what's next done with it."
  Buffett pairs the essay with [[Ben Graham]]'s unpublished 1936 accounting satire as a
  warning that reported "earnings can be as pliable as putty when a charlatan heads the
  company." He flags that 1991 look-through will fall (Gillette preferred converting; media
  earnings declining).
- [1991] **The predicted decline arrives:** look-through earnings **fell 14%** to ~**$516M**
  (from ~$602M) — the first drop. Buffett attributes it to four forces, two foreseen and two
  not: (foreseen) the secular **media** earnings decline and the **[[Gillette]] preferred
  conversion** (the prior $45M after-tax preferred dividend exceeded the 1991 blend of three
  months' preferred + nine months' common look-through); (unforeseen) a **[[Wells Fargo]]
  break-even** (dividends offset by negative retained earnings) and lower insurance profits.
  The mechanics: ~$230M Berkshire share of investees' undistributed earnings, less ~$30M
  hypothetical tax, plus $316M reported operating earnings = ~$516M. Buffett extends the idea
  to **individual investors**: each should build a portfolio (a personal "company")
  maximizing *its own* look-through earnings "a decade or so from now," forcing a focus on
  long-term business prospects over stock prices — "to put runs on the scoreboard one must
  watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."
- [1992] Look-through earnings rebounded to **$604M** (≈ the 1990 level), and Buffett turns
  the metric into a **concrete decade target**: to compound intrinsic value at 15%, they
  "will need to grow to more than **$1.8 billion by the year 2000**" — "when we allocate
  capital today, we are thinking about what will maximize look-through earnings in 2000." He
  guards the long view against abuse, though: managers who "fail to deliver for extended
  periods and blame it on their long-term focus" deserve the same suspicion as those who
  juice short-term numbers ("It must come sometimes to jam *today*"). The 1992 build:
  $298M Berkshire share of investees' undistributed earnings, less $42M hypothetical tax,
  plus $348M reported operating earnings.
- [1993] Look-through earnings rose to **$856M**; the 2000 target ticked up to **$1.85B**
  (more shares outstanding after Dexter). Buffett pledges accountability — "we've criticized…
  shooting the arrow… and *then* painting the target… We will instead risk embarrassment by
  painting first and shooting later" — while refusing marginal deals just to deploy cash
  ("there's no use running if you're on the wrong road"). The **[[Capital Cities]] one-third
  sale** is offered as proof that look-through *understates* value: over eight years the shares'
  retained earnings (taxed at the look-through method's 14%) came to $152M, yet the sale alone
  produced a **$297M after-tax gain** — "we expect such pleasant outcomes to recur." Note: the
  hypothetical-tax rate used is now stated as **14%**, the rate Berkshire actually pays on
  dividends received.
- [1994] Look-through earnings reached **$1,030M**, leaving Berkshire "modestly ahead of
  schedule" toward the **$1.85B-by-2000** goal — though Buffett cautions the figure is flattered
  by abnormally high super-cat earnings. New investee contributors: **[[American Express]]**
  ($25M), Gannett ($4M), PNC ($10M); [[Wells Fargo]] jumped to $73M and [[Coca-Cola]] to $116M.
  The build: $492M Berkshire share of investees' undistributed earnings, less $68M (14%)
  hypothetical tax, plus $606M reported operating earnings.
- [1996] Look-through earnings reached **$1,522M**: $954M reported operating earnings + **$661M**
  Berkshire share of major investees' undistributed earnings − **$93M** hypothetical tax (at the
  14% rate Berkshire pays on dividends). The eight itemized investee contributions: [[Coca-Cola]]
  $180M, [[American Express]] $132M, [[Wells Fargo]] $84M, [[Freddie Mac]] $77M, [[Gillette]]
  $73M, [[Disney]] $50M, [[McDonald's]] $38M, [[Washington Post]] $27M. Buffett reiterates that
  reported earnings are "a poor measure of economic progress at Berkshire" because they count only
  dividends, and that investees' retained earnings are "more valuable to us than the portion paid
  out" since they reinvest "at high rates of return." The closing investor lesson: "our
  look-through earnings have grown at a good clip over the years, and our stock price has risen
  correspondingly … **Had those gains in earnings not materialized, there would have been little
  increase in Berkshire's value.**"
- [1997] Look-through earnings jumped to **$1,930M**: $1,292M reported operating earnings + **$743M**
  investee retained share − **$105M** hypothetical tax (14%). The seven itemized contributions:
  [[Coca-Cola]] $216M, [[American Express]] $161M, [[Wells Fargo]] $103M, [[Freddie Mac]] $86M,
  [[Gillette]] $82M, [[Disney]] $65M, [[Washington Post]] $30M. (No [[McDonald's]] line this year.)
  The same rationale recurs verbatim — dividends are "a poor measure," and investees' retained
  earnings are "more valuable to us than the portion paid out" because they reinvest "at high rates
  of return."
- [1999] Look-through earnings essentially flat at **$1,926M**: $1,318M reported + **$707M**
  investee retained − **$99M** tax (14%). The seven contributions: [[American Express]] $228M
  (now the largest), [[Coca-Cola]] $144M, [[Freddie Mac]] $127M, [[Wells Fargo]] $108M, [[Gillette]]
  $53M, [[Washington Post]] $30M, **M&T Bank** $17M (the former [[First Empire State]]). The same
  rationale recurs verbatim; the year's lesson, though, is that look-through *understates* the pain
  when investees' **stock prices** fall faster than their retained earnings — the equity portfolio's
  slump (not look-through) drove Buffett's worst year.
- [2000] Look-through earnings rose to **$2,415M**: $1,779M reported + **$740M** investee retained −
  **$104M** tax (14%). The seven contributions: [[American Express]] $265M (largest), [[Coca-Cola]]
  $160M, [[Wells Fargo]] $117M, [[Freddie Mac]] $106M (the year Berkshire **sold nearly all** of it,
  to 0.3%), [[Gillette]] $51M, M&T Bank $23M, [[Washington Post]] $18M. As the operating businesses
  multiply (eight acquisitions), reported operating earnings ($1,779M) now dwarf the investee
  retained share — a reminder that Berkshire's center of gravity is shifting from a stock portfolio
  toward wholly-owned businesses.
- [[2019]] **The idea returns, renamed "[[The Power of Retained Earnings]]."** After years out of the
  letters, Buffett revives the look-through argument as a full essay, grounding it in **Edgar Lawrence
  Smith**'s 1924 book and **Keynes**'s "compound interest operating in favour of a sound industrial
  investment." A table of the **10 largest stock holdings** shows the modern split: Berkshire's share of
  **$3,798M of dividends** (what GAAP reports) vs. **$8,332M of retained earnings** (the unrecognized,
  compounding value). He reaffirms Owner Principle 6 — the group will deliver capital gains "at least
  equal to" the retained earnings — now often via investees' **buybacks** (the [[American Express]]
  12.6%→18.7% case). "**Mr. Smith got it right.**"
