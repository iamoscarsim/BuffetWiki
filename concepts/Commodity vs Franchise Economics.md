# Commodity vs Franchise Economics

One of Buffett's central distinctions: businesses selling **undifferentiated
commodities** in capital-intensive industries are condemned to poor returns, while
businesses with a **franchise** (a product or service customers want, with no close
substitute and pricing power) can earn high returns on little capital.

The [[1978]] letter gives the textbook statement using [[Berkshire Hathaway Textiles]]:
"producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses
must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real
shortage. As long as excess productive capacity exists, prices tend to reflect
direct operating costs rather than capital employed." Even bargain-priced equipment
and diligent management can't escape this — because competitors are "just as
diligently doing the same thing," any efficiency gain is competed away to the
customer.

The franchise side of the contrast is embodied by [[See's Candies]] (pricing power,
high returns, little incremental capital). The distinction underlies Buffett's
whole preference for businesses with "tailwinds" and durable moats, and he sharpens
it into the explicit "franchise vs. business" framework in later letters (notably
1991).

## Evolution
- [1978] Textbook statement via textiles: excess capacity forces commodity prices
  to operating cost, not cost of capital, guaranteeing inadequate returns.
- [1982] Generalized into a rule: "persistent over-capacity without administered
  prices (or costs) equals poor profitability." Differentiation works for candy bars
  (bought by brand) but not sugar ("a cup of coffee with cream and C&H sugar,
  please"). The only commodity-business survivors are those with "a cost advantage
  that is both wide and sustainable" (e.g. [[GEICO]]). Insurance is "a textbook case"
  — capacity created instantly "by capital plus an underwriter's willingness to sign
  his name." "Nothing fails like success" (cyclical over-expansion).
- [1985] The textile shutdown supplies the definitive case (see
  [[Berkshire Hathaway Textiles]]): in a commodity business, cost-cutting capex is
  "illusory" because rivals match it and savings pass to customers (the parade
  "tiptoe" trap). Burlington Industries spent $3B over 21 years and left shareholders
  worse off. Lesson: "a good managerial record... is far more a function of what
  business boat you get into than... how effectively you row."
- [1986] Two extensions. The insurance "corn" metaphor sharpens the commodity point —
  calls for pricing "statesmanship" are as futile as asking corn growers to market
  with restraint: "what's needed is not more statesmen, but less corn" (raising
  capital just plants more corn). And the **tax-incidence** corollary: who keeps a
  corporate tax cut depends on franchise strength. Strong unregulated franchises (and
  many of Berkshire's businesses) pocket the cut — "the most able brain surgeon or
  lawyer" won't cut fees because tax rates fell — while price-competitive industries
  (aluminum, autos, department stores) pass it through, the free market "regulating"
  after-tax profits much as a utility commission does.
- [1991] **The formal "franchise vs. business" definition** (in the media-economics essay).
  An **economic franchise** arises from a product or service that (1) is needed or desired,
  (2) is thought by customers to have **no close substitute**, and (3) is **not price-
  regulated** — proven by the company's ability "to regularly price its product or service
  aggressively and thereby to earn high rates of return on capital." Critically, a franchise
  "**can tolerate mis-management**": inept managers "may diminish a franchise's profitability,
  but they cannot inflict mortal damage." A **"business,"** by contrast, "earns exceptional
  profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply… is tight," and — unlike a
  franchise — "**can be killed by poor management**." Buffett places most operations on a
  spectrum between these poles ("weak franchises or strong businesses"). The essay's
  occasion is the migration of media properties *from* franchise *toward* business
  (see [[Evolving Views — Newspapers]]); it also explains why management quality matters far
  more for a [[USAir]]-type business than for a See's-type franchise.
