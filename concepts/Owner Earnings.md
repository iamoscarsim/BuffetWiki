# Owner Earnings

"Owner earnings" is Buffett's preferred measure of what a business is really worth to
its owner — introduced in the famous **Appendix to the [[1986]] letter**, "Purchase-
Price Accounting Adjustments and the 'Cash Flow' Fallacy." It is one of the most-
cited ideas in the whole corpus, a direct refinement of [[Intrinsic Business Value]]
and a corrective to both GAAP earnings and Wall Street "cash flow."

The definition:

> Owner earnings = **(a)** reported earnings, plus **(b)** depreciation, depletion,
> amortization, and certain other non-cash charges, **less (c)** the average annual
> capitalized expenditures for plant, equipment, etc. that the business requires to
> fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume (including any
> increment of working capital).

Buffett builds the case from his [[Scott Fetzer]] purchase. He prints two earnings
statements — "Company O" (old) and "Company N" (new) — that depict the *same*
business with identical sales, wages, taxes and cash generation, differing only in
accounting: Berkshire paid $315M for net assets carried at $172.4M, and the resulting
$142.6M of **purchase-price adjustments** (inventory and fixed-asset write-ups, plus
$24.3M of [[Economic Goodwill]]) loaded ~$11.6M of extra, non-tax-deductible
depreciation and amortization onto "Company N," dropping its reported net income from
$40.2M to $28.6M. Which column is "truth"? Neither, exactly — but their *owner
earnings* are identical, because (a) + (b) is the same in both and (c) is necessarily
the same. This is why Berkshire reports its operating units' earnings **before**
purchase-price adjustments: those figures sit closer to owner earnings than the GAAP
ones.

The sharp edge of the essay is its attack on the **"cash flow" fallacy**. Wall Street
brochures routinely tout (a) + (b) while ignoring (c) — implying a business is "the
commercial counterpart of the Pyramids, forever state-of-the-art, never needing to be
replaced." For most manufacturers, retailers, extractive firms and utilities, (c) is
always significant; only rare businesses (a bridge, a very long-lived gas field)
genuinely resemble the cash-flow picture. Buffett's cynical read: the metric is used
"to justify the unjustifiable" — to service junk-bond debt or rationalize foolish
stock prices. "Though dentists correctly claim that if you ignore your teeth they'll
go away, the same is not true for (c)."

Crucially, (c) is a *guess*, not a GAAP number — "vaguely right" beats "precisely
wrong" (Keynes). A business where required maintenance capex (c) merely equals
depreciation (b) reports honest GAAP earnings; one where **(c) exceeds (b)** — the
oil industry being his example — has GAAP earnings that *overstate* owner earnings.
[[See's Candies]] is the happy opposite case Buffett flags: capex runs only $0.5–1M
above depreciation, so See's owner earnings sit close to its reported earnings. The
accountants' job "is to record, not to evaluate"; the evaluation falls to investors
and managers, for whom accounting "is but an aid to business thinking, never a
substitute for it."

## Evolution
- [1986] Defined in the Appendix: owner earnings = (a) + (b) − (c); the Scott Fetzer
  "Company O vs. Company N" demonstration; the "cash flow" (a)+(b) fallacy; (c) as a
  necessary guess; "vaguely right rather than precisely wrong." See [[Economic Goodwill]]
  and the [[1983]] goodwill appendix for the companion essay on purchase premiums.
