# Insurance Float

Float is money an insurer holds that it does not own: premiums collected before
claims are paid, available for investment in the interim. It is the central reason
Buffett built Berkshire on insurance — a stream of investable funds that grows
with the business and, if underwriting breaks even or better, costs nothing or
less than nothing.

The [[1977]] letter does not yet use the word "float," but the concept is plainly
present. Buffett notes that growth in insurance reserves "produced by our large
gain in premium volume" drove insurance investments from $134.6M to $252.8M over
two years, and that reinsurance in particular "generates unusually high funds for
investment as a percentage of premium volume." The pairing is explicit: good
[[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]] produces both a profit *and* a growing pool
of other people's money to invest. The formal "float" framing, with its cost-of-
float accounting, is developed in later letters; this page tracks that evolution.

## Evolution
- [1977] Concept present without the term: premium growth swells investable
  reserves; reinsurance generates especially high investable funds per premium
  dollar.
- [1990] **The "cost of float" framework — the definitive treatment.** Buffett publishes
  a 24-year table (1967–1990) of the insurance group's underwriting loss against average
  float, deriving an annual "**cost of funds developed from insurance**" (underwriting
  loss ÷ average float). The verdict: Berkshire held ~**$1.6B** of float in 1990 at a
  ~**1.6%** cost, and developed funds **below the U.S. government's borrowing cost in 19
  of 24 years** (in profitable-underwriting years the cost is *less than zero*). The
  analytical payoff: an insurer creates value for owners only if its cost of float (plus
  a ~1-point "tax penalty" on indirect investment) is **below** alternative funding
  costs — "if the cost is *significantly* lower, the insurance business qualifies as a
  very valuable asset." [[GEICO]] (48%-owned) is the extreme: underwriting at a profit, it
  has a cost of float "considerably less than zero" — "policyholders, in aggregate, pay
  the company interest on the float." Two caveats: ultimate cost is unknown until all
  long-tail claims settle ("the fat lady has yet to gargle"), and shareholders bear a tax
  penalty. He also reframes the combined ratio: a better profitability measure is
  **underwriting loss vs. float developed**, and long-tail lines must be priced with a
  margin of safety ("aiming for 110–115 is business suicide"). The float that funds this
  is increasingly written through [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]].
- [1991] The cost-of-float table is extended to **25 years (1967–1991)**. A bad underwriting
  year (loss **$119.6M**, driven by super-cat and softer pricing) pushed the 1991 cost of
  funds up to **6.31%** — yet still **below the 7.40%** year-end government long-bond yield.
  Average float grew to ~**$1,895M**. The career scorecard: Berkshire's cost of funds beat the
  government's rate in **20 of 25 years**, "often by a wide margin." Buffett restates the
  doctrine that combined ratios "our own or the industry's" are "largely irrelevant" to
  Berkshire — "**what counts with us is… the cost of float**" — and gives the precise
  definition: float = loss reserves + loss-adjustment-expense reserves + unearned-premium
  reserves − agents' balances − prepaid acquisition costs − deferred reinsurance charges. He
  expects float to keep growing; "the challenge will be to garner these funds at a reasonable
  cost."
- [1992] Cost of float improved to **4.76%** on average float of **$2,290.4M** (up sharply
  from $1,895M), again under the **7.39%** government long-bond yield — Berkshire has now beaten
  the government's rate in **21 of 26 years**. Buffett states the bedrock test plainly: "If,
  on average, we didn't beat the Government's rate, there would be no economic reason for us
  to be in the business." Two operations wrote at *less-than-zero* cost (underwriting profits):
  [[National Indemnity Company|National Indemnity]]'s commercial-auto/general-liability book
  under Don Wurster, and the homestate operation under Rod Eldred; the bulk of the float now
  comes from [[Ajit Jain]]'s large reinsurance transactions, expected to grow it further.
- [1993] Berkshire **retires the industry combined-ratio table** it had long published: because
  interest rates have fallen, "the value of float has substantially declined," so a given
  combined ratio "now… [is] a far less attractive business than it did" in the 1980s.
  Buffett's refined principle: the true economics of an insurer require weighing underwriting
  results against "the **current risk-free earnings obtainable from float**" — the float's
  "transfer price… should be determined simply by the risk-free, long-term rate of interest"
  (a *separate* question from the insurer's actual investment skill). The year was a banner one:
  an **underwriting profit of $31M** on **$2,624.7M** average float — "we had the use of $2.6
  billion at no cost; in fact we were paid $31 million… to hold these funds." Homestate (Rod
  Eldred), workers' comp (Brad Kinstler), credit-card (the Kizers), and NICO auto/GL (Don
  Wurster) all contributed underwriting profits.
- [1994] **A second straight underwriting-profit year** — float grew to **$3,056.6M** at
  *negative* cost again. Buffett restates the core test crisply: "An insurance business is
  profitable over time if its cost of float is less than the cost the company would otherwise
  incur to obtain funds. But the business has a **negative value** if the cost of its float is
  higher than market rates." He repeats the 1993 caution — the business "is not as good as it
  currently looks," because the super-cat profits buoying float are episodic. The same four
  units (homestate, workers' comp, credit-card, NICO auto/GL) again wrote at underwriting
  profits with substantial float.
- [1995] **The "float as equity" essay** — the clearest statement of why cost-free float is so
  powerful. A company's profitability turns on (1) what its assets earn, (2) what its liabilities
  cost, and (3) its **leverage**. Because Berkshire's float "has cost us virtually nothing… it
  has in effect served as equity" — but *without* the dilution real equity requires. The thought
  experiment: replace $3.4B of float with $3.4B of equity and you'd own the same assets, earn
  *less* (the float actually threw off profit), and have far more shares — "materially
  reduc[ing] the value of our stock." A given combined ratio is worth less than before because
  low rates cut float's value; an insurer generating float "at a cost that exceeds what the funds
  are worth" turns leverage into a *disadvantage*. The 1995 table: a **third straight underwriting
  profit** on **$3,607.2M** average float (negative cost); float has compounded **20.7%/yr since
  1967**. The **[[GEICO]]** acquisition adds ~$3B more — though, unlike the internally generated
  float, "we paid a very substantial price for the GEICO float." Small units posted superb
  combined ratios (NICO 84.2, homestate 81.4).
- [1996] **Float vaults to $6,702.0M** — nearly double 1995's $3,607M — as the wholly-owned
  **[[GEICO]]** is consolidated into the table for the first time. A **fourth straight
  underwriting profit** keeps the cost of float **below zero** ("in effect, we have been
  paid for holding money"). Buffett restates the doctrine compactly: float "is money we hold
  but don't own"; the underwriting loss *is* the cost of float; "an insurance business has
  value if its cost of float over time is **less than the cost the company would otherwise
  incur to obtain funds** … but the business is an albatross if [it is] higher than market
  rates." Since entering insurance in 1967, **float has compounded 22.3%/yr**, and "in more
  years than not, our cost of funds has been **less than nothing**." Owning GEICO outright
  "materially increases the probability that we can continue to obtain 'free' funds in
  increasing amounts." Float is flagged as "a major component of Berkshire's intrinsic value
  **not reflected in book value**."
- [1997] A **fifth straight underwriting profit** lifts average float to **$7,093.1M** at
  negative cost; since 1967 float has compounded **21.7%/yr** and "has cost us nothing, and in
  fact has made us money." Buffett crystallizes the "**accounting irony**": "Though our float
  is shown on our balance sheet as a **liability**, it has had a value to Berkshire **greater
  than an equal amount of net worth** would have had" (the float-as-equity insight of 1995,
  now stated as paradox). He adds a reserving caution — estimating latitude makes an insurer's
  true cost of float hard to verify ("at times I have been amazed by the numbers that big-name
  auditors have implicitly blessed"), so Berkshire reports conservatively since "virtually all
  surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones." Float will **dip in Q1 1998** as several large
  contracts expire, but is expected to grow over the long term.
- [1998] **Float roughly triples to $22,762M (year-end)** as **[[General Re]]** joins — Gen Re
  alone is about **two-thirds** of the total. Buffett reframes the whole insurance operation around
  three determinants — "(1) the amount of float … (2) its cost; and (3) most important of all, the
  long-term outlook for both." Over 32 years float has compounded **25.4%/yr** at a cost "well
  under zero," and he restates the now-familiar paradox in its strongest form: "though our net
  float is recorded on our balance sheet as a **liability**, it has had **more economic value to us
  than an equal amount of net worth** would have had. As long as we can continue to achieve an
  underwriting profit, **float will continue to outrank net worth in value**." Near-term growth may
  be "modest" (the reinsurance market is soft, and Gen Re's relationships change slowly), but the
  cost should stay "very attractive."
- [1999] Float grew to **$25,298M** (now broken out by segment: GEICO $3,444M, General Re
  $15,166M, Other Reinsurance $6,285M, Other Primary $403M) — but the cost turned sharply
  *positive*: a **$1.4 billion underwriting loss** drove the **cost of float to 5.8%**, almost
  entirely from an "aberrational" [[General Re]] result. Buffett stresses cost over growth
  ("growth of float is important — but its cost is what's vital") and notes a silver lining: ~$400M
  of the loss he "enthusiastically welcomed" because it "stems from business that will deliver …
  **exceptional float over the next decade**" (retroactive/large-deal reinsurance). Cumulative
  through 1998 had been an underwriting *profit*. The **"other primary"** cluster (Rod Eldred, Brad
  Kinstler, John Kizer, Don Towle, Don Wurster) delivered a **$192M underwriting profit over five
  years**; **[[Ajit Jain]]**'s reinsurance now holds **$6.3B** of float built "from scratch" at an
  underwriting profit. General Re's and Cologne's incentive comp is **newly tied to float growth
  and cost of float** — "the same variables that determine value for owners."
- [2000] Float grew to **$27,871M** (GEICO $3,943M / Gen Re $15,525M / Other Reinsurance $7,805M /
  Other Primary $598M), but a **$1.6 billion underwriting loss** held the cost at **6%** (vs. a
  near-zero historical average; "as high as 19% in 1984"). Buffett introduces **two unusual,
  Berkshire-specific items** that distort the cost upward but are economically attractive: (1)
  "**pain-today, gain-tomorrow**" deals — insurers offloading current losses for float Berkshire
  keeps for years (1999 $400M / 27.8% of the loss; 2000 $482M / 34.4%); and (2) **retroactive
  reinsurance** — assuming a company's *past* losses (always capped) in exchange for a large
  premium, booked as a **"deferred charges applicable to assumed reinsurance"** asset that amortizes
  into underwriting losses "for many years, often … decades," while Berkshire holds "lots of" float.
  Net: future cost of float should be "very attractive" but "rarely … 'no-cost'" because of that
  annual retroactive charge — and "the ultimate benefits … depend not only on its cost but … **how
  effectively we deploy it**." Both lines are "almost single-handedly the work of [[Ajit Jain]]."
- [2001] **The teaching essay — "The Economics of Property/Casualty Insurance."** Buffett opens
  with the cleanest definition yet: float is "**money we hold but don't own**," arising because
  "premiums are received before losses are paid." The three determinants restated: amount, cost,
  and "**most critical of all, the long-term outlook for both**." Float surged to **$35,508M**
  (GEICO $4,251M / Gen Re $19,310M / Other Reinsurance $11,262M / Other Primary $685M), but
  September 11th — "the insurance industry's largest loss in history" — pushed the **cost of float
  to a staggering 12.8%**, the worst since 1984 (when it hit 19%). With government bonds yielding
  little and short-term funds under 2%, Buffett warns that "each of our insurance operations, save
  one, must deliver an **underwriting profit**" to be a good business — the exception being the
  retroactive-reinsurance book, whose ~$425M annual underwriting loss is structurally acceptable.
  [[GEICO]] alone earned a **$221M underwriting profit** (it "actually paid" Berkshire to hold
  $4.25B of float).
- [2002] **"Getting back to low-cost float feels good."** Float grew **$5.7B to $41,224M** (GEICO
  $4,678M / Gen Re $22,207M / Other Reins $13,396M / Other Primary $943M) at a **cost of just 1%** —
  remarkable given it absorbed **$1.75B (≈4.6% of float)** of "charges": Gen Re's further $1.31B
  prior-year reserve correction plus the $440M annual retroactive-reinsurance amortization. The
  three tests for low-cost float are codified: insurers must "(a) underwrite with unwavering
  discipline; (b) **reserve conservatively**; and (c) avoid an aggregation of exposures that would
  allow a supposedly 'impossible' incident to threaten their solvency." Buffett warns the falling
  interest-rate environment has "transformed underwriting losses that formerly were tolerable into
  burdens that move insurance businesses deeply into the **lemon** category" — so the bar is now an
  underwriting profit. [[Ajit Jain]]'s book ($13.4B float, $534M UW profit) was the main engine.
- [2003] **"A standout… float reached record levels and it came without cost."** Float grew to
  **$44,220M** (GEICO $5,287M / Gen Re $23,654M / Other Reins $13,948M / Other Primary $1,331M) and
  *all* major segments contributed to a **$1.7 billion pre-tax underwriting profit**. Buffett's
  scorecard: in **18 of 37 years** Berkshire operated at an underwriting profit ("we were actually
  paid for holding money"), against only five "terrible years" costing more than 10%. "Insurers
  sell a non-proprietary piece of paper containing a non-proprietary promise… the critical
  variables… are **managerial brains, discipline and integrity**."
- [2004] Float grew **$1.9B to $46,094M** (the $20M of 1967 float now multiplied 2,300×) at
  better-than-costless terms — a **$1,551M underwriting profit** (Gen Re $3M / B-H Reinsurance $417M
  / GEICO $970M / Other Primary $161M). "We were paid more than $1.5 billion to hold an average of
  about $45.2 billion." Buffett frames the year around *how* the float is generated cheaply — NICO's
  ebb-and-flow [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|discipline]] (see [[National Indemnity Company]])
  and GEICO's low-cost model — and warns 2005 pricing "will be less attractive."
- [2005] Float reached **$49,287M** (the 1967 $20M now ~$49B). Despite **$3.4B of hurricane losses**
  (Katrina, Rita, Wilma), float was again **costless** — a slim **+$53M** overall underwriting
  profit, as **[[GEICO]]'s +$1,221M** more than offset reinsurance losses (Gen Re −$334M, B-H
  Reinsurance −$1,069M). The episode shows the value of *diversified* insurance: a mega-cat that
  guts the reinsurance book can still net to a profit when the primary engine is strong.
- [2006] **The 40th anniversary — and a banner year.** The $17M of float that came with NICO in 1967
  has grown to **$50,887M** (+ ~$7B from the **[[Equitas]]** deal). "Mother Nature went on vacation,"
  so *every* segment profited: a record **$3,838M underwriting profit** (Gen Re +$526M, B-H
  Reinsurance +$1,658M, GEICO +$1,314M, Other Primary +$340M) — "you won't soon see another [view]
  like it." Buffett flags a structural quirk: **retroactive float "automatically drifts down over
  time,"** so growing float will require new acquisitions — but the **cost of float over time is
  likely below the industry's, perhaps below zero.** The Equitas DCRA charge lifts the annual
  retroactive drag to ~**$450M**, a hurdle other operations must clear for cost-free float.
- [2007] Float reached **$58,698M** (and now "**funds $59 billion of our investments**" — "free as
  long as… underwriting breaks even"), with a second straight catastrophe-light year producing a
  **$3,374M underwriting profit**. Buffett reframes Berkshire's value as two buckets: **investments**
  (then $141B, largely float-funded) and **non-insurance earnings** — float being "an **unencumbered
  source of value**" so long as underwriting averages breakeven or better, which "over our entire
  history… we've been profitable." He warns the party is over: 2008 margins "**will fall
  significantly**."
- [2008] **A sixth straight underwriting profit — and Berkshire is *paid* to hold its float.**
  Despite the predicted fall from 2007's peak, underwriting earned **$2,792M** (Gen Re +$342M, BH
  Reinsurance +$1,324M, GEICO +$916M, Other Primary +$210M), so the **$58,488M of float cost *less
  than zero*** — "we were **paid $2.8 billion to hold our float** during 2008. Charlie and I find
  this enjoyable." Buffett contrasts Berkshire with the industry, where "most insurers experience a
  substantial underwriting loss" (insurer ROE averaged 8.5% over 1983–2007 vs. 14.0% for the Fortune
  500). A conceptual aside this year: Berkshire's **derivatives "float"** ($8.1B of up-front
  premiums on equity-index puts and CDS) "is similar to insurance float" — free money held and
  invested, with the payout far in the future (see [[Derivatives]]).
- [2009] **A seventh straight underwriting profit; float reaches $62 billion.** Buffett restates the
  P/C economic model for BNSF newcomers: "collect-now, pay-later" leaves float that "remains
  remarkably stable in relation to premium volume," so "as our business grows, so does our float" —
  $16M at the 1967 entry to **$62B** now. Underwriting profit **$1,559M** (Gen Re $477M, BH Reins
  $349M, GEICO $649M, Other Primary $84M; float $61,911M). The caution recurs: **cost-free float is
  not the industry norm** — most insurers run underwriting losses, so "the industry's overall return
  on tangible equity has for many decades fallen far short of… the S&P 500"; Berkshire's edge is its
  managers and "unusual businesses." He also separates a **derivatives "float"** (~$6.3B) from the
  $62B of insurance float.
- [2010] **An eighth consecutive underwriting profit; float $65,832M.** Buffett gives the decade
  table (float $39M in 1970 → $237M → $1,632M → $27,871M → **$65,832M** in 2010) and the eight-year
  underwriting-profit tally of **$17B** — "if we accomplish that, our float will be **better than
  cost-free**." The contrast is sharpened with **State Farm**, "by far the country's largest insurer
  and a well-managed company," which ran underwriting losses in **seven of the last ten years**
  (>$20B aggregate) — proof that cost-free float is "**not an outcome to be expected for the P/C
  industry as a whole.**" UW profit $2,013M (Gen Re $452M, BH Reins $176M, GEICO $1,117M, Other
  Primary $268M). [[Ajit Jain]]'s book alone holds ~$30B of float.
- [2011] **Record float $70,571M and a ninth straight underwriting profit** ($248M in a heavy-cat
  year: BH Reins −$714M, Gen Re $144M, GEICO $576M, Other Primary $242M). Buffett flags that float is
  now "**unlikely to grow much — if at all**" (it's outsized relative to premium volume) but any
  decline would be "**very gradual.**" The year's conceptual addition is **how float bears on
  intrinsic value**: float is deducted *in full* as a liability "just as if we had to pay it out
  tomorrow," yet a costless, long-enduring, revolving fund is worth far more than that — and the
  **$15.5B of insurance "goodwill"** on the books understates "what we would pay to purchase float of
  similar quality." Float is thus "**a huge reason** why… Berkshire's intrinsic business value
  substantially exceeds book value." (Cost-free float remains atypical: "we don't think there is much
  'Berkshire-quality' float existing in the insurance world.")
- [2012] **"$73 billion of free money" — a tenth straight underwriting profit.** Buffett's CEOs "set
  out to prove me wrong" about float plateauing and grew it $2.5B to **$73,125M**; he now expects any
  future decline to be "very gradual — at the outside no more than 2% in any year." Underwriting profit
  **$1,625M** (BH Reins $304M, Gen Re $355M, GEICO $680M, Other Primary $286M); the 10-year cumulative
  is **$18.6B**. He sharpens the **intrinsic-value point**: float, fully deducted as a liability, is
  worth far less than its accounting figure when "costless and long-enduring," and the **$15.5B of
  insurance goodwill** is carried far below true value — "a huge reason… Berkshire's intrinsic business
  value substantially exceeds its book value." A new caution: insurers' earnings are being flattered by
  "**legacy" bond portfolios** yielding more than reinvestment will allow — "today's bond portfolios
  are, in effect, wasting assets." (In 37 of the 45 years ending 2011, industry premiums were
  inadequate.)
- [2013] **An eleventh straight underwriting profit; float $77,240M** (UW profit $3,089M: BH Reins
  $1,294M, Gen Re $283M, GEICO $1,127M, Other Primary $385M). Eleven-year cumulative underwriting
  profit **$22B** — "all of this began with our 1967 purchase of [[National Indemnity Company|National Indemnity]] for $8.6 million." Buffett vividly restates float as a **revolving fund**: in 2013 alone
  Berkshire paid "**some $17 billion to more than five million claimants**," yet new business replaced
  it. P/C float "can never be subject to immediate demands… large compared to our cash" (unlike some
  life insurance). State Farm ran underwriting losses in 9 of the 12 years ending 2012.
- [2014] **A twelfth straight underwriting profit; float $83,921M** (UW profit $2,668M: BH Reins $606M,
  Gen Re $277M, GEICO $1,159M, Other Primary $626M; 12-yr cumulative $24B). The revolving-fund point is
  made vivid: in 2014 Berkshire paid "**a huge $22.7 billion to more than six million claimants**," yet
  new business replaced it — "**owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises… is worlds
  different from owing $1 that will go out the door tomorrow**," though GAAP treats them as equal.
  Float is "**a key pillar in Berkshire's economic fortress**." A new headwind: prolonged low interest
  rates depress earnings *on* float, worsening the industry's already-subnormal returns. Munger's
  anniversary essay credits casualty insurance — "**ordinarily… a producer of mediocre results**" —
  as the engine that, via Jain and GEICO, turned "marvelously well" for Berkshire (see [[The Berkshire System]]).
- [2015] **A 13th straight underwriting profit; float $87,722M** (~$1.8B; 13-yr cumulative **$26.2B**)
  — "**Berkshire's largest unrecorded wealth lies in its insurance business**… it can't be replicated."
  [[National Indemnity Company|NICO]] is now the **largest P/C company in the world by net worth**.
  Revolving-fund point restated ($24.5B of claims to 6M claimants in 2015). Notably, almost the entire
  $15.5B of insurance goodwill "**was already on our books in 2000. Yet we subsequently tripled our
  float**" — the carried value vastly understates the real worth. **Two headwinds named:** prolonged
  **low interest rates** "virtually guarantee… earnings on float will steadily decrease for many years
  to come," and reinsurance pricing has softened — "industry results over the next ten years will fall
  short of those recorded in the past decade." The 2015 methodology change folds (now-stable)
  **underwriting income into the intrinsic-value calculation** (see [[Intrinsic Business Value]]).
- [[2016]] **A 14th straight underwriting profit; float $91,577M** (UW profit **$2,131M**: BH Reins $822M,
  Gen Re $190M, GEICO $462M, Other Primary $657M; 14-yr pre-tax total **$28B**). Buffett prints the
  long-arc table ($39M in 1970 → $237M → $1,632M → $27,871M → $65,832M → **$91,577M**) and notes a "huge
  policy" written shortly after year-end pushed float **over $100 billion** (the **[[AIG retroactive reinsurance]]** cover). The **revolving-fund** argument is given its fullest form: $27B paid to 6M+
  claimants in 2016, continuously replaced — "**owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises… is
  worlds different from owing $1 that will go out the door tomorrow.**" Two structural points: float "may
  in time" decline but only "**very gradual[ly] — at the outside no more than 3% in any year**" (by
  design — never subject to near-term demands of significance), and the **$15.5B insurance "goodwill"**
  asset vastly understates float's worth — "almost the entire $15.5 billion… was already on our books in
  2000 when float was $28 billion. Yet we have subsequently increased our float by **$64 billion**, a gain
  that in no way is reflected in our book value." Headwind restated: dramatically lower world interest
  rates will steadily depress industry earnings on float, "particularly… companies that specialize in
  reinsurance." [[General Re]]: **[[Tad Montross]] retired** after 39 years; **[[Kara Raiguel]]** now CEO.
- [[2017]] **The 15-year streak ends — but float crosses $114 billion.** After **14 consecutive years of
  underwriting profit ($28.3B pre-tax)**, Berkshire took a **$3.2 billion pre-tax underwriting loss** from
  **Hurricanes Harvey, Irma and Maria** (~$2B after tax; ~3% of a ~$100B industry loss) — Buffett's
  long-standing warning that "the catastrophe-light period… was not a new norm" made fact. Float still
  grew to **$114,500M** (premium volume $60,597M), powered by the record **$10.2B [[AIG retroactive reinsurance]]** policy (up to $20B of long-tail losses assumed). Buffett restates float's defensive
  virtue versus banks/life insurers: **p/c float "can't be withdrawn,"** so Berkshire can't suffer a
  "run" in a crisis — a structural strength it factors into investment decisions ("we never will operate
  Berkshire in a manner that depends on the kindness of strangers"). He sets the **mega-cat math**: a ~2%
  annual chance of a **$400B** U.S. insured mega-catastrophe, Berkshire's ~$12B share "**far below the
  annual earnings we expect from our non-insurance activities**," while "perhaps most" of the p/c world
  "would be out of business." The Lloyd's 1980s near-collapse is cited as a caution on long-tail risk.
- [[2018]] **Float $122,732M; the "fifth grove."** Underwriting returned to profit (**+$2B pre-tax**) — so
  Berkshire has now earned an underwriting profit in **15 of the past 16 years** ($27B pre-tax over the
  span). In the **[[The Five Groves|five-groves]]** framing, insurance is the fifth grove, valuable "in a
  less obvious manner" because its float **funds much of the other four groves at cost-free (or better)
  terms**. The float table is extended to **$122,732M** (now explicitly **including life/annuity/health
  float**). Float is named one of Berkshire's **four funding sources** (with equity, debt, and deferred
  taxes) — "of more utility to us than an equivalent amount of equity… we have been paid in most years for
  holding and using other people's money" — tempered by the standing warning that a mega-cat ("**The Big
  One**," even a cyber attack) "will occur" and the losses "will be big — very big."
- [[2019]] **Float $129,423M; the value of float falls industry-wide.** Underwriting earned **+$400M** —
  a profit in **16 of the last 17 years** (17-year pre-tax total **$27.5B**), with the lone 2017 exception.
  Buffett's new emphasis: for the **industry**, the financial value of float "is now far less than it was
  for many years," because bond-heavy insurers must "recycle their 'old' investment portfolios" into the
  decade's "pathetically low rates" — earning "**only 2 cents or 3 cents**" per dollar of float where they
  "once could safely earn 5 cents or 6 cents." Berkshire's edge is its "unrivaled mountain of capital" and
  freedom to invest float beyond high-grade bonds. He sharpens the caveat: "**we will most certainly not
  have an underwriting profit in 16 of the next 17 years**." The growth showcase is **[[Guard Insurance| GUARD]]** (premium +379% to $1.9B since [[Ajit Jain]]'s 2012 purchase) — Wilkes-Barre as a new Omaha.
- [[2020]] **Float $138B — and the largest, in value, of the "Big Four."** Insurance is named Berkshire's
  **most valuable business** ([[Ajit Jain]]). Its defining edge in 2020: the fortress balance sheet lets
  the insurers run an **equity-heavy** strategy "not feasible for the overwhelming majority of insurers,"
  who must hold bonds — and "**bonds are not the place to be these days**" (the 10-year Treasury yielded
  just **0.93%**, down 94% from 15.8% in Sept 1981; trillions of sovereign debt at negative yields).
  Reaching for yield in "shaky" credit is "not the answer" (the S&L collapse the cautionary tale). Float
  "**has been costless to us**" cumulatively and "is likely to remain near its present level for many
  years." (Buffett, unusually, refers new readers to the reprinted 2019 float section rather than
  re-explaining.)
- [[2021]] **Float $147B — "very sticky," and the Ajit hiring story.** Float grew **$9B to $147 billion**
  (from $19M when Berkshire entered insurance in 1967), and Buffett stresses its **stickiness**: "Funds
  attributable to our insurance operations come and go daily, but their aggregate total is immune from
  precipitous decline" — so float can be invested **long-term**. Over 55 years it has "**cost… less than
  nothing**" (a modest cumulative underwriting profit). Insurance is the first of the **Four Giants** (the
  100%-owned cluster), and Buffett retells the **1986 [[Ajit Jain]] hiring**: asked his insurance
  experience, Jain said "None"; "I said, '**Nobody's perfect**,' and hired him." A buyback footnote: since
  repurchases shrink the share count, **float per A-share rose 25%** over 2020–21 ($79,387 → $99,497). He
  warns there *will* be years of underwriting loss, "perhaps involving very large sums" — but "Berkshire
  is constructed to handle catastrophic events as no other insurer."
- [[2022]] **Float $147B → $164B, lifted by [[Alleghany Corporation|Alleghany]].** The ~$11.6B purchase of
  Alleghany (P/C insurer/reinsurer under returning CEO **[[Joe Brandon]]**) added float and equity-
  investable capital — its value flowing from Berkshire's "**unmatched financial strength**," which lets
  its insurers "follow valuable and enduring investment strategies unavailable to virtually all
  competitors." Since the 1967 NICO purchase, float has grown **8,000-fold**; "with disciplined
  underwriting, these funds have a decent chance of being cost-free over time."
- [[2023]] **"It carried the day."** Insurance "performed exceptionally well," setting records in sales,
  float and underwriting profits (underwriting profit **$5,428M**, up from −$30M; investment income surged
  to **$9,567M** as the T-bill pile "finally began to pay us far more than the pittance"). P/C premium
  volume has grown **~5,000-fold ($17M → $83B)** since 1967 "and we have much room to grow." The defining
  rule for a Berkshire underwriter: "they can't be **optimists at the office**." Surprises in P/C "are
  almost always negative," and inflation — "monetary and the 'legal' variety" — is "a wild card." Berkshire's
  managers are "to P/C insurance what Cooperstown's honorees are to baseball" (see [[Ajit Jain]]).
- [[2024]] **Float $46B → $171B over two decades; the model explained once more.** Buffett devotes a P/C
  essay to the "rare — very rare" collect-now, pay-later model (asbestos claims still paid 50 years on):
  in long-tail lines a careless or crooked insurer "may report large but fictitious profits… for many
  years — even decades." Over the past two decades Berkshire's insurance earned **$32 billion of after-tax
  underwriting profit** (~3.3¢ per dollar of sales) while **float grew from $46B to $171B** (likely to
  grow "a bit" and, with luck, costless). Berkshire's structural edges: it can absorb extreme losses
  "without blinking" and is **not dependent on reinsurers** ("a material and enduring cost advantage").
  **[[Mike Goldberg]]**'s rule: "**We want our underwriters to daily come to work nervous, but not
  paralyzed**" — "definitely not a business for optimists." Insurance-underwriting earnings of **$9,020M**
  (up from $5,428M) and investment income **$13,670M** led the record year.
