# The Inevitables

"The Inevitables" is the label Buffett coins in the **[[1996]]** letter for the rare
companies "virtually certain to possess enormous competitive strength ten or twenty
years from now" — businesses whose dominance is so durable that no honest competitor
"questions that [they] will dominate their fields worldwide for an investment lifetime."
His two named examples are **[[Coca-Cola]]** and **[[Gillette]]**: "Forecasters may
differ a bit … but … their dominance will probably strengthen." The concept is the
maturation of the **[[Economic Moat]]** metaphor — an Inevitable is a business whose moat
is so wide and deep that the future is, for investment purposes, knowable.

Buffett wraps the idea in careful caveats:

- **Inevitability is not guaranteed by leadership alone.** "Witness the shocks … at
  General Motors, IBM and Sears." For every Inevitable "there are dozens of
  **Impostors** — companies now riding high but vulnerable to competitive attacks."
  Because true Inevitables are so few, Berkshire adds a tier of "**Highly Probables**."
- **You can overpay even for the best.** "The overpayment risk … may now be quite high
  for the purchasers of virtually all stocks, The Inevitables included." Buying into an
  overheated market can mean a long wait for value to catch up to price.
- **The greatest danger is self-inflicted: loss of focus.** "Would you believe that a
  few decades back they were growing shrimp at Coke and exploring for oil at Gillette?"
  When a great company's managers neglect the base business for so-so diversification,
  "the suffering of investors is often prolonged."

The Inevitables frame Buffett's preference for **predictability over growth rate**: "I
would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one." He explicitly
sets this against the temptation of "high-tech businesses or embryonic industries" that
"will grow much faster in percentage terms" — Berkshire applauds change "as citizens"
but, "as investors … prefer to skip the ride." The same predictability test governs
whole-business purchases like [[See's Candies]], whose *reasons* for being bought "are
virtually unchanged from … the 1920s."

## Evolution
- [1996] Coined for [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Gillette]]; framed against "Impostors" and
  softened by "Highly Probables." Three caveats: leadership ≠ certainty, you can overpay,
  and loss of focus is the killer. Robert Woodruff on selling Coke: "I've never sold
  any." Tied to the index-fund advice and "if you aren't willing to own a stock for ten
  years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes" (see [[Circle of Competence]],
  [[Permanent Ownership]]).
