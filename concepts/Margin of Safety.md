# Margin of Safety

"Margin of safety" is [[Ben Graham]]'s three-word distillation of sound investing, and
the principle Buffett invokes in the [[1990]] letter to condemn the 1980s debt mania.
From the final chapter of *The Intelligent Investor*: "Confronted with a challenge to
distill the secret of sound investment into three words, we venture the motto, **Margin
of Safety**." Buffett: "Forty-two years after reading that, I still think those are the
right three words."

The 1990 setting is the collapse of junk bonds. Buffett rejects the "**dagger** thesis"
— the claim that huge debt would sharpen managers "much as a dagger mounted on the
steering wheel of a car." A very alert driver, yes — but "another certain consequence
would be a deadly… accident if the car hit even the tiniest pothole… The roads of
business are riddled with potholes; a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for
disaster." Capital structures "were concocted that guaranteed failure" (a Tampa TV
station whose interest exceeded its gross revenues).

He dismantles the salesmen's "proof" that diversified junk out-returns high grade: it
wrongly assumed **manufactured junk** behaved like genuine **"fallen angels"** (bonds
downgraded *after* issuance, like Berkshire's [[WPPSS Bonds]]). The difference is the
issuer: a fallen-angel manager "yearned to regain investment-grade status," while the
junk operator behaved "much as a heroin user might… finding another fix." "Beware of
past-performance 'proofs' in finance: if history books were the key to riches, the
Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." Berkshire has "never bought a *new issue* of a
junk bond" ("the only time to buy these is on a day with no 'y' in it") but will buy
distressed *fallen angels* with a margin of safety — as it did with **RJR Nabisco**
bonds ($440M in 1990), judging RJR's credit better than perceived.

The concept is the bond-market face of the same discipline that runs through
[[Intrinsic Business Value]], [[Financial Strength]], and the [[Mr. Market]] insistence
on buying well below value — and the inverse of the [[Zero-Coupon Bonds|zero-coupon and EBDIT abuses]] of the era.

## Evolution
- [1990] Graham's "margin of safety" invoked against the junk-bond collapse: the "dagger
  on the steering wheel" fallacy; manufactured junk vs. "fallen angels"; the statistical
  error in the pro-junk "proof"; Berkshire's RJR Nabisco bonds as a margin-of-safety buy.
- [1992] Named "the **cornerstone of investment success**" within the value/growth essay
  (see [[Value and Growth]]). After computing a stock's value via the [[John Burr Williams]]
  discounted-cash formula, "if we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly
  higher than its price, we're not interested in buying." Paired with the [[Circle of Competence]] discipline (only estimate "coupons" you can): "an investor needs to do very few
  things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes."
- [1997] **"The margin of safety … has materially eroded."** In a frothy market, Buffett judges
  stocks not *necessarily* overvalued — *if* interest rates stay low and corporate ROE stays
  exceptionally high — but warns those conditions "are not a sure thing to remain." He recalls his
  summer-1979 *Forbes* article, "**You pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery
  consensus**": then, pessimism "drove down prices to truly attractive levels"; now "we have a very
  cheery consensus," which has "materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified
  as the cornerstone of intelligent investing." It is the valuation companion to the **wait-for-the
  -fat-pitch** Ted Williams discipline and the **net-saver** logic of the same letter (see [[Mr. Market]]).
- [2001] **Distressed debt as the margin-of-safety frontier.** A more-active year in "junk,"
  with the discipline restated: "We have **never purchased a newly-issued junk bond**" ("the only
  kind most investors are urged to buy"), but Berkshire's "50-year experience in distressed debt
  has proven rewarding" — citing [[WPPSS Bonds]], Chrysler Financial, Texaco and RJR Nabisco, "all
  of which returned to grace." Two 2001 cases show distressed debt becoming a path to control:
  **[[Fruit of the Loom]]** (senior debt at ~50¢, ~15% current yield, leading to the whole-company
  bid) and **[[FINOVA Group]]** (13% of the debt at ~⅔ of face, leading to the $5.6B Berkadia
  workout with [[Leucadia National]]). The warning stands: "if we stay active in junk bonds, you
  can expect us to have losses from time to time."
- [2002] **Junk-bond commitments "sextupled" to $8.3 billion** as three years of falling prices and
  a few distressed situations created opportunity. Buffett contrasts the two disciplines: in
  *stocks* Berkshire concentrates on "conservatively financed businesses with strong competitive
  strengths" so "losses should be rare" — over 38 years its equity gains beat losses ~**100 to
  one**; in *junk* it deals with "far more marginal" enterprises (over-levered, low-return, sometimes
  questionable management), so "**we expect… occasional large losses**." The margin of safety in junk
  comes from price-vs-value scanning across "hundreds of securities" — the same hunt, lower-quality
  prey.
