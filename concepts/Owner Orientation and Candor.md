# Owner Orientation and Candor

Buffett's conviction that shareholders are *owners* of the business, entitled to
the same candid, manager-to-owner reporting he himself expects from Berkshire's
operating managers. It governs the tone and content of the letters themselves and
later hardens into Berkshire's formal "Owner's Manual."

The [[1979]] letter lays out the philosophy. Communications come "from the fellow
you are paying to run the business," not a PR staffer: "A once-a-year report of
stewardship should not be turned over to a staff specialist or public relations
consultant." Because ~98% of shares are held by the same owners year to year, the
report *builds on* prior years rather than restating boilerplate. Buffett invokes
**Phil Fisher's** restaurant analogy ([[Phil Fisher]]): a company attracts the
shareholder "clientele" it seeks; vacillating between strategies, like a restaurant
flip-flopping "between French cuisine and take-out chicken," yields "a revolving
door of confused and dissatisfied customers." He therefore courts long-horizon
owners and is wary of managements that crave high share turnover — which by
definition means wanting existing owners "continually to desert them." Candor cuts
both ways: report mistakes as plainly as successes (this very letter confesses the
Waumbec and bond errors).

## Evolution
- [1979] Full statement: CEO-to-owner candor, build-on-prior-years reporting,
  Phil Fisher's clientele analogy, courting low-turnover long-term owners.
- [1983] Codifies a numbered set of **owner-related business principles** — the
  seed of the later "Owner's Manual": partnership attitude, directors who "eat our
  own cooking," the per-share [[Intrinsic Business Value]] goal, minimal debt, no
  managerial wish list at owner expense, candor about pluses and minuses ("the CEO
  who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private"). Pairs
  with [[Capital Allocation]] (the $1 retention test) and [[Permanent Ownership]].
- [1983] **Why Berkshire won't split its stock**: split-chasers (who "prefer paper
  to value") would "downgrade the quality of our shareholder group" — "shareholder
  eugenics" via self-selection (opera vs. rock concert). Adds a critique of
  **trading friction**: a hyperactive market is "the pickpocket of enterprise" and
  "an invisible foot" levying a chair-changing tax; Berkshire's low turnover (~3%/yr)
  and tight policy keep price "rationally related to intrinsic business value."
- [1988] The **accounting-disclosure** essay. GAAP is "a beginning rather than an end" to
  informing owners; a CEO who reports "barebones GAAP numbers" omits what he'd demand
  from any subsidiary manager. Good reporting should let a reader answer three questions:
  *how much is the company worth, can it meet its obligations, and how good a job are the
  managers doing given their hand?* Hence Berkshire's new **four-sector** supplementary
  statements (which the auditors "in no way bless"). He warns of GAAP gamesmanship —
  earnings "smoothing," the "big bath," and outright fraud — "It has been far safer to
  steal large sums with a pen than small sums with a gun." The **NYSE listing** (see
  [[Intrinsic Business Value]]) extends the clientele logic: Berkshire wants low turnover
  and owners who "stay with us indefinitely" — "at what other organization… do leaders
  cheer when members leave?"
- [1992] **Accounting honesty as candor.** Buffett's twin accounting essays press the theme
  that reported numbers must reflect economic reality. On **post-retirement health
  liabilities** (newly required to be booked) he derides decades of "out-of-sight,
  out-of-mind" cash-basis treatment, and on **stock options** he wages his expensing campaign
  (detailed under [[Executive Compensation]]). The governing maxim is **Lincoln's riddle**:
  "How many legs does a dog have if you call his tail a leg? Four — because calling a tail a
  leg does not make it a leg." "It behooves managers to remember that Abe's right even if an
  auditor is willing to certify that the tail is a leg." He also defends Berkshire's
  famously lean overhead (after-tax corporate expense under 1% of operating earnings; no
  legal/PR/HR/strategic-planning departments) and reaffirms the **no-split** policy and
  low-turnover shareholder "clientele" first set out in [[1983]].
- [1996] **The Owner's Manual goes public.** The economic principles, long listed in the front of
  the report, are reproduced in full (and were mailed separately in June after the Class B
  offering) — the codification the 1983 principles foreshadowed. The fairness doctrine sharpens:
  managed "as a family partnership," "**fairness prevails when market price and intrinsic value
  are in sync**," and a manager can "do much to foster equity" so that no group of shareholders
  gains "at the expense of others." **Costs as a fiduciary matter:** Berkshire's after-tax HQ
  expense runs "**less than two basis points** … against net worth" (vs. ~100 bp for equity mutual
  funds, a levy likely to cut investors' returns "by 10% or more over time") — "**We are here to
  make money *with* you, not *off* you.**" Buffett also reaffirms courting long-horizon owners
  ("Berkshire probably ranks number one … in the percentage of its shares held by owners with a
  long-term view") and the [[Class B Shares]] offering's deliberate design to attract them.
- [1998] **"Accounting — Part 2": the campaign against earnings management.** Buffett judges
  restructuring and merger accounting "even worse" than option accounting — "**Bad accounting drives
  out good**" (Son of Gresham). The tricks: the **"restructuring charge"** and **"big bath,"** where
  a quarter "already fated to disappoint" is loaded with costs so future quarters "consistently
  exceed expectations by five cents" — dramatized by the golf scorecard (turn in 140, 80, 80, 80, 80
  instead of 91, 94, 89, 94, 92) and the two-bank-CEO exchange ("We'll make it bigger than that —
  that's why we're doing the deal"). R.G. Associates counted **1,369 special charges totaling
  $72.1B** in 1998 (vs. Fortune 500 1997 earnings of $324B). Auditors "kowtow … to the managers who
  … dole out their pay" ("Whose bread I eat, his song I sing"), and Buffett endorses SEC chairman
  **Arthur Levitt**'s landmark anti-"earnings management" speech ("Managing may be giving way to
  manipulating; integrity may be losing out to illusion"). He also condemns **selective disclosure**
  — favoring analysts/institutions — pledging that one B share is "the equal of our large
  institutional investors" and that earnings-guidance seekers will simply be "guide[d] … to our
  public documents." And the **CEO mandate** that flows from candor: run each business "**as if (1)
  you own 100% of it; (2) it is the only asset … your family … will ever have; and (3) you can't
  sell or merge it for at least a century**," letting **no decision be affected by accounting
  considerations** — "think about what counts, not how it will be counted" (see [[Decentralization]],
  [[Loss Reserving]]).
- [2000] **"Full and Fair Reporting."** Full reporting = "the information that we would wish you to
  give to us if our positions were reversed" — all the important facts plus "the CEO's frank view of
  the long-term economic characteristics of the business," in the CEO's "own words" (not "a public
  relations department"). Famous lines: "**References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management
  think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?**" Fair reporting = simultaneous disclosure
  to all 300,000 owners (financials posted on the Internet over a weekend); Buffett applauds SEC
  chairman **Arthur Levitt**'s crackdown on **selective disclosure** (Regulation FD) — "corrupt
  behavior … embraced by both Wall Street and corporate America." He adds a warning against CEOs
  **predicting growth rates** ("fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies in 2000 will
  attain 15% annual [EPS] growth over the next 20 years"), because lofty targets "corrode CEO
  behavior" into operating contortions and then accounting games — "**More money … has been stolen
  with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun**." A coda on press accuracy: the *Wall Street
  Journal*'s false front-page report that Berkshire was buying Conseco bonds — rumors deserve "a
  warning label."
- [2002] **Pro-forma honesty, inverted; and the auditor's job.** Mocking the era's "flood of
  'pro-forma' earnings statements" ("don't count this, don't count that — just count what makes
  earnings fat"), Buffett "make[s] a little history" by disclosing that on a pro-forma basis
  Berkshire earned *less* than it reported — two non-recurring breaks (no megacatastrophe + favorable
  bond conditions) had flattered results by ~$500M. He restates the EBITDA scorn ("does management
  think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"; the "pay employees ten years up front"
  parable) and the cockroach maxim ("there is **seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen**"). The
  **Audit Committee** reform belongs here too: auditors must "worry more about misleading [the
  committee] than about offending management," achieved by four pointed questions whose answers are
  reported to shareholders (see [[Corporate Governance]]). Companion campaigns: option-expensing (see
  [[Executive Compensation]]) and the [[Derivatives]] "mark-to-myth" critique.
- [[2020]] **"The Berkshire Partnership" — the attitude codified, with the founding history.** Buffett
  retells the **partnership era** (BPL from 1956; the 1962 amalgamation; Munger's parallel 1962
  partnership; BPL's 1969 dissolution distributing its 70.5% of Berkshire; the 1983 Blue Chip merger) to
  explain Berkshire's founding creed — the first 1983 owner principle: "**Although our form is corporate,
  our attitude is partnership.**" He maps Berkshire's ownership into **five "buckets"** (himself as
  "founder"; two institutional buckets — passive **index funds** "on automatic pilot" and **active
  managers**; active individuals; and his cherished fifth, the **million-plus long-term individual
  holders** who "simply trust us… with no intent to leave"), illustrated by 100-year-old **Stan Truhlsen**
  and the 1959 "Emdee, Ltd." doctors' partnership. **[[Phil Fisher]]**'s 1958 restaurant analogy returns —
  Berkshire has "been serving hamburgers and Coke for 56 years" and "cherish[es] the clientele this fare
  has attracted." The investor lesson restated: productive assets reward "**the passage of time, an inner
  calm, ample diversification and a minimization of transactions and fees**" — even a dart-throwing monkey
  prospers if it never trades — but "your expenses are Wall Street's income… **Wall Streeters do not work
  for peanuts.**"
