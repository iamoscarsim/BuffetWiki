# Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance

"Super-cat" reinsurance is the business of selling protection against rare, enormous
catastrophes — hurricanes, windstorms, earthquakes — to *other insurers and reinsurers*
who want to "lay off" part of their own catastrophe exposure. It became one of
Berkshire's signature insurance lines, built on its unmatched [[Financial Strength]] and
[[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]], and the operation [[Ajit Jain]] would grow into a
giant. Introduced in practice in the [[1989]] letter (Berkshire's $250M post-Hugo CAT
offer) and given its full framework in the [[1990]] letter.

The economics are deliberately the **opposite of normal insurance**: rather than
spreading risk across many independent policies, super-cat **concentrates** it. A
typical contract might pay a reinsurer, say, $10M only if a catastrophe causes both
specific losses to that buyer above a threshold *and* aggregate industry losses above,
say, $5 billion. Because the buyer only needs to collect "at times of extreme stress —
perhaps even chaos," it "seek[s] financially strong sellers," and "in the industry, our
strength is unmatched."

Consequences Buffett spells out:

- **Wild volatility.** On, say, $100M of annual super-cat premiums, "we are very likely
  in any given year to report either a profit of close to $100 million or a loss of well
  over $200 million." The combined ratio "will be close to either zero or 300%," never
  the industry's 100–120. "While we expect our super-cat business to produce satisfactory
  results over… a decade, we're sure it will produce absolutely terrible results in at
  least an occasional year."
- **Judgment over data.** "Historical loss data are of very limited value… as we decide
  what rates to charge today" — pricing rests on "subjective judgments."
- **Temperament as edge.** Most insurers can't tolerate the swings, or fear shareholders
  dislike volatility; Berkshire can, because its primary-property book is small and its
  owners, "if properly informed, can handle unusual volatility… so long as the swings
  carry… the prospect of superior long-term results." The credo: "**we always have
  preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%.**"

Berkshire writes super-cat "only at prices we believe to be commensurate with risk… If
competitors become optimistic, our volume will fall." By 1990 it was "the largest U.S.
writer of super-cat business" — "when a major quake occurs in an urban area or a winter
storm rages across Europe, light a candle for us."

## Evolution
- [1989] First in practice: after Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake left CAT
  reinsurance short, Berkshire offered up to **$250M** of catastrophe coverage and
  retained it (no lay-off) — possible only because of its ~$6B insurance net worth.
- [1990] The full framework: concentrate-don't-spread risk; combined ratio ~0% or ~300%;
  "a lumpy 15% [over] a smooth 12%"; judgment over historical data; Berkshire now the
  largest U.S. super-cat writer. Team under [[Mike Goldberg]] with [[Ajit Jain]].
- [1991] Now "**perhaps the largest in the world**." Buffett sets the pricing target
  explicitly: priced to **pay out ~90% of premiums over the long term**, on ~$100M of
  expected annual premium, with any single year likely to look "enormously profitable or
  enormously unprofitable" ($100M profit to $200M loss range). 1991 had one qualifying
  super-cat (triggering only ~25% of policies), yielding ~$11M of catastrophe underwriting
  profit. The year's biggest insured catastrophe was **not** the Oakland fire or Hurricane
  Bob but a **September typhoon in Japan** ($4–5B industry loss, surpassing Hurricane Hugo).
  Buffett's competitive-advantage thesis sharpens: financial strength wins because "when 'the
  big one' comes, many reinsurers who found it easy to write policies will find it difficult
  to write checks." Warning: "**innocents**" who don't understand the risk will eventually
  return and crater pricing.
- [1992] **Hurricane Andrew** — "the largest insured loss in history" — is the first real
  test. Berkshire's super-cat book absorbed ~**$125M** of Andrew losses (roughly its entire
  1992 super-cat premium), so with negligible other cat losses the line netted just **−$2M**
  for the year; investee [[GEICO]] separately lost ~$50M (Berkshire's look-through share
  ~$25M). Andrew added ~4 points to the industry combined ratio and revealed insurers whose
  own catastrophe reinsurance "was far from adequate" — "it's only when the tide goes out that
  you learn who's been swimming naked." Both 1991 and 1992 came in near break-even, which
  Buffett calls **aberrations**, sticking to his forecast of "huge annual swings." He spells
  out the **policy mechanics**: activation requires both the buyer's own "retention" loss
  *and* an industry-wide threshold (usually **$3B+**); coverage is often geographically
  limited, restricted to a "**second-** (or third-/fourth-) event," or to a specific peril
  like earthquake; one policy obliges a **$100M** payout. Berkshire is **second in U.S. P&C
  net worth** (behind State Farm), giving it capacity "that interests virtually no other
  company," yet it **rejects more than 98%** of business offered — discipline Buffett credits
  to **[[Ajit Jain]]** ("simply the best in this business"). Pricing can't extrapolate the
  past: **global warming**, and a mushrooming of insured coastal values, mean "a hurricane
  that caused *x* dollars of damage 20 years ago could easily cost 10*x* now"; he cites the
  1886 Charleston and 1812 New Madrid quakes as proof the "unthinkable happens." Accounting
  note: super-cat **revenue is deferred** until a loss occurs or the policy expires, so big
  losses can hit any quarter but **profits surface only in Q4**.
- [1993] A **loss-free year — by luck, not skill.** The Midwest floods didn't trigger super-cat
  losses (few private flood policies), helping Berkshire to its $31M underwriting profit. Buffett
  warns hard against extrapolating: "Suppose an event occurs 25 times in every century. If you
  annually give **5-for-1 odds** against its occurrence… you will have many more winning years
  than losers… You also will eventually go broke." Berkshire believes it gets ~**3.5-for-1**,
  "but there is no way… to calculate the true odds… it will take decades… to find out whether
  our underwriting judgment has been sound." Andrew cost $125M in 1992; because the book has
  grown, an identical storm now could cost **$600M** (the 1994 Northridge quake losses were
  "nominal," but a 7.5 instead of 6.8 "would have been a different story"). Two competitive
  facts: Berkshire's net worth is **10–20× its main super-cat competitors'** (capacity rivals
  can't match, since cat risk can't be diversified or laid off), and **each of the world's four
  largest reinsurers buys cover from Berkshire**. A caution: ~**$5B** of new reinsurance equity
  has been raised, prices are deteriorating, and Berkshire will accept "much-reduced volume"
  rather than underprice.
- [1994] **Another highly profitable, "close to a best-case" year** (the only significant loss
  was the January California [Northridge] quake; no major loss expected from the early-1995
  **Kobe** quake) — but Buffett repeats that the line "is not as good as it currently looks."
  Financial strength is the moat: a buyer's mega-catastrophe is also the event that could make
  many reinsurers default, so "the certainty that Berkshire will be both solvent and liquid
  after a catastrophe of unthinkable proportions is a major competitive advantage." Berkshire
  wrote a **$400M** California-quake policy "immediately," and will quote up to **$500M** of
  cover same-day — "No one else in the industry will do the same." It deliberately accepts
  "lumpy financial results" rather than "smooth" earnings. Buffett puts the **worst-case
  super-cat insurance loss at ~$600M after-tax** (slightly more than a year's other earnings),
  noting a true worst case could pair a catastrophe with a market drop in See's, Wells Fargo,
  and Freddie Mac — and bluntly: "**If you are not comfortable with this level of exposure, the
  time to sell your Berkshire stock is now, not after the inevitable mega-catastrophe.**" 1995
  volume will fall as capital chases premiums. [[Ajit Jain]]'s "value to us is simply enormous."
- [1995] **A third straight lucky year** — "the results of years like the past three will be at
  least partially offset by some truly terrible year in the future. We just hope that 'partially'
  turns out to be the proper adverb." 1995 had catastrophes but no insured super-cats (Opal abated
  before landfall; Kobe was huge economically but barely insured). [[Ajit Jain]], "the guiding
  genius," also writes **large single-risk** (non-cat) deals — illustrated by three from 1995:
  insuring **Mike Tyson's life**, **Lloyd's** against more than 225 of its "names" dying in the
  year, and the **launch and a year of orbit of two Chinese satellites**. Berkshire wins this
  business on three edges — unmatched financial strength, the **fastest quote** in the industry,
  and **limits larger than anyone else** will write (it offered a **$1 billion** policy; the
  largest a client *accepted* was $400M) — precisely because it doesn't lay off risk via
  reinsurance treaties. Its **once-in-a-century worst case** is "far less severe — relative to net
  worth — than that faced by many… primary companies," whose small policies aggregate into
  uncapped exposure. Prices are weakening, but "we will not knowingly write business at inadequate
  rates" — a discipline rooted in the early-1970s mistakes still billing Berkshire (and GEICO's own
  early-1980s reinsurance folly). "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and
  impossible to exit."
- [1996] **A fourth straight lucky (loss-free) year** — and Buffett's bluntest warning yet:
  "a truly terrible year in the super-cat business is **not a possibility — it's a
  certainty. The only question is when it will come.**" He pre-empts panic-selling on
  catastrophe news with Robert Woodruff on selling Coke ("I've never sold any"). Berkshire
  took on its two largest cat risks ever: a mid-year **Allstate** contract covering Florida
  hurricanes ("then … the largest single catastrophe risk ever assumed by one company for
  its own account"), and a ~**$1 billion** layer for the new **California Earthquake
  Authority** (effective April 1, 1997; called upon if the Authority's aggregate losses
  through 3/31/2001 exceed ~$5B) — exposing Berkshire to a loss "more than **twice**" the
  Florida contract, with all risk retained for its own account. Worst-case after-tax mega-cat
  loss is "probably **no more than $600 million** … less than 3% of book value and 1.5% of
  market value." The three competitive advantages are restated (certain payment even amid a
  post-cat financial panic; capacity reserved first for long-standing clients, now earning
  "**stand-by fees**" from reinsurers nailing down access; and unmatched single-name limits).
  Buffett dismisses catastrophe **computer models**: their projected precision "is a chimera"
  that can "lull decision-makers into a false sense of security" (the Northridge quake already
  blew past model expectations) — echoing "portfolio insurance" in 1987. Pricing aims for
  ~**90% of premiums** to be eventually paid out. The discipline is "**reverse engineering**"
  via Charlie's dictum: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there."
  [[Ajit Jain]]'s value "is simply enormous."
- [1997] **A fifth lucky (loss-free) year** — "the world suffered no catastrophes that caused
  huge amounts of insured damage, so virtually all premiums … dropped to the bottom line"
  ($283.0M super-cat underwriting profit). The dark side is a new fad: **"catastrophe bonds,"**
  which Buffett calls "an **Orwellian misnomer**" — "a true bond obliges the issuer to pay;
  these bonds, in effect, are contracts that lay a provisional promise to pay on the
  *purchaser*," a dodge to write unlicensed insurance and lull "unsophisticated buyers." He
  illustrates catastrophic mispricing with **dice**: insure a 1-in-36 roll (a 12) for a $1M
  premium against a $50M payout and "there is actually a **75.4% probability that you would go
  for a decade without paying out a dime** … Eventually, however, you would go broke." The
  promoters' **"expert" "puts no money on the table"** (a fixed up-front fee "no matter how
  inaccurate his predictions"). This "investor" money "has caused super-cat prices to
  deteriorate materially," so Berkshire will **write less in 1998**, cushioned by two big
  multi-year contracts (the Florida-hurricane and California Earthquake Authority policies).
  Worst-case after-tax loss is still ~**$600M** (the CEA maximum, ~**1% of market value**) —
  "if we could get appropriate prices, we would be willing to **significantly increase**" it.
  Buffett credits **[[Ajit Jain]]**, who "**developed [super-cat] from scratch**" and has "both
  the discipline to walk away … and the imagination to then find other opportunities."
- [1998] Super-cat underwriting profit was **$154M** (down from 1997's $283M, on more catastrophe
  activity). The year's bigger development is that the super-cat *logic* — Berkshire's willingness
  to **accept volatility for the prospect of long-term profit** — is generalized to all-lines
  reinsurance with the **[[General Re]]** acquisition. Buffett notes Berkshire spent "the past
  decade" using its balance-sheet strength "to build a powerful super-cat business," and now gives
  General Re the same freedom to **retain** rather than retrocede volatile business (see [[General Re]], [[Financial Strength]]). "We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."
- [1999] **[[Ajit Jain]]**'s reinsurance operation now holds **$6.3 billion of float**, built "from
  scratch" at an underwriting profit. Buffett reminds shareholders that because Jain "specializes in
  super-cat reinsurance, a line in which losses are infrequent but extremely large when they occur,
  his business is sure to be far more volatile than most insurance operations. To date, we have
  benefitted from **good luck** on this volatile book." (Berkshire's overall float cost spiked to
  5.8% in 1999 — but the damage came from [[General Re]], not the super-cat book.)
- [2000] [[Ajit Jain]]'s large-risk franchise broadens well beyond classic super-cat into
  **retroactive reinsurance** and **one-off mega-policies** (see [[Insurance Float]]): a record
  **$2.4B** retroactive premium covering a U.K. company, an Alex Rodriguez disability policy, and the
  Grab.com $1B-prize cover. The unifying logic is unchanged — accept volatility and the risk of "a
  horrible quarterly earnings number" for business "whose overall economics are certain to be
  favorable," because rivals "can't stomach what huge underwriting losses do to their reported
  results." Such policies "can't [be] lay[ed] off … through standard reinsurance arrangements," so
  only a financially-fortified, earnings-volatility-tolerant insurer will write them.
- [2005] **The terrible year arrives — and prompts Pascal's wager.** After the record 2005 hurricane
  season (Katrina the most destructive in industry history; Berkshire's total hurricane losses
  **$3.4B**, with B-H Reinsurance −$1,069M and Gen Re −$334M), Buffett confronts the open question of
  whether climate/ocean changes have permanently raised hurricane frequency and intensity (59
  Category-3+ storms hit the SE/Gulf in the century before 2004; then 3 in 2004 and 4 in 2005). His
  answer is **[[Circle of Competence|epistemic humility]]** operationalized as **Pascal's wager**:
  "since [we] didn't know the answer, [our] personal gain/loss ratio dictated" caution — write
  mega-cat **only at prices far higher than 2005's**, and only at an aggregate exposure that "would
  not cause us distress if shifts in some important variable produce far more costly storms." Yet "if
  prices seem appropriate, we continue to have both the ability and the appetite to be the **largest
  writer of mega-cat coverage in the world**."
- [2006] **The luck-driven black year — and a deliberate retreat.** With no hurricanes, B-H
  Reinsurance swung to a **+$1,658M** underwriting profit. But Buffett **acts on the climate caution**:
  as "a flood of capital… entered the super-cat field" and drove rates down, Berkshire **sharply
  reduced its wind exposures** — "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are
  fearful." He raises the stated single-event appetite to "**lose $6 billion in a single event, if we
  have been paid appropriately**," while refusing "even very small exposures at prices that don't
  reflect our evaluation of loss probabilities." Katrina, he insists, is not "anything close to a
  worst-case event."
