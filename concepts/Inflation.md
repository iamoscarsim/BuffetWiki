# Inflation

Inflation is the macro threat Buffett returns to again and again, especially in the
high-inflation years around 1980. His core insight: a business can post fine
*nominal* returns and still destroy its owners' *real* wealth once inflation and
taxes are accounted for.

The fullest early statement is in the [[1979]] letter. Buffett coins the
**"investor's misery index"** — the inflation rate plus the percentage of capital
an owner must pay (income tax on dividends, capital-gains tax on retained earnings)
to move the business's earnings into his own pocket. "When this index exceeds the
rate of return earned on equity by the business, the investor's purchasing power
(real capital) shrinks even though he consumes nothing at all." At a 14% inflation
rate, a 20%-on-equity business leaves an after-tax purchasing-power gain "very
close to zero." He drives it home with the gold parable: Berkshire's 1964 book
value bought about half an ounce of gold, and fifteen years of retained earnings
later it still buys about half an ounce — "government has been exceptionally able
in printing money... but is unable to print gold or create oil."

This concern also shapes his [[1979]] **bond** views (long-dated fixed-dollar
bonds as possibly "obsolete instruments") and his preference for businesses that
need little incremental capital and possess pricing power, which weather inflation
best. The theme recurs through the early-1980s letters and resurfaces whenever
monetary stability is in question.

## Evolution
- [1979] "Investor's misery index" introduced; inflation + taxes can turn a 20%
  nominal return into a zero or negative real return. Gold half-ounce parable.
  Long-term bonds judged dangerous in an inflationary world.
- [1980] The **hamburger parable**: forgo 10 hamburgers, get back dividends +
  proceeds buying only 8 → "you may feel richer, but you won't eat richer." Inflation
  raises the **hurdle rate** of ROE needed to give taxable owners any real return;
  the average taxpaying investor is "running up a down escalator." True **indexing**
  requires ROE to rise with prices *without added capital* — almost no business can,
  "and Berkshire Hathaway isn't one of them." Reaffirms: "Berkshire has no corporate
  solution to the problem."
- [1981] The **"corporate tapeworm"**: inflation "preemptively consumes its
  requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host" —
  more dollars for receivables, inventory and fixed assets just to maintain unit
  volume. The **value-added / crossbar** argument: with passive returns soaring
  (1981 long taxable bonds >16%, tax-exempts >14%), a typical 14%-ROE business "is no
  longer worth one hundred cents on the dollar" to a taxable individual; equity "in
  aggregate, produces no value-added for individual investors." Applauds Volcker but
  stays bearish: "Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable of maintenance,
  but not of restoration."
- [1983] The inflation hedge inverted: **asset-light franchises beat asset-heavy
  businesses** in inflation, because inflation forces every business to pour more
  capital into receivables, inventory and fixed assets just to stand still — and the
  asset-light franchise needs the least. "During inflation, [[Economic Goodwill]] is
  the gift that keeps giving," debunking the "In Goods We Trust" belief that tangible
  assets protect against inflation.
- [1986] Inflation drives Buffett's **aversion to long-term bonds**: "our fear that we
  will see much higher rates of inflation within the next decade," since "the behavior
  of our currency will be determined by the behavior of our legislators." In the
  Taxation essay he predicts the unrealistic 1988 tax rates will force "either higher
  tax rates or higher inflation rates... within, say, five years — and it would not
  surprise us to see both." NHP's property residuals are noted as a potential inflation
  hedge (see [[NHP]]).
- [1987] The **currency-debasement** thesis behind Buffett's bond aversion. The "enormous
  trade deficit" piles "claim checks" (U.S. bonds, deposits) in foreign hands; the
  government depends, "Blanche DuBois"-like, "on the kindness of strangers." When claim
  checks grow numerous and the issuer controls their purchasing power, "the pressure on
  the issuer to dilute their value by inflating the currency becomes almost
  irresistible" — for a debtor government inflation is "the economic equivalent of the
  'H' bomb." Procrastination ("I'll think about it tomorrow") will have "inflationary
  consequences," so Berkshire keeps "a general fear of long-term bonds."
- [1988] The aversion restated as near-permanent: "We will become enthused about [long-
  term bonds] only when we become enthused about prospects for long-term stability in the
  purchasing power of money. And that kind of stability isn't in the cards: both society
  and elected officials simply have too many higher-ranking priorities that conflict with
  purchasing-power stability." The only long-term bonds Berkshire holds remain the
  [[WPPSS Bonds]].
