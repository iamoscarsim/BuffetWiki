# Economic Moat

The "moat" is Buffett's enduring metaphor for a durable competitive advantage — the
barrier that protects a business's high returns from competitors the way a moat
protects a castle. Though the underlying idea runs through the letters from the start
(the franchise economics of [[See's Candies]], [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]),
the **image itself first appears in the [[1986]] letter**, applied to [[GEICO]]:

> "The difference between GEICO's costs and those of its competitors is a kind of
> **moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle**. No one
> understands this moat-around-the-castle concept better than Bill Snyder, Chairman
> of GEICO. He continually **widens the moat** by driving down costs still more,
> thereby defending and strengthening the economic franchise."

The 1986 framing carries the key nuances Buffett returns to for decades. The moat is
specific and measurable — GEICO's underwriting + loss-adjustment expense of 23.5% of
premiums, ~15 points below many major rivals. It must be **actively widened**, not
merely defended: [[Bill Snyder]] keeps cutting costs; a static advantage erodes. And
it is the source of the business's *value*, not just its safety — the moat is what
lets the castle keep earning.

The moat concept fuses several threads already in the wiki: the low-cost loops of
GEICO and [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]], the brand-and-pricing power of See's, and the
[[Economic Goodwill]] that such advantages create above tangible book. It is the
qualitative counterpart to [[Owner Earnings]] — the reason a franchise's earnings
prove durable.

## Evolution
- [1986] First use of the "moat-around-the-castle" image, for GEICO's low-cost edge
  (23.5% expense ratio); [[Bill Snyder]] praised for continually *widening* the moat.
- [1993] The metaphor applied to **consumer brands** in the risk essay (see [[Risk and Volatility]]): [[Coca-Cola]] (≈44% of world soft-drink volume) and [[Gillette]] (>60% of
  the blade market by value) have, through "the might of their brand names, the attributes of
  their products, and the strength of their distribution systems… an enormous competitive
  advantage, **setting up a protective moat around their economic castles**. The average
  company, in contrast, does battle daily without any such means of protection." Peter Lynch:
  stocks of commodity-product companies should carry the label "**Competition may prove
  hazardous to human wealth**." The moat is what lets an investor judge that Coke and Gillette
  carry far less *business* risk than "any computer company or retailer" — the **pet rock vs.
  Barbie** distinction beta cannot see.
- [1995] The metaphor returns to its origin, **[[GEICO]]**, as Berkshire buys the whole company:
  "In business, I look for **economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.'**" GEICO's moat is
  its rock-bottom cost (expense + loss-adjustment down to **23.6% of premiums** in 1995, nearly a
  point better than 1994) — "Thanks to [[Tony Nicely]] and his management team, **GEICO's moat
  widened in 1995**." Buffett also distinguishes "have-to-be-smart-once" businesses (a network TV
  station whose moat protects even "a shiftless and backward nephew") from
  "have-to-be-smart-every-day" retailers where "to coast is to fail" (see [[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]) — a reminder that moats vary enormously in durability.
- [1996] The moat language recurs for **[[GEICO]]** ("the economies of scale we enjoy should
  allow us to maintain or even **widen the protective moat surrounding our economic
  castle**") and matures into the broader concept of **[[The Inevitables]]** — companies like
  [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Gillette]] whose moats are so wide that worldwide dominance "for an
  investment lifetime" is, for investment purposes, knowable. Buffett pairs the optimism with
  its limits: leadership alone is no moat (GM, IBM, Sears), and the surest way to drain a moat
  is **loss of focus** (Coke once grew shrimp; Gillette explored for oil).
- [2005] **"Widening the moat" as the governing daily discipline.** Buffett gives the metaphor its
  fullest *operational* statement: "Every day… the competitive position of each of our businesses
  grows either weaker or stronger" through "almost unnoticeable actions" (delighting customers,
  cutting waste, improving products) — and when short- and long-term conflict, "**widening the moat
  must take precedence**." A manager who hits short-term earnings targets at the expense of costs,
  customer satisfaction or brand strength gets "behind the eight-ball," and "no amount of subsequent
  brilliance will overcome the damage" (the auto and airline industries the cautionary tales; Ben
  Franklin's "an ounce of prevention…"). Berkshire's managers "are passionate about… moat-widening."
- [2007] **The moat criteria, sharpened** (in the [[The Great the Good and the Gruesome|great/good/ gruesome]] essay). "A truly great business must have an **enduring 'moat'** that protects excellent
  returns on invested capital" — because "the dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will
  repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns." Two requirements follow:
  the moat must be in a **stable industry** ("a moat that must be continuously rebuilt will
  eventually be **no moat at all**"; "creative destruction… precludes investment certainty"), and it
  must **not depend on a single great manager** — "if a business requires a superstar… the business
  itself cannot be deemed great" ("you can count on the moat of the **Mayo Clinic** to endure, even
  though you can't name its CEO"). Low-cost (GEICO, Costco) and brand (Coke, Gillette, Amex) are the
  durable forms; "**Roman Candles**" are the businesses whose moats prove illusory.
