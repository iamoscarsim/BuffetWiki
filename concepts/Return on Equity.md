# Return on Equity

Return on equity capital is the yardstick Buffett uses to judge managerial
economic performance — and the [[1977]] letter opens with it. He dismisses the
common definition of "record" earnings as a new high in earnings per share,
pointing out that "even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily
rising interest earnings each year because of compounding." Because companies
routinely add to their equity base year after year, a rise in EPS can be
unimpressive once measured against the capital that produced it.

So, "except for special cases (for example, companies with unusual debt-equity
ratios or those with important assets carried at unrealistic balance sheet
values)," the better measure is **return on beginning equity capital**. In 1977
Berkshire earned **19%** on beginning equity — above its own long-term average and
that of American industry. Buffett pointedly notes that although operating EPS rose
37%, beginning capital was up 24%, "making the gain in earnings per share
considerably less impressive than it might appear at first glance."

This is the root of a lifelong theme: capital is not free, growth financed by
retained earnings must be judged by the return it earns, and EPS growth alone is a
vanity metric. It connects directly to his later insistence on retaining a dollar
only when it creates at least a dollar of market value.

## Evolution
- [1977] Established as the primary performance yardstick (19% on beginning
  equity); EPS growth dismissed as easily manufactured by compounding a growing
  capital base.
- [1978] 19.4% return on beginning equity (near the 1972 record). Buffett broadens
  the long-run measure to **per-share net worth growth** including capital gains:
  ~doubled over three years (~25% annual compounding). Cautions both rates are
  unsustainable as the insurance cycle turns. Introduces the **Sources of Earnings**
  table to show each business's contribution.
- [1979] 18.6% on beginning equity (down again); EPS up ~20% but dismissed — "even
  a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is
  low." States the durable rule: "the primary test of managerial economic
  performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital
  employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.)." For *long-term*
  results he uses book value at market: **$19.46 (9/30/64) → $335.85 (YE79),
  20.5% compounded** — see [[Financial Performance Over Time]].
- [1980] 17.8% on beginning equity (down again). Long-term book value reaches
  **$400.80** (16 yrs, still 20.5% compounded). The long-term return exceeds the
  average of yearly returns because non-controlled holdings' [[Look-Through Earnings]]
  translate into market-value gains over time. Concedes [[Financial Strength]]
  conservatism "penalizes" ROE "somewhat."
- [1981] 15.2% on beginning equity (down again). GAAP book value reaches **$526.02**
  (17 yrs, **21.1% compounded**). Buffett warns the rate "is highly likely to drift
  downward." Notes the accounting exclusion of [[Look-Through Earnings]] now
  "diminish[es] the utility of our annual return on equity calculation." Over half of
  1981's $124M net-worth gain came from GEICO's market move alone.
- [1982] **Yardstick formally retired for Berkshire.** ROE fell to 9.8%, but Buffett
  argues the figure has lost meaning given [[Look-Through Earnings]] — and warns
  against managers who, when results sour, "shoot the arrow... into a blank canvas and
  then carefully draw the bullseye around the implanted arrow." Shifts emphasis to
  **growth in net worth** (1982: +$208M, +40%; book value $737.43, 22.0% over 18 yrs)
  and to "economic" earnings. "Geometric progressions eventually forge their own
  anchors."
