# Premium Takeovers

Buffett's critique of the high-premium corporate takeover — why most acquisitions
that pay a large premium over market destroy value for the acquirer's shareholders.
It is the mirror image of Berkshire's own [[Acquisition Criteria]] and a recurring
target across the letters.

The classic statement is in the [[1981]] letter. Buffett would "rather buy 10% of
Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X." He diagnoses three
usually-unspoken motives behind premium takeovers: (1) managerial **animal spirits**
("the corporate pulse never beats faster than when an acquisition is in prospect");
(2) the worship of **size over profitability** (managers know their rank by sales,
not by return); and (3) the **"managerial kiss" delusion** — the fairy-tale belief
that the acquirer's touch will transform a toad of a target into a prince. His
verdict: "Investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors
instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the toad,
those kisses had better pack some real dynamite. We've observed many kisses but very
few miracles." Crucially, Berkshire "will not normally pay a lot... for what we are
supposed to bring to the party — for we find that we ordinarily don't bring a lot."

He allows two categories of genuinely great acquirers: those who bought
inflation-resistant franchises (pricing power + low capital needs), and rare
managerial superstars (Henry Singleton at Teledyne, [[Tom Murphy]] at [[Capital Cities]])
— who, tellingly, "have made very few deals in recent years, and often have found
repurchase of their own shares to be the most sensible employment of corporate
capital" ([[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]).

## Evolution
- [1981] Full statement: the toads-and-princesses critique, three takeover motives,
  "we don't bring a lot to the party," and praise for disciplined acquirers who
  prefer buybacks.
- [1982] On 1982's big deals: "managerial intellect wilted in competition with
  managerial adrenaline; the thrill of the chase blinded the pursuers to the
  consequences of the catch." Quotes Pascal: "all men's misfortunes spring from the
  single cause that they are unable to stay quietly in one room." Buffett confesses
  he "left the room once too often" and nearly "starred in the Acquisition Follies of
  1982" — a large committed deal that fell through "for reasons totally beyond our
  control" (his "blown deal" he'd put as a blank-page centerfold). See also
  [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]].
- [1992] The toads-and-princesses parable returns in full, now with Buffett implicating
  *himself*: acquisition-hungry managers "mesmerized by their childhood reading of… the
  frog-kissing princess… pay dearly for the right to kiss corporate toads," and when the
  toads croak, announce "an enormous 'restructuring' charge" (Santayana: "Fanaticism consists
  of redoubling your effort when you've forgotten your aim"). "In my early days as a manager I,
  too, dated a few toads… I kissed and they croaked." The lesson — via a golf pro's "**Practice
  doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent**" — is the resolution to "buy **good
  businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices**," realized in the
  [[Central States Indemnity]] purchase (see [[Acquisition Criteria]]).
- [1997] **The takeover calculus generalized.** "The reasoning that Berkshire applies to the merger
  of public companies *should* be the calculus for **all** buyers": paying a takeover premium makes
  sense only if (a) the buyer's stock is overvalued relative to the target, or (b) "the two
  enterprises will earn more combined than they would separately." Acquirers "normally hew to the
  second argument because very few are willing to acknowledge that their stock is overvalued" — but
  "**voracious buyers — the ones that issue shares as fast as they can print them — are tacitly
  conceding that point**," often running "Wall Street's version of a **chain-letter scheme**."
  Projected synergies are frequently "illusory," and "if a CEO is enthused about a particularly
  foolish acquisition, both his internal staff and his outside advisors will come up with whatever
  projections are needed to justify his stance. **Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they
  are naked.**" (See [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]].)
