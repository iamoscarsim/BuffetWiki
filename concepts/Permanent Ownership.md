# Permanent Ownership

Berkshire's unusual commitment to *keeping* the businesses it buys — a promise that
makes it the buyer of choice for owners who care what happens to their company and
employees after a sale. Stated in the [[1983]] business principles: "regardless of
price, we have no interest at all in selling any good businesses that Berkshire
owns, and are very reluctant to sell sub-par businesses as long as we expect them to
generate at least some cash and... feel good about their managers and labor
relations."

Buffett explicitly rejects **"gin rummy managerial behavior (discard your least
promising business at each turn)"**, accepting that this will "penalize our overall
results a bit." The stance flows from [[Owner Orientation and Candor]] and from
treating Berkshire as a permanent *home* for businesses and managers, not a portfolio
to be optimized by churn. It also has a practical edge: sellers of fine family
businesses (like the [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]) choose Berkshire precisely because
it won't flip or dismantle them. The policy is later codified in the Owner's Manual,
with the caveat that Berkshire will not throw good money after bad in a truly failing
operation (see the eventual textile closure).

## Evolution
- [1983] Stated as principle: won't sell good businesses at any price; very reluctant
  to sell sub-par ones; rejects "gin rummy" management even at a cost to results.
- [1986] Names **three permanent marketable holdings** — [[Capital Cities]]/ABC,
  [[GEICO]], [[Washington Post]] — that Berkshire "would not anticipate selling... even
  if these securities were to appear significantly overpriced," just as it would not
  sell See's or the Buffalo News. The "'til-death-do-us-part policy," set against the
  restructuring fad ("hate the sin but love the sinner") and the "institutional
  investor" oxymoron — managers who treat businesses as a "portfolio" to churn.
- [1987] Extended to marketable stocks under the heading "Marketable Securities —
  Permanent Holdings." Buffett "would not sell even though they became far overpriced"
  the three holdings ([[Capital Cities]]/ABC, [[GEICO]], [[Washington Post]]), viewing
  them "exactly like our successful controlled businesses" — with one qualifier (an
  insurance company could sell portions to pay "extraordinary insurance losses"). The
  stance is openly part-personal: "we would rather achieve a return of X while
  associating with people whom we strongly like and admire than realize 110% of X by
  exchanging these relationships." Churchill: "You shape your houses and then they shape
  you." But not dogmatic about *all* stocks — Berkshire will sell an ordinary holding if
  it becomes overvalued or to fund something more undervalued; only the three are
  inviolable. Quotes the "most foolish" Wall Street maxim: "You can't go broke taking a
  profit."
- [1988] The doctrine gets its most-quoted phrasing: when Berkshire owns "portions of
  outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, **our favorite holding period is
  forever**." Applied to the new [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Freddie Mac]] stakes; contrasted
  with those "who hurry to sell and book profits when companies perform well but…
  tenaciously hang on to businesses that disappoint" — Peter Lynch's "**cutting the
  flowers and watering the weeds**." The NYSE listing reinforces it: Berkshire wants
  long-term owners with "no timetable or price target for sale."
- [1989] The **tax math** behind buy-and-hold. Berkshire's deferred-tax liability on
  unrealized gains (~$1.1B) is reframed as "an **interest-free loan from the U.S. Treasury
  that comes due only at our election**" — a "transfer tax" payable only on selling. The
  famous illustration: a dollar that doubles 20 times *within a single holding* grows to
  ~$1,048,576 and leaves ~**$692,000** after one 34% tax, versus ~**$25,250** from 20
  separately-taxed doubles — a **27:1** edge from deferral alone. Yet Buffett insists the
  policy isn't *driven* by the math: "We have found splendid business relationships to be
  so rare and so enjoyable that we want to retain all we develop" — "akin to marrying for
  money — a mistake under most circumstances, insanity if one is already rich." The
  'til-death attitude also frames the **"single-dip"** future (see [[Intrinsic Business Value]]).
- [1990] Extended to **succession**. With [[Ken Chace]] retiring from the board, Buffett
  names **[[Susan Buffett]]** as the controlling-shareholder-to-be, who "shares my view
  that neither Berkshire nor its subsidiary businesses and important investments should be
  sold simply because some very high bid is received." Were he to die "tomorrow," "none of
  my stock would have to be sold" and a like-minded controller and manager would follow.
  The estate plans aim at "preserving the character of Berkshire and **returning the
  fortune to society**," not the family.
- [1993] The **"crown jewel"** argument: corporate managers grasp that "a parent company that
  owns a subsidiary with superb long-term economics is not likely to sell that entity
  regardless of price" — yet the same CEO will "offhandedly… move from business to business"
  in his personal portfolio on a broker's quip ("You can't go broke taking a profit"). "What
  makes sense in business also makes sense in stocks." The **Li'l Abner** parable sharpens the
  tax case for holding: a dollar doubling 27½ times in a *single* investment yields ~$130M
  after one final tax, dwarfing a succession of separately-taxed doubles — "tax-paying
  investors will realize a far, far greater sum from a single investment that compounds
  internally… than from a succession of investments compounding at the same rate." The lone
  exception this year — selling **one-third of [[Capital Cities]]** at $630 — is the kind of
  trim of a non-inviolable holding the policy always allowed, not a break from it.
- [1996] **Inactivity reframed as a virtue.** "We continue to make more money when **snoring**
  than when active" — feverishly trading "wonderful businesses" because of a Fed-rate forecast or
  a pundit's reversal makes as little sense for stocks as for subsidiaries. Buffett favors
  businesses "**unlikely to experience major change**," sought for their "virtual[] certain[ty]"
  of competitive strength "ten or twenty years from now" (see [[The Inevitables]]). Robert
  Woodruff, who built Coca-Cola, on when to sell Coke: "I don't know. **I've never sold any.**"
  And the discipline of holding winners even as they dominate a portfolio: selling down your best
  investments because they've grown large "is akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael
  Jordan because he has become so important to the team."
- [1998] **Permanence as the operating mandate, not just the investing stance.** Buffett tells
  Berkshire's CEOs to run each business as if "**you can't sell or merge it for at least a
  century**," precisely because Berkshire's shareholder base "has the longest investment horizon to
  be found in the public-company universe" — "a **majority of our shares are held by investors who
  expect to die still holding them**." That permanence is what lets him ask managers to "manage for
  maximum long-term value, rather than for next quarter's earnings" (see [[Decentralization]]). He
  reaffirms the personal pledge: "I'll keep at least **99% of my net worth in Berkshire** for as
  long as I am around."
- [2000] **Permanence as Berkshire's competitive edge in acquisitions.** Buffett argues the policy
  makes Berkshire "an almost unique home" and thus the **buyer of choice**: "When a business
  masterpiece has been created by a lifetime … of unstinting care … it should be important to the
  owner what corporation is entrusted to carry on its history." Because "Berkshire's ownership
  structure ensures that we can fulfill our promises," sellers can "take to the bank" assurances
  like keeping Justin headquartered in Fort Worth or not merging the Bridges' jeweler. The image:
  "How much better it is for the 'painter' of a business **Rembrandt** to personally select its
  permanent home than to have a trust officer or uninterested heirs auction it off … **We'll leave
  the auctions to others**" (see [[Acquisition Criteria]]).
