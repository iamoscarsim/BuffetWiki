# Issuing Shares for Acquisitions

Buffett's rule on using stock as acquisition currency, set out at length in the
[[1982]] letter: **"We will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic
business value as we give."** It is a direct corollary of [[Stocks as Fractional Ownership]]
and the antidote to the value destruction catalogued under [[Premium Takeovers]].

The core arithmetic: a company's stock often trades *below* intrinsic value, but a
seller in a negotiated deal commands *full* value. So an acquirer paying with
undervalued stock "must give up $2 of value to receive $1 of value" — "gold valued
as gold cannot be purchased intelligently through the utilization of gold — or even
silver — valued as lead." Buffett reframes the language to expose the trade: a
stock-funded "Company A to Acquire Company B" is really "Part of A sold to acquire
B," so issuing shares shrinks existing owners' stake in Berkshire's own
hard-to-match businesses (the 120-acre-farm analogy). He skewers the standard
rationalizations ("we have to grow"; the 51%-stock/49%-cash tax dodge — "a man is
not charmed if a spaniel defaces his lawn"), and argues the obsession with EPS
"dilution" misses the point: what matters is dilution of **intrinsic business
value**, not accounting EPS.

He names three honest ways to issue stock: (1) a true business-value-for-
business-value merger (the intended Berkshire–[[Blue Chip Stamps]] deal); (2) when
the acquirer's stock trades *at or above* intrinsic value; (3) repurchasing an equal
number of shares afterward to convert a stock deal into a cash deal ("damage-repair
repurchases"). And he warns of the **"double whammy"**: value-diluting issuance
destroys value *and* earns the stock a permanently lower price/value ratio, because
the market punishes managements shown to be "insensitive to the interests of
owners."

## Evolution
- [1982] Full essay. The $2-for-$1 logic, "Part of A sold to acquire B," the
  intrinsic-value (not EPS) test of dilution, three honest paths, and the
  double-whammy penalty.
- [1992] The rule restated as policy — "**we have a firm policy about issuing shares of
  Berkshire, doing so only when we receive as much value as we give**" — noted as hard to
  satisfy "since we have always valued our shares highly." Illustrated by an "amusing but
  value-destroying" anecdote: a bank Berkshire owned, its management "visibly in heat,"
  swapped stock for a smaller bank at over twice its own valuation — prompting the acquiree's
  owner to extract a promise that "once… I have become a major shareholder, you'll never again
  make a deal this dumb." The year's actual issuance — 6,106 shares from the **zero-coupon
  debenture** conversion (at $11,719/share) — was a redemption mechanic, not an acquisition;
  net worth's growth came >98% from earnings and appreciation. (Berkshire now has 1,152,547
  shares, barely above the 1,137,778 of October 1964.)
- [1993] **[[Dexter Shoe]]** is a rare instance of Berkshire actually *paying in stock*
  (25,203 shares). Buffett defends it on the ground that the shares were issued "above their
  book value" and that sellers [[Harold Alfond]] and [[Peter Lunder]] (who wanted no cash)
  got fair value — but the deal silently violates the very test of this page, since he handed
  over a fraction of Berkshire's wonderful businesses for a single shoe company that would
  later wither. It becomes the textbook cautionary case for the $2-for-$1 logic (see
  [[Dexter Shoe]], [[Mistakes Ledger]]) — the cost being not what Dexter earned, but the
  forgone compounding of the Berkshire stock surrendered.
- [1994] Berkshire asks shareholders to **authorize preferred stock**, after a failed
  attempt to merge with a large family-controlled business "on terms that included a Berkshire
  convertible preferred." Buffett wants the flexibility "to move quickly" on a similar deal —
  but pledges the familiar test: "you can be sure that Charlie and I will not use these shares
  without being completely satisfied that we are **receiving as much in intrinsic value as we
  are giving**." The capital-allocation companion (see [[Capital Allocation]]) is his attack on
  EPS-accretion thinking — a deal must raise *per-share intrinsic value*, not reported earnings.
- [1995] Berkshire **issued stock for two acquisitions** ([[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]] and
  [[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]), defending it as a feature, not a lapse: a stock "backed by an
  extraordinary collection of outstanding businesses" lets a tax-sensitive family seller **defer
  personal taxes indefinitely** while gaining diversification and a permanent home. Buffett judges
  "this calculus played an important part" in both deals (shares outstanding rose only 1.3%).
  Separately, the **[[Class B Shares]]** offering (≥$100M) is framed as *not* dilutive precisely
  because the stock is **not undervalued** at $36,000 — "the offering we propose will not diminish
  the per-share intrinsic value of our existing stock" — the mirror image of the $2-for-$1 trap.
- [1997] **"A Confession" — the blunt empirical verdict.** Aggregating *all* of Berkshire's
  stock-only mergers (excluding the affiliated Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip deals), Buffett
  finds "**our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the
  transactions** … when I've issued stock, I've cost you money" — and notably *not* because sellers
  underperformed (they were candid and effective). The cause is structural: Berkshire's roster is
  "filled with **.380 hitters**," so "trading away a portion of them for something new almost never
  makes sense" (the baseball corollary to the $2-for-$1 logic; Camelot's Mordred — "Everybody is
  above him"). Hence the strong preference for **cash** (its great cash buys — NICO, See's, Buffalo
  News, Scott Fetzer, GEICO — "delivered Berkshire tremendous value"), and, when stock *must* be
  used, an **attractive cash option** to tempt sellers away from it (see [[International Dairy Queen]], [[FlightSafety International]]). For **public-company** mergers the discipline is a
  general rule (see [[Premium Takeovers]]): a premium is justified only if the buyer's stock is
  overvalued *or* genuine synergy exists.
- [1998] **Stock-funded deals and the book-value illusion.** Berkshire paid for **[[General Re]]**
  (all stock, ~$22B) and **[[Executive Jet]]** (half stock) — and the resulting per-share *book*
  value jumped because Berkshire trades at a large premium to book, so issuing shares "instantly
  increases our per-share book-value figure, even though we've earned not a dime." But per-share
  **intrinsic** value didn't get that bump: "what we give and what we get are roughly equal." This
  is the same $2-for-$1 arithmetic viewed from the *high-premium* side — issuing fairly-valued
  (here, premium-to-book) stock for fairly-valued businesses neither creates nor destroys intrinsic
  value, even as it flatters book. (See [[Intrinsic Business Value]] for the 48.3% "not as good as
  it looks" point.)
- [1999] **Cash wins on taxes, too.** Both 1999 acquisitions ([[Jordan's Furniture]],
  [[MidAmerican Energy]]) were bought **for cash, "issuing no Berkshire shares"** — "the method of
  acquisition that Charlie and I vastly prefer." In the acquisition-accounting essay Buffett ranks
  the **tax** structures: buying **assets for cash** lets the buyer amortize goodwill over 15 years
  (a real cash boost — done with Jordan's); buying **stock for cash** loses that deduction; and a
  **stock-for-stock** deal is "**the worst of all**" (a huge price with no basis step-up, so the
  seller may owe capital-gains tax even on a real economic loss). Berkshire has "**never done an
  inefficiently-structured deal … to make our figures look better**." He predicts that ending
  **pooling** would make managers "structure acquisitions more sensibly … based on the real
  consequences for their shareholders rather than … their reported earnings." (See [[Economic Goodwill]] for the goodwill-amortization argument.)
- [2000] **The Dexter confession crystallizes the whole doctrine.** Of the eight 2000 acquisitions,
  **97% was paid in cash** (shares up just ⅓ of 1%) — and Buffett ties the discipline back to his
  worst error: "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for **[[Dexter Shoe]]** in 1993.
  Furthermore, I **compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment**."
  The lesson is the page's thesis made vivid: handing over a slice of Berkshire's wonderful
  businesses (shares that kept compounding) for a single business that went to zero multiplied the
  loss many times the headline price. Stock issuance is thus reserved for the rare case (Ben Bridge
  and U.S. Liability were half-stock by the sellers' preference), always with the cash-preference
  default (see [[Mistakes Ledger]]).
- [2009] **"An Inconvenient Truth (Boardroom Overheating)" — the doctrine at full force.** The
  **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]]** deal forced Berkshire to issue ~95,000 shares (6.1% of
  those outstanding): "**Charlie and I enjoy issuing Berkshire stock about as much as we relish
  prepping for a colonoscopy.**" Buffett gives the cleanest statement of the rule: if you wouldn't
  sell the *whole* company at the current price, don't "sell" a piece of it by issuing stock in a
  merger. The **A & B parable** makes it arithmetic — two firms each intrinsically worth $100 but
  trading at $80; A's CEO, "long on confidence and short on smarts," swaps 1¼ A-shares per B-share,
  handing B-holders $125 of value to get $100, leaving A's owners poorer while the CEO "now runs a
  company twice as large." Overvalued stock, by contrast, is "counterfeit money" for acquirers (the
  late-1960s "air-for-assets" era). On BNSF specifically: sellers valued the offer at $100/share, but
  since 40% was paid in (undervalued) stock and Berkshire already owned a chunk for cash, only ~30% of
  the total cost was stock — "**a close one**… if we had needed to use more stock… it would have made
  no sense." Two memorable jabs: the **Chihuahua-vs-St.-Bernard** bank-merger story (managers "in
  heat" overpaying 3× book "to show that we are in the hunt") and "**Don't ask the barber whether you
  need a haircut**" — directors should hire a *second* advisor paid only if the deal is killed.
- [2014] **"You can't get rich trading a hundred-dollar bill for eight tens."** In the 50th-anniversary
  essay Buffett re-quantifies **[[Dexter Shoe]]**: the shares he handed over are now worth **$5.7
  billion** for a business that "went to zero" — "as a financial disaster, this one deserves a spot in
  the Guinness Book of World Records." The rule, stated as a vow for his successors: "**the intrinsic
  value of the shares you give in an acquisition must not be greater than the intrinsic value of the
  business you receive**." He skewers the banker's stock-deal playbook (customary premiums,
  EPS-accretion, conjured "synergies," absent post-mortems) and the 1960s conglomerate "hocus-pocus":
  "**serial issuances of overpriced shares… most assuredly redistribute wealth, but in no way create
  it.**" Munger's paired essay: Berkshire bought with cash because "it was hard to get anything in
  exchange for Berkshire stock that was as valuable as what was given up." The flip side is celebrated
  this year — swapping low-basis Phillips 66 and Graham Holdings *stock* for whole businesses
  (tax-efficient and value-accretive).
