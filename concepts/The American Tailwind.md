# The American Tailwind

Buffett's name — coined in the [[2018]] letter's capstone essay — for the force he credits with much of
Berkshire's success: the long-run, compounding prosperity of the American economy. It is the synthesis of
an optimism theme that runs through the late letters (the [[2015]] "bet on America" / "luckiest crop in
history" essay; the [[2016]] "miraculous… $90 trillion" essay) and the practical core of why Buffett
counsels **owning American business and sitting still** ([[Index Funds and Fees]], [[Risk and Volatility]]).

**The 1942 calculation.** Buffett anchors the essay in his **first investment — March 11, 1942**, age 11:
**$114.75** into three shares of Cities Service preferred, amid the darkest news of World War II. Had that
sum instead gone into a **no-fee S&P 500 index fund** with dividends reinvested, it would have grown to
**$606,811** by January 31, 2019 — a **5,288-for-1** gain. Scaled up, a $1-million tax-free institution of
1942 would hold **$5.3 billion** — but **only $2.65 billion** had it paid a mere **1% of assets per year**
to "helpers" (the 11.8% market return recalculated at 10.8%): the recurring fee critique, in compounding
form.

**Gold vs. mettle.** To skewer doom-mongering, Buffett notes the national debt rose **~400-fold (40,000%)**
over those 77 years — yet someone who, fearing "runaway deficits and a worthless currency," had bought
gold with the same $114.75 (3¼ ounces) would hold only ~**$4,200**, "less than 1%" of the index result.
"**The magical metal was no match for the American mettle.**"

**The deeper point.** Prosperity arrived "in a bipartisan manner" (7 Republican + 7 Democratic presidents,
through inflation, a 21% prime rate, wars, a presidential resignation, and the 2008 panic — "all
engendered scary headlines; all are now history"). Its engine is the same as Berkshire's: **retained
earnings** — in the national accounts, "**savings**." Household wealth reached **$108 trillion**. Buffett
pairs the optimism with humility — "the tidy rows of simple white crosses at Normandy should shame those
who" claim to have "done it alone" — and hope that other nations thrive too. "Over the next 77 years,
however, the major source of our gains will almost certainly be provided by The American Tailwind. We are
lucky — gloriously lucky — to have that force at our back."

## Evolution
- [[2015]] The "bet on America" essay — GDP per capita six times its 1930 level; "the babies being born…
  are the luckiest crop in history"; "for 240 years it's been a terrible mistake to bet against America."
- [[2016]] The "miraculous" essay — $90 trillion of national wealth from a standing start 240 years ago;
  fear is the investor's friend, personal fear the enemy.
- [[2018]] **"The American Tailwind"** named: the 1942 $114.75 → $606,811 index calculation; the fee and
  gold comparisons; retained earnings/"savings" as the engine; Normandy humility.
- [[2019]] Reaffirmed and married to **[[The Power of Retained Earnings|Smith's compounding]]**: if rates
  and corporate tax stay low, "**equities will over time perform far better than long-term, fixed-rate debt
  instruments**." The warning attached: markets can drop "50% magnitude or even greater," so the Tailwind
  rewards only "the individual who does not use borrowed money and who can control his or her emotions.
  **Others? Beware!**"
- [[2020]] **"Never bet against America."** The optimism essay relocates from Wall Street to Main Street:
  in "A Tale of Two Cities," Buffett celebrates the founders behind See's, GEICO, National Indemnity,
  Nebraska Furniture Mart, Clayton and Pilot — émigrés and small-town strivers (Mrs. B's $2,500; "Big
  Jim" Haslam's $6,000 service station) who "needed America's framework for prosperity — a unique
  experiment when it was crafted in 1789." Despite "severe interruptions," U.S. economic progress "has
  been breathtaking," and the country retains its aspiration toward "a more perfect union." The conclusion
  is blunt: "**Never bet against America.**" (Mrs. B's holiday song — "God Bless America.")
- [[2021]] **"When you see the flag, say thanks."** The optimism case is recast as the **government-as-
  silent-partner** story: in the nine years after the 1955 textile merger Berkshire paid the Treasury just
  **$337,359** ("$100 per day"); after 1965's redeployment it now pays "**~$9 million daily**." Buffett
  again anchors on his **March 11, 1942** first purchase ($114.75 of Cities Service; the Dow at 99) —
  "**Never bet against America**" — and credits Berkshire's success to having "operated in America":
  "Absent our American home… Berkshire would never have come close to becoming what it is today."
- [[2022]] **"I owe the country no less."** The optimism case is framed through **federal taxes**: Berkshire
  paid **$32 billion** of corporate income tax over the decade ending 2021 (~0.1% of all Treasury receipts;
  had ~1,000 companies matched it, no other business or household "would have needed to pay any taxes").
  Buffett expects to "pay much more… during the next decade. We owe the country no less: America's dynamism
  has made a huge contribution to whatever success Berkshire has achieved." "I have been investing for 80
  years… I have yet to see a time when it made sense to make a **long-term bet against America**."
- [[2023]] **"America has been a terrific country for investors."** Buffett anchors again on March 11,
  1942 (the Dow fell below 100 that day; "I was down about $5 by the time school was out") — "now that
  index hovers around 38,000. All they have needed to do is **sit quietly, listening to no one**." The
  unchanging rule: "**Never risk permanent loss of capital**… the arena in which we operate has been — and
  will be — rewarding if you make a couple of good decisions during a lifetime and avoid serious mistakes."
  Embodied in his sister **[[Bertie Buffett|Bertie]]**, who made "no new trades" for 43 years and "became
  very rich."
- [[2024]] **"So thank you, Uncle Sam."** The optimism case crescendos through a **record tax payment**:
  Berkshire paid the IRS **$26.8 billion in 2024** — "about 5% of what all of corporate America paid," and
  **more federal income tax than any company in U.S. history** — bringing cumulative payments since 1965
  past **$101 billion**, all enabled by paying just **one dividend ever** (1967) and reinvesting. "**America
  is Exhibit A**": from "a base of only four million people… America changed the world in the blink of a
  celestial eye." A warning rides alongside the optimism: "**Paper money can see its value evaporate if
  fiscal folly prevails**… Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency" — Buffett
  asks the Treasury to "maintain a stable currency" and "take care of the many who… get the short straws."
