# The Power of Retained Earnings

The thesis — given its fullest, named treatment in the [[2019]] letter — that the **compounding of
reinvested corporate earnings** is the deep engine of equity returns, and the reason Berkshire's stock
holdings are worth far more than the dividends they pay. It is the conceptual root system beneath
[[Look-Through Earnings]], [[Capital Allocation]], and [[The American Tailwind]].

**Edgar Lawrence Smith and Keynes.** Buffett credits the 1924 book *Common Stocks as Long Term
Investments* by **Edgar Lawrence Smith** — "an obscure economist and financial advisor" — which "changed
the investment world." Smith set out to prove stocks beat bonds in inflation and lose in deflation, but
"the facts" refused to cooperate; his book "began… with a confession: 'These studies are the record of a
failure — the failure of facts to sustain a preconceived theory.'" The discovery, as Smith's reviewer
**John Maynard Keynes** crystallized it: "Well-managed industrial companies do not, as a rule, distribute
to the shareholders the whole of their earned profits… **there is an element of compound interest
operating in favour of a sound industrial investment.**" Before Smith, "Gentlemen preferred bonds" and
stocks were seen as short-term speculations — even though Carnegie, Rockefeller, and Ford had visibly
grown rich by retaining and reinvesting earnings.

**Why it matters for Berkshire — the "non-recognition of earnings."** GAAP records, in Berkshire's
reported operating earnings, only the **dividends** received from its non-controlled stock holdings — not
its share of the **retained earnings** those investees plow back. For most companies that omission is
trivial; for Berkshire it is "a standout omission." The [[2019]] letter quantifies it with a table of the
**ten largest holdings**: Berkshire's share was **$3,798M of dividends against $8,332M of retained
earnings**. Those retained dollars are "working hard and creating much added value" — expanding the
businesses or, often, **repurchasing shares** that quietly raise Berkshire's ownership (the [[American Express]] 12.6%→18.7% case). Over time, Buffett argues, the group will "**realize capital gains at least
equal to — and probably better than — the earnings… they retained**" (consistent with **Owner Principle
6**, that retained earnings deliver ≥$1 of value per $1). "**Mr. Smith got it right.**"

The same compounding logic governs Berkshire itself: it pays **no dividend**, having retained essentially
all earnings since 1965, turning the original $22M of capital into the federation of the [[The Five Groves|five groves]]. In the national accounts the equivalent is **"savings"** ([[The American Tailwind]]).
