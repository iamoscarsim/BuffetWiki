# Trade Deficit and the Dollar

One of Buffett's rare sustained **macro** positions: a mid-2000s bearishness on the U.S. dollar,
rooted in a worry about the persistent **trade (current-account) deficit**. He frames it carefully —
"the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters… we have made few macro
forecasts at Berkshire" — but acts on it nonetheless.

The thesis ([[2003]]): the trade deficit is "**force-feeding huge amounts of claims on, and ownership
in, America to the rest of the world**." Foreigners' appetite for U.S. assets, once ample, "started
choking on this diet" in late 2002, and the dollar began to slide; prevailing rates wouldn't close
the gap, so foreigners "will continue to be flooded with dollars," with consequences that "could…
be troublesome." Because Berkshire holds many billions of dollar cash-equivalents, Buffett bought
**foreign-currency contracts** as "at least a partial offset."

He dramatizes the idea in a 2003 *Fortune* article and, in [[2005]], the famous **"Squanderville
vs. Thriftville"** parable: a nation that consistently consumes more than it produces must sell off
ever more of its assets (and IOUs) to foreigners, becoming a "sharecropper society" working to pay
the carrying cost of past over-consumption.

## Evolution
- [2003] The thesis stated; Berkshire holds **~$12 billion of foreign-exchange contracts** across
  five currencies (entered 2002, enlarged 2003), plus ~$1B of euro-denominated junk bonds. FX
  contributed **$825M** of realized gains.
- [2004] The FX position is held/grown (~$21.4B at peak); Buffett expands the trade-deficit argument
  and the "**sharecropper society**" warning — Americans risk working to service foreign-owned
  claims on U.S. output.
- [2005] **"Squanderville vs. Thriftville."** The direct-currency bet **cost money** as the dollar
  strengthened, so Buffett begins **shifting the approach**: rather than holding forward contracts,
  he prefers to own **foreign earnings** — buying businesses and stocks that earn in other
  currencies — and pares the direct FX position.
- [2006] The direct foreign-currency position is **largely wound down** (a modest cumulative
  profit since 2002, ~$2.2B), as Berkshire increasingly gets its currency diversification through
  **foreign operating earnings** (e.g. ISCAR) and foreign equities rather than forwards.
- [2007] The currency theme recedes from the letters as the dollar-hedging is mostly expressed
  through owning non-U.S. businesses; the trade-deficit worry remains but the explicit FX trade is
  essentially over.
