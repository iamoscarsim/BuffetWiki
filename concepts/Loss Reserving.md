# Loss Reserving

The estimation of an insurer's future claim costs — and the central reason a
property-casualty company's reported earnings are, in Buffett's words, only "a
first rough draft of earnings." The subject of a candid [[1984]] essay and a
recurring theme thereafter (Berkshire's huge later [[General Re]] and asbestos/
environmental reserve issues belong here too).

Loss expense in a given year combines: (1) losses paid; (2) estimates for reported-
but-unsettled claims; (3) **IBNR** — "incurred but not reported"; and (4) revisions
of prior years' estimates. Because revisions can lag "decades," an error in year X
distorts both year X *and* the year of correction. Buffett illustrates with a claim
estimated at $10,000 in 1979 that settles for $100,000 in 1984 — misstating both
years. The honest goal: a reserve with "a 50-50 chance of being proved either
slightly too high or slightly too low."

He confesses Berkshire's own reserves had been **repeatedly too optimistic**
(publishing a table of restated underwriting losses), apologizing because it
misled shareholders and caused tax overpayments, and blaming casualty/reinsurance
long tails, "deep pocket" jury trends, and clients' own reserving errors flowing
through to Berkshire. He warns the imprecision invites abuse: "the corpse is
supposed to file the death certificate," and insolvent insurers can be "broke but
flush" — the "walking dead" who write at any price and stick solvent insurers
(including Berkshire, via guaranty-fund assessments) with the bill. Memorably:
"We buried Dad in a rented suit."

## Evolution
- [1984] Full essay. IBNR and the four loss components; Berkshire's own reserves
  too optimistic (table + apology); the "rented suit"/"corpse files the death
  certificate"/"broke but flush" lessons on industry abuse.
- [1986] **Third straight year of under-reserving** revealed by the 1985 reserve
  development — "what I told you last year about our loss liabilities was far from
  true... If the physiological rules that applied to Pinocchio were to apply to me, my
  nose would now draw crowds." Coins a memorable critique of the euphemism "**reserve
  strengthening**": when executives belatedly set proper reserves, the noble-sounding
  phrase is really a "correction of previous untruths (albeit non-intentional ones)."
  Berkshire "made a special effort" to reserve accurately at year-end 1986 — but had
  tried just as hard at year-end 1985, so "only time will tell." See [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [1987] Turns the critique on **auditors**. Loss development "looks better this year,"
  but with so much **long-tail** business (product liability, D&O) "one year of reserve
  development tells you very little." Investors should "be very suspicious of any
  earnings figures reported by insurers (including our own)." He faults auditors not for
  being unable to assess reserves but for "failing to publicly acknowledge that they
  can't do this job," and drafts a candid substitute opinion letter ("We can express no
  opinion about the accuracy of these figures…"). Where "earnings can be created by the
  stroke of a pen, the dishonest will gather. For them, long-tail insurance is heaven."
- [1998] **Loss reserves as a merger-accounting weapon.** In the "Accounting — Part 2" essay (see
  [[Owner Orientation and Candor]]), Buffett exposes a P&C-specific trick: a buyer of an insurer
  "sometimes simultaneously increases its loss reserves, often substantially," coinciding "uncann[ily]"
  with the inking of a deal — which "sets up the possibility of 'earnings' flowing into income at
  some later date, as reserves are released." Berkshire's counter-policy: "**In all of our
  acquisitions, we have left the loss reserve figures exactly as we found them**," having joined only
  with managers "knowledgeable about their business and honest in their financial reporting." When an
  acquirer immediately and substantially raises a target's reserves, "simple logic says that at least
  one of those virtues must have been lacking — or … the acquirer is laying the groundwork for future
  infusions of 'earnings.'" *(The irony — given General Re's later reserve shortfalls — will surface
  in subsequent letters.)*
- [2001] **The irony lands — the "loss development" essay.** [[General Re]] belatedly recognized
  **$800 million** of loss costs that "actually occurred in earlier years, but… were not then
  recorded" — exactly the under-reserving Buffett had warned of, now striking Berkshire's own
  marquee reinsurer. He demands the euphemisms be scrapped: "loss development" and its "equally ugly
  twin, **reserve strengthening**" wrongly suggest "some natural, uncontrollable event"; the truth
  is "management made an **error in estimation**." The 2001 essay reprises the canonical images —
  the **"rented suit"** funeral parable, and insurance accounting as a **"self-graded exam"** in
  which "the corpse… file[s] the death certificate." Under-reserving misled General Re into
  underpricing, overstated profits, excess incentive pay, and premature taxes. Buffett rejects
  proposals to **discount** P/C reserves (since they can't be precisely set, discounting would
  "exacerbate" inadequacy and "provide a new tool for the companies… inclined to fudge"). See
  [[General Re]], [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [2002] **The shortfall deepens — and Buffett names the cure.** At year-end 2001 General Re had
  "attempted to reserve adequately… but we failed badly," so 2002 absorbed an **additional $1.31B**
  to "correct the estimation mistakes of earlier years" ("I wish I didn't know now what I didn't
  know then"). Buffett identifies the root: "the **natural tendency of most casualty-insurance
  managers is to underreserve**," requiring "a particular mindset — which… has nothing to do with
  actuarial expertise" to overcome, and reinsurers face far harder reserving than primary insurers.
  He makes avoiding inadequate reserving Berkshire's "top priority going forward." The lesson
  generalizes: "any insurer that has no idea what its costs are is heading for big trouble."
- [2005] **"Surprises in insurance are far from symmetrical."** On buying **[[Medical Protective]]**,
  Berkshire immediately **raised its loss reserves by ~$125M** for the long-tail malpractice claims
  it inherited — "no one knows with any precision what amount will be required." Buffett's maxim:
  "You are lucky if you get [a pleasant surprise] for every ten that go the other way," dramatized by
  the switchblade-fight joke ("You never touched me." — "Just wait until you try to shake your
  head."). The conservative reserve add is the opposite of the acquisition-accounting reserve
  *games* he condemned in 1998 — here he over-reserves a target's book, not to manufacture future
  earnings but to face reality.
