# Value and Growth

One of Buffett's most-quoted clarifications, set out in the [[1992]] letter: the
conventional opposition between "value" and "growth" investing is a false one. "We view
that as fuzzy thinking… the two approaches are **joined at the hip**: Growth is *always* a
component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range
from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be **negative as well as positive**."

The argument runs through several blades:

- **"Value investing" is redundant.** "What is 'investing' if it is not the act of seeking
  value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid?" Knowingly paying more than
  calculated value, hoping to resell higher, "should be labeled **speculation**."
- **Cheap metrics ≠ value; rich metrics ≠ no value.** A low price/book, low P/E, or high
  dividend yield is "far from determinative"; the opposite characteristics are "in no way
  inconsistent with a 'value' purchase."
- **Growth can destroy value.** Growth only helps "when each dollar used to finance the
  growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value." In a low-return business
  needing capital, "growth hurts the investor." The domestic airline industry is the
  cautionary case: "it would have been far better if Orville had failed to get off the
  ground at Kitty Hawk."
- **The unifying formula.** Buffett invokes **[[John Burr Williams]]'** *Theory of
  Investment Value*: the worth of any asset is its future cash flows discounted at an
  appropriate rate — the same equation "for stocks as for bonds," differing only because an
  equity analyst must *estimate* the future "coupons" (which management quality can
  dramatically swing). The cheapest asset by this test is the one to buy, "irrespective of
  whether the business grows or doesn't."

This synthesis ties together [[Intrinsic Business Value]] (the discounted-cash definition),
[[Margin of Safety]] (the discipline on price), [[Circle of Competence]] (only estimate
coupons you can), and [[Good Business at a Fair Price]] (the best business compounds
incremental capital at high returns).

## Evolution
- [1992] Full statement: growth and value "joined at the hip"; "value investing"
  redundant; growth can be value-negative; the Williams DCF formula as the single
  underlying definition.
- [2000] Restated inside the **[[Bird in the Bush]]** (Aesop) essay, at the height of the dot-com
  mania: common yardsticks (dividend yield, P/E, P/B, growth rates) "have nothing to do with
  valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows," and
  "**growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs … that exceed the discounted value of the
  cash those assets will generate**." The verdict on the style labels sharpens: "Market commentators
  and investment managers who glibly refer to '**growth' and 'value' styles** as contrasting
  approaches to investment are **displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication**. Growth is
  simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation."
