# Zero-Coupon Bonds

A **zero-coupon bond** pays no current interest; the investor's yield comes entirely
from buying at a deep discount to maturity value. Buffett devotes a major [[1989]]
essay to them — prompted by Berkshire's own issue of **$902.6 million face of
zero-coupon convertible debentures** (sold at 44.314% of face for ~$400M proceeds,
15-year, convertible at a 15% premium; underwritten by [[Salomon]] Brothers). For
Berkshire the structure was a genuine positive: it deducts the accruing 5.5% interest
for tax each year while paying no cash, producing positive cash flow.

But the essay's real target is **abuse**. Buffett traces the instrument's honorable
roots — the WWII **Series E Savings Bond** (a zero in all but name) and the benign
**Treasury "strips"** that solved pension funds' reinvestment-risk problem — before
turning to "what the wise do in the beginning, fools do in the end." In the late-1980s
junk-and-LBO era, zeros and **PIK** ("pay-in-kind") bonds let ever-shakier credits
borrow on the principle that "it is impossible to default on a promise to pay nothing":
the time "between folly and failure" is simply stretched out. Their spread was greased
by the **"EBDIT"** yardstick (Earnings Before Depreciation, Interest and Taxes) — Buffett
calls ignoring depreciation "clearly delusional," since maintenance capex "is every bit
as real an expense as labor" (the [[Owner Earnings]] point), and then by the further
trick of measuring EBDIT against *cash* interest only, ignoring accrued zero/PIK interest
entirely. He invokes Galbraith's **"bezzle"** (undiscovered embezzlement that makes both
parties feel richer) and demands investment bankers act like a "**responsible
bartender**… who refuses the profit from the next drink to avoid sending a drunk out on
the highway." The footnote that aged grimly: S&Ls bought these bonds with insured
deposits, booking interest they never received — "it was the managers who should have
been wearing the ski masks."

His rule: "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT — or whenever someone
creates a capital structure that does not allow all interest… to be comfortably met out
of current cash flow *net of ample capital expenditures* — zip up your wallet."

## Evolution
- [1989] Full essay. Berkshire's own $902.6M zero issue (a legitimate, cash-flow-positive
  use); the history from Series E and Treasury strips to junk/PIK abuse; the EBDIT and
  "cash interest only" frauds; the "bezzle"; "bartender morality"; the S&L disaster.
- [1997] **Zeros as a Berkshire *investment*, not just an issue.** Berkshire's largest
  non-traditional position at year-end was **$4.6 billion (amortized cost) of long-term
  zero-coupon U.S. Treasuries** — a deliberate **macro bet on falling interest rates**, since
  zeros "pay no interest" and so "their market prices move rapidly when interest rates change."
  Rates fell in 1997, producing a **$598.8M** unrealized pre-tax gain (reflected in year-end book
  value). This is the **benign Treasury-strips** use Buffett distinguished in 1989 from junk/PIK
  abuse — bought as an alternative to cash when no wonderful business was available (see
  [[Unconventional Commitments]]).
