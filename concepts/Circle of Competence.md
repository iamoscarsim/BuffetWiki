# Circle of Competence

The discipline of investing only in businesses one can genuinely understand. It is
the first of the four [[Acquisition Criteria]] — "one that we can understand" —
stated in the [[1977]] letter, and it governs both what Berkshire buys and what it
deliberately passes by. The size of the circle matters less than knowing its
boundary. This principle later becomes Buffett's stated reason for long avoiding
technology businesses (see the technology thread as it develops) and for
concentrating in insurance, consumer brands, and media where he could assess
durable economics.

## Evolution
- [1977] Embedded as criterion #1 ("a business we can understand").
- [1992] Sharpened in the value/growth essay (see [[Value and Growth]]). Because estimating
  a business's future cash "coupons" is where even smart analysts go wrong, Berkshire copes
  in two ways: first, "**we try to stick to businesses we believe we understand**" — "relatively
  simple and stable in character," since "if a business is complex or subject to constant
  change, we're not smart enough to predict future cash flows." The memorable formulation of
  the principle's spirit: "**what counts for most people in investing is not how much they
  know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know.**" Paired with a
  [[Margin of Safety]], it means "an investor needs to do very few things right as long as he
  or she avoids big mistakes."
- [1993] The **"pet rock vs. Barbie"** test and the case for staying inside the circle. In the
  risk essay (see [[Risk and Volatility]]), Buffett notes that for "many industries… Charlie and
  I can't determine whether we are dealing with a 'pet rock' or a 'Barbie'" — and couldn't
  "even if we were to spend years intensely studying those industries," whether from "our own
  intellectual shortcomings" or "the nature of the industry." A business "that must deal with
  fast-moving technology is not going to lend itself to reliable evaluations of its long-term
  economics" (he cites his failure to foresee the TV-manufacturing and computer industries
  thirty years prior). The conclusion: "**We'll stick instead with the easy cases. Why search
  for a needle buried in a haystack when one is sitting in plain sight?**" An ordinary investor
  "by confining himself to a relatively few, easy-to-understand cases… can judge investment
  risks with a useful degree of accuracy."
- [1994] Two compact restatements. "**The truly big investment idea can usually be explained
  in a short paragraph**" — Berkshire likes "a business with enduring competitive advantages…
  run by able and owner-oriented people." And the **Olympic-diving** point: the investor's
  "scorecard is not computed using Olympic-diving methods — degree-of-difficulty doesn't count.
  If you are right about a business whose value is largely dependent on a single key factor that
  is both easy to understand and enduring, the payoff is the same" as solving a far harder case.
  Hence Berkshire prefers to **price, not time**, purchases of understandable businesses (see
  [[Acquisition Criteria]]).
- [1996] The clearest **advice to the ordinary investor**: most people "will find that the best
  way to own common stocks is through an **index fund** that charges minimal fees." For those who
  build their own portfolio, "intelligent investing is not complex" but requires "the ability to
  correctly evaluate **selected** businesses … You only have to be able to evaluate companies
  within your circle of competence. The **size of that circle is not very important; knowing its
  boundaries, however, is vital.**" One need not understand "beta, efficient markets, modern
  portfolio theory, option pricing or emerging markets" — "you may, in fact, be better off knowing
  nothing of these." Only two courses matter: "**How to Value a Business, and How to Think About
  Market Prices.**" Buffett favors businesses "unlikely to experience major change" because a
  fast-changing industry "precludes the certainty we seek" (see [[The Inevitables]]).
- [1999] **Why Berkshire owns no tech stocks** — stated at the height of the dot-com mania. "We
  share the general view that our society will be transformed by [tech] products and services," but
  "our problem — which we can't solve by studying up — is that **we have no insights into which
  participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage.**" The strength,
  he says, "is in **recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when
  we are approaching the perimeter**" — predicting fast-changing industries "is simply far beyond
  our perimeter." Crucially, he refuses to be shamed by others' validated claims: "we neither envy
  nor emulate them … If we stray, we will have done so inadvertently, not because we got restless
  and **substituted hope for rationality**." (He extends the humility beyond tech: "we bring nothing
  to the table when it comes to evaluating patents, manufacturing processes or geological
  prospects.") A timeless complement to the same year's market-valuation warning.
- [2004] **"Mono-linked chains" — the probability case for simplicity.** Drawing the lesson from
  [[MidAmerican Energy]]'s failed **$579M California zinc-recovery project** (where "we solved most
  of the problems. But one proved intractable, and that was one too many"), Buffett states a
  guideline applied "in investments as well as operations": **stay with simple propositions**. The
  arithmetic: one key variable with a 90% chance → 90% success; but **ten** independent variables
  each at 90% → only **35%**. "Since a chain is no stronger than its weakest link, it makes sense to
  look for… **mono-linked chains**." The complement to the same year's NICO/[[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|discipline]] essay — predictability beats brilliance.
