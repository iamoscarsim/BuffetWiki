# Decentralization

Berkshire's organizing principle: **centralize capital allocation at the very top,
delegate everything operational to the managers of each business**. Buffett states
it in the [[1979]] letter — "centralization of financial decisions at the top (the
very top) and rather extreme delegation of operating authority to a number of key
managers at the individual company or business unit level" — run from a corporate
headquarters of about 1,500 square feet that "could just field a basketball team."

He acknowledges the trade-off: extreme delegation "produces an occasional major
mistake that might have been eliminated... through closer operating controls," but
it "eliminates large layers of costs and dramatically speeds decision-making," and
above all lets Berkshire attract and keep exceptional managers "who find working
for Berkshire to be almost identical to running their own show." This pairs with
[[Owner Orientation and Candor]] and his preference for managers who already
"think like owners" ([[Ben Rosner]], [[Gene Abegg]]).

## Evolution
- [1979] Stated: central capital allocation, extreme operational delegation, tiny
  HQ; trade-off of occasional error for speed, low cost, and manager attraction.
- [1986] The managerial philosophy in full. Buffett and Munger have only **two jobs**;
  attracting/keeping outstanding managers "hasn't been all that difficult" because they
  come with the businesses and "our main contribution has been to not get in their way"
  (the golf-team analogy: you don't tell Nicklaus or Palmer how to swing). The David
  Ogilvy maxim — hire people bigger than you and become "a company of giants" — and the
  scalability point: with able, high-character managers "you can have a dozen or more
  reporting to you and still have time for an afternoon nap." Berkshire works only with
  people it likes and admires ("marrying for money is probably a bad idea under any
  circumstances, but absolute madness if you are already rich").
- [1991] **The Salomon stress-test.** Buffett's months-long absence as Interim Chairman of
  [[Salomon]] became unintended proof of the model: he could "materially reduce the time I was
  spending at the company and yet remain confident that its economic progress would not skip a
  beat," because the operating managers (the Blumkins, the Friedmans, [[Mike Goldberg]], the
  Heldmans, [[Chuck Huggins]], [[Stan Lipsey]], [[Ralph Schey]], [[Frank Rooney]]) "are all
  masters of their operations and need no help from me. My job is merely to treat them right
  and to allocate the capital they generate." The George Mira "left-handed touchdown pass"
  story — coach Gustafson's "Now *that's* what I call coaching" — captures Buffett's view of
  his own light-touch role. He also announces he will henceforth **rotate** detailed coverage
  of the operating businesses, one or two per letter, as their number grows.
- [1998] **The "commercial to managers of public companies" — the fullest statement of the
  Berkshire environment.** Berkshire makes even the best managers more effective by (1) eliminating
  "all of the ritualistic and nonproductive activities that normally go with the job of CEO" (board
  meetings, press interviews, banker presentations, analyst calls) and (2) giving each a simple
  mission: run it **"as if (1) you own 100% of it; (2) it is the only asset … your family … will
  ever have; and (3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century"** — and let **no decision be
  affected even slightly by accounting considerations** ("think about what counts, not how it will
  be counted"). This is possible only because Berkshire has "the **longest investment horizon to be
  found in the public-company universe**" (a majority of shares "held by investors who expect to die
  still holding them"), so its CEOs can "manage for maximum long-term value, rather than for next
  quarter's earnings." **[[GEICO]]/[[Tony Nicely]]** is the proof: "Charlie and I haven't taught
  Tony a thing — and never will — but we *have* created an environment that allows him to apply all
  of his talents to what's important." Most managers (~75% "independently wealthy") "wouldn't work
  for us if they got a lot of backseat driving" — "the Mark McGwires of the business world [who]
  need no advice from us as to how to hold the bat." (See [[Owner Orientation and Candor]].)
