# The Berkshire System

**Charlie Munger's name for the deliberate corporate design behind Berkshire**, codified in his
**"Golden Anniversary" essay** in the [[2014]] letter (paired with Buffett's own retrospective). It is
the most explicit single statement of *why Berkshire is built the way it is* — and Munger's argument
that it will outlast its founders.

**The elements** (Munger's list). A holding company that is **forever diversified**; a permanent home
for subsidiaries that **runs them with extreme [[Decentralization|decentralization]]** (no system-wide
HR, options, or budgets; "no committee… no legal office"); always includes **casualty insurers** for
their underwriting gains and [[Insurance Float|float]]; buys new subsidiaries **for cash, not stock**
(see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]); **pays no dividend** while $1 retained creates >$1 of value
(see [[Evolving Views — Dividends]]); maintains **near-perfect creditworthiness** and oceans of cash
(see [[Financial Strength]]); **almost never sells a subsidiary** ([[Permanent Ownership]]) and never
forces a CEO out for age; and seeks CEOs with "**trustworthiness, skill, energy, and love for the
business**." The chairman reserves only a handful of activities — managing investments, choosing and
paying subsidiary CEOs, deploying surplus cash, writing the annual letter, being the cultural
exemplar, and (notably) keeping "**much time for quiet reading and thinking.**"

**Why it worked** (Munger's four factors): the "constructive peculiarities" of Buffett, the
constructive peculiarities of the system, good luck, and the "weirdly intense, contagious devotion" of
shareholders. Buffett's choice to **limit himself to a few activities for 50 years** was "a
lollapalooza" — the **John Wooden effect** (concentrate the playing time on your best player, who then
improves with use), "out-Woodened" because the skill resided in one ever-improving person and was
mirrored in long-serving subsidiary CEOs, producing a **virtuous circle** that drew in better
businesses and managers. Insurance turned "a producer of mediocre results" into a marvel via [[Ajit Jain]]'s from-nothing reinsurance giant and [[GEICO]]'s quadrupled share.

**The post-Buffett prediction.** Munger argues Berkshire would "**almost surely remain a
better-than-normal company for a very long time**" even if Buffett left tomorrow, his successors were
ordinary, and it never bought another large business — because of entrenched competitive advantage,
the utility/railroad reinvestment runway, and bolt-ons. But the successors won't be ordinary:
**[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]]** are "**world-leading**… in some important ways, each is a better
business executive than Buffett." Buffett's companion essay reinforces this with the **conglomerate
defense** (rational, tax-free, frictionless capital allocation; buyer-of-choice for families) and the
**three rules of financial staying power** (diversified earnings, ≥$20B cash in Treasury bills, no
large near-term cash demands). Munger's broader claim: "**versions of the Berkshire system should be
tried more often elsewhere**," and bureaucracy "treated like the cancer[] it… resemble[s]."

## Evolution
- [2014] Munger's anniversary essay defines and names "the Berkshire System," explains why it worked
  (the Wooden effect; the virtuous circle; insurance as engine), and predicts durable success after
  Buffett — with [[Greg Abel]] and [[Ajit Jain]] "world-leading." Buffett's paired essay supplies the
  conglomerate defense and the three rules of financial staying power.
- [[2017]] **The prediction begins to be realized.** "I've saved the best for last": Berkshire's board
  elected **[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]]** — the two Munger had called "world-leading" in 2014 — as
  **directors and Vice Chairmen**, dividing the operating company between them (Jain over insurance, Abel
  over everything else) while Buffett and Munger keep "investments and capital allocation." The formal
  step that turns the succession *thesis* of the Berkshire System into an operating *reality* — "each has
  been with Berkshire for decades, and Berkshire's blood flows through their veins."
- [[2020]] **"Two Strings to Our Bow" — the conglomerate defense restated.** Buffett accepts the
  "conglomerate" label "only in part." Classic conglomerates failed because great businesses wouldn't
  sell, so dealmakers chased mediocre ones and overpaid — often using a **deliberately overvalued stock
  as acquisition currency** ("I'll pay you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats"),
  propped by "promotional techniques and 'imaginative' accounting" until "the party ends." Berkshire's
  version is the opposite: own good businesses **whether controlled or not**, bought for cash on
  durable competitive strength, management, and price — his "20-year struggle with the textile operation"
  having taught that "owning a non-controlling portion of a **wonderful** business is more profitable…
  than struggling with 100% of a marginal enterprise." A pillar of the System: capital flows seamlessly
  inside one entity ("you are awarded no points… for 'degree of difficulty'"). The same letter names the
  **"Big Four"** value centers (insurance, BNSF, Apple, BHE) and reveals Berkshire owns the most U.S.
  property/plant/equipment of any company ($154B).
