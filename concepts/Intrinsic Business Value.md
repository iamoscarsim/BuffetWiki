# Intrinsic Business Value

The single most important measure in Buffett's framework — and, he stresses, an
estimate rather than a precise figure. Defined in the [[1983]] letter: **intrinsic
business value is "an economic concept, estimating future cash output discounted to
present value,"** in contrast to **book value**, "an accounting concept, recording
the accumulated financial input." Pithily: "Book value tells you what has been put
in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out."

His analogy: two children given equally costly educations have equal book value of
schooling, but the *present value of the future payoff* "might vary enormously — from
zero to many times the cost." So too with businesses. At Berkshire's 1965 start,
book value of $19.46 *overstated* intrinsic value (the textile assets couldn't earn
their keep — "a largely-wasted education"); by 1983 intrinsic value far *exceeds*
book, owing to unrecorded [[Economic Goodwill]] and unrealized gains. Buffett uses
book value as a reported proxy because it is objective and easy to compute, while
repeatedly reminding readers that the two are "very different." Maximizing per-share
intrinsic value is Berkshire's stated economic goal.

## Evolution
- [1983] Formally defined and distinguished from book value (the education analogy).
  Berkshire's intrinsic value now far exceeds book; book used as a conservative proxy.
- [1986] Buffett quantifies the proxy: book value has for "more than a decade" served
  as "a reasonable if somewhat conservative proxy for business value" — business value
  has "moderately exceeded" book at a fairly steady ratio. In 1986 the gain in business
  value "probably exceeded the book value gain" (driven by unrecorded manager-built
  [[Economic Goodwill]]), though business value is "a soft number" on which "two equally
  well-informed observers might make judgments more than 10% apart." The [[Owner Earnings]]
  Appendix supplies the tool for estimating it.
- [1988] Business value "significantly exceeds" book and has grown faster over 24 years —
  though in 1988 "book value grew the faster, by a bit." The **NYSE listing** turns the
  concept into a stated stock-price *goal*: unusually, Berkshire does **not** want to
  maximize its share price but to have it "trade in a narrow range centered at intrinsic
  business value" — Buffett is "bothered as much by significant overvaluation as
  significant undervaluation," since either makes shareholders' results diverge from the
  business's. The **size** problem reframes the stakes: a 15% decade return now requires
  **$10.3B** of profit (vs. $3.9B four years earlier).
- [1989] The crisp **DCF definition**: intrinsic value is "all future cash flows of a
  business — in and out — discounted at prevailing interest rates," by which standard
  "all businesses, from manufacturers of buggy whips to operators of cellular phones,
  become economic equals." Berkshire's intrinsic value has compounded "somewhat" faster
  than its 23.8% book-value growth (book once *over*stated value when it was all textiles;
  now most businesses are worth far more than carrying value). The key forward warning —
  the **"double-dip → single-dip"**: past returns enjoyed both the portfolio companies'
  rising value *and* a market re-rating of their once-cheap stocks; with those stocks now
  fully priced, only the first dip remains. Carl Sagan's bacteria parable dramatizes the
  **size** anchor on future growth.
- [1992] The DCF definition is **attributed to its source** — **[[John Burr Williams]]'**
  *Theory of Investment Value* (1938): "the value of any stock, bond or business today is
  determined by the cash inflows and outflows — discounted at an appropriate interest rate —
  that can be expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset." Buffett stresses the
  formula "is the same for stocks as for bonds," differing only in that equity "coupons" must
  be *estimated* (and are swung by management quality). This grounds the year's [[Value and Growth]] synthesis — growth is just one variable in the value calculation — and reaffirms
  book value as a "conservative, but useful, proxy" for the 15%-intrinsic-value goal. The
  related accounting subtlety: a 1993 deferred-tax rule re-accrues all $7.6B of unrealized
  appreciation at 34%, but "what is important in the end is the tax rate at the time we sell."
- [1994] **The [[Scott Fetzer]] worked example** — the most concrete demonstration of book vs.
  intrinsic value in the letters. Bought 1986 for $315.2M against $172.6M book (a $142.6M
  premium = intrinsic value near *double* book). Over nine years its **earnings roughly
  doubled** ($40.3M → $79.3M) while its **book value fell** (cash paid out) — so ROE became
  "truly extraordinary" (would rank **#1 on the Fortune 500**). Meanwhile GAAP forced Berkshire
  to **amortize the purchase premium**, marking Scott Fetzer's carrying value *down* to
  **$148.2M** — "less than half" the acquisition figure — even as intrinsic value soared (the
  charges are "not, in our view, an economic cost"). The **college-education analogy** is
  refined: book value = the cost (including forgone earnings); intrinsic value = the discounted
  excess lifetime earnings — and "book value is meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value."
  The managerial corollary (see [[Capital Allocation]]): managers must raise *per-share*
  intrinsic value, never chase EPS-accretion — "Go to where the puck is going to be."
- [1996] **The two-column intrinsic-value table** (introduced 1995, now in its second year)
  becomes Buffett's standing tool for "anyone trying to estimate Berkshire's intrinsic value":
  it splits the company into **investments per share** (cash + securities) and **pre-tax
  operating earnings per share excluding all income from investments** — "what Berkshire would
  have reported had it been broken into two parts." In 1996 the two indices hit **$28,500**
  (+29.0%) and **$421.39** (+63.2%); since 1965 they have compounded 33.4% and 14.7%. Buffett
  also restates the **price-tracks-value** ideal: managed "as a family partnership," fairness
  "prevails when market price and intrinsic value are in sync," and over time "the aggregate
  gains made by Berkshire shareholders must of necessity **match the business gains of the
  company**." In 1996 the stock *underperformed* the (GEICO-lifted) business, making the
  price/value relationship "more appropriate" than a year earlier. Float is flagged as "a major
  component of … intrinsic value **not reflected in book value**" (see [[Insurance Float]]).
- [1997] The two-column table updates to investments **$38,043/share** (+33.5%) and operating
  earnings **$717.82/share** (+70.3%); since 1967 they have compounded **25.6%** and **24.2%**.
  Buffett rebuts the "de facto investment company" view by pointing to the second column —
  pre-tax operating earnings grown from $1M (first acquisition, 1967) to **$888M** — and stresses
  that **book and intrinsic value "tend to move roughly in tandem"** (in 1997 both grew ~34.1%,
  led by GEICO). The "**preening duck**" warning frames the gain: in a bull market that lifts the
  S&P (which bears no corporate tax) nearly as fast, a 34.1% book gain rates only a "**Quack**";
  Berkshire's true yardstick is "a **modest advantage over the Index** … over time," after
  overcoming its **$4.2B** annual tax drag. The size brake is dramatized anew: a 1951 idea worth
  $10,000 moved his year 100 points; a $500M profit now adds one — "we weren't smarter then, just
  smaller."
- [1998] **Book value can mislead *upward*, too.** A 48.3% book-value gain "was not as good as it
  looks" because **most of it came from issuing shares in acquisitions** (General Re, Executive Jet):
  since Berkshire trades at a large premium to book, any share issuance "instantly increases our
  per-share book-value figure, even though we've earned not a dime" — Berkshire receives more *book*
  than it gives, but "what we give and what we get [in **intrinsic value**] are roughly equal." So
  intrinsic value grew "very substantially" but "well short of the 48.3%." The two-column table (now
  including General Re pro forma) reaches investments **$47,647/share** (+25.2%) while operating
  earnings per share *fell* 33.9% to **$474.45** — both moved by General Re (huge investments swell
  column one; its 1998 underwriting loss dents column two). The 15% intrinsic-value goal is now "at
  the very peak of possible outcomes" (15% over five years = **+$58B** of net worth). See [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]] for the mirror-image trap when the premium runs the other way.
- [1999] In Buffett's **worst year** (book value +0.5%), intrinsic value still "**far exceeds**" the
  **$57.8B** book value — See's and the Buffalo News alone are "worth **fifteen to twenty times** the
  value at which they are carried." A standing structural wedge: the **~$500M/year goodwill
  amortization** charge mechanically shrinks book value while economic goodwill usually *grows*, so
  "even if economic goodwill were to remain constant, the annual amortization charge would
  persistently widen the gap between intrinsic value and book value." The two-column table's second
  column went *negative* (−$458.55/share operating loss) on General Re's underwriting hit — a book
  reflection of a real but "aberrational" event, not of intrinsic-value decline. (See [[Economic Goodwill]] for the goodwill-accounting argument.)
- [2000] **The fable form: [[Bird in the Bush]].** Buffett distills valuation to Aesop's "a bird in
  the hand is worth two in the bush," requiring three inputs — *certainty* of the cash (birds),
  their *amount and timing*, and the *risk-free rate*. "The formula for valuing all assets … has
  been unchanged since … about 600 B.C. … neither the advent of the steam engine … nor the creation
  of the automobile changed [it] one iota — nor will the Internet." Two refinements land here: using
  "**a range of possibilities**" beats "pseudo-precise" point estimates (occasionally revealing the
  **Inefficient Bush Theory** — a price "startlingly low in relation to value"), and **growth is
  merely a component of the value equation**, not a rival "style" (see [[Value and Growth]]). He also
  restates that he'd **rather buy whole businesses than stocks** — partly for the **tax** edge of
  80%+ ownership (the government is a "partner twice" with a stock stake, "only once" with control).
  Housekeeping: FASB's resolution of the **pooling** fight ends Berkshire's intangible-amortization
  charge, so "reported earnings will more closely reflect economic reality" — though "none of this
  will have an effect on Berkshire's **intrinsic value**."
- [2010] **The "three pillars" framework — and the GEICO case study.** For Berkshire's many new BNSF
  shareholders, Buffett formalizes how he and Munger estimate intrinsic value from **two measurable
  pillars plus one subjective one**: (1) **investments** per share (stocks/bonds/cash — $158B, or
  $94,730/share); (2) **non-insurance pre-tax earnings** per share ($5,926); and (3) the subjective
  **"what-will-they-do-with-the-money"** factor — the efficacy of *future* retained-earnings
  reinvestment, which can turn dollars "into fifty-cent pieces" or "two-dollar bills" (Sears/Montgomery
  Ward CEOs vs. **Sam Walton**). Book value remains an understated proxy. The **[[GEICO]]** story is the
  worked example: economic goodwill of ~**$14B** sits on the books at $1.4B (see [[Economic Goodwill]]).
  "We would rather be **approximately right than precisely wrong**."
- [2015] **The two-pillar measure is refined to include underwriting income.** For the first time,
  Buffett folds **insurance underwriting income into the second pillar** (per-share earnings from
  non-investment businesses, now **$12,304**, of which underwriting contributed **$1,118**; 10-year
  average $1,434). The original framework had *excluded* underwriting because catastrophe-heavy results
  were too volatile ("if the wind didn't blow and the earth didn't shake, we made large profits"); now
  that Berkshire has **de-emphasized catastrophe coverage** for stable bread-and-butter lines, the
  income is dependable enough to count. The first pillar (per-share cash + investments) reached
  **$159,794** (Kraft Heinz at market). Since 1970: investments +18.9%/yr, earnings +23.7%/yr — the
  stock tracking both. The asymmetric accounting (winners never written up, losers written down) means
  intrinsic value "**far exceeds**" book — the basis for the 120%-of-book buyback line.
- [[2018]] **Book value retired as the headline metric — and replaced by "[[The Five Groves|five groves]]."** After ~three decades leading the letter with per-share book-value change, Buffett
  **abandons it** as a metric that "has lost the relevance it once had," for three reasons: Berkshire has
  **morphed from marketable stocks into operating businesses** (carried far below worth); the new
  mark-to-market rule whipsaws the figure; and Berkshire's **buybacks above book but below intrinsic
  value** now *raise* per-share intrinsic value while *lowering* per-share book value — so the book
  scorecard grows "increasingly out of touch with economic reality." Going forward he will point to
  **market price** as "the best measure of business performance" over time. In place of book value he
  offers the **[[The Five Groves|five-grove]]** appraisal: sum the four asset-laden groves (controlled
  businesses, equities, jointly-controlled companies, cash/Treasuries), subtract deferred tax on
  unrealized securities gains, and credit the fifth grove (float) as cost-free funding — refusing to net
  out hypothetical taxes on selling wonderful businesses ("it would be foolish… to sell any"). The thesis:
  "**the whole is greater — considerably greater — than the sum of the parts.**"
