# Capital Allocation

How a company deploys the cash it generates — reinvest, acquire, pay dividends, or
repurchase shares — is, in Buffett's view, the most important job of management and
the lens through which he judges CEOs (and himself).

The governing test appears in the [[1978]] letter: retain earnings only if they can
be "utilized internally at attractive rates"; otherwise "earnings should be paid
out or used to repurchase shares — often by far the most attractive option for
capital utilization." This is the early form of the **one-dollar test** Buffett
later states explicitly: every retained dollar should create at least a dollar of
market value. The same logic drives his enthusiasm for investees that reinvest well
([[Look-Through Earnings]]) and his disdain for managers "plowing capital into
projects of low profitability."

This page tracks the evolution of Buffett's capital-allocation thinking — the
one-dollar test, his shifting stance on [[Evolving Views — Dividends]] and
[[Evolving Views — Buybacks]], and acquisition policy.

## Evolution
- [1978] Test stated: retain only if reinvestment earns attractive returns; else
  distribute or buy back stock. Buybacks named "often the most attractive option."
- [1979] Dollar test made explicit for investees (see [[Look-Through Earnings]]):
  a retained dollar should become "a dollar or more of subsequent market value."
  Berkshire run on [[Decentralization]] — capital allocation centralized "at the
  very top," operations delegated.
- [1980] Strong endorsement of investee **buybacks below intrinsic value** (see
  [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]). Acquisition preference stated: businesses that
  **generate** cash, not consume it — wary of operations that must reinvest all
  internal funds just to maintain volume ("a certain mirage-like quality"). Borrows
  ahead of need ([[Financial Strength]]) to keep "financial firepower."
- [1981] The **retention logic** stated via the parable of the talents (Matthew
  25:14–30): high-return businesses should retain earnings; low-return businesses
  should pay them out so owners can redeploy ("good money is not thrown after bad").
  But [[Inflation]] perversely forces even "bad" businesses to retain. Berkshire
  retains "for offensive, not defensive or obligatory, reasons." Warns against
  disguised dividend weakness: dividend-reinvestment plans and "selling newly issued
  shares to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul." See also [[Premium Takeovers]].
- [1983] The **$1 retention test** formalized: retention is justified only if, over a
  **five-year rolling** period, each $1 retained creates at least $1 of market value.
  Also: when acquisition costs are similar, Berkshire prefers "$2 of earnings that is
  not reportable... than $1 of earnings that is reportable" (see [[Look-Through Earnings]]).
  Issue stock — in any form — only for equal [[Intrinsic Business Value]].
- [1986] Capital allocation framed as one of Buffett and Munger's only **two jobs**
  (the other: attracting and keeping managers). It matters far more at Berkshire than
  at most firms because "we earn more money than average; we retain all that we earn;
  and... our operations require little incremental capital." A business earning 23% and
  retaining it all is shaped overwhelmingly by today's allocation choices, unlike one
  earning 10% and paying out half. But 1986 was "tough work": no marketable equities
  met the tests, so the main moves were paying off debt and stockpiling cash — "neither
  is a fate worse than death, but they do not inspire us to do handsprings either."
- [1987] The essay on **why most CEOs are poor capital allocators**. A CEO who retains
  10% of net worth annually will, after a decade, have deployed **>60% of all the
  capital** in the business — yet most rose through marketing, production, engineering
  or politics and "may have never tackled" allocation: "it's as if the final step for a
  highly-talented musician was not to perform at Carnegie Hall but, instead, to be named
  Chairman of the Federal Reserve." Turning to staff, consultants or bankers tends to
  "accentuate the problem." This is the **first advantage of control** over a marketable
  holding (you allocate the capital); the second is the tax efficiency of an 80%+ stake.
  Berkshire's non-controlled investees are the lucky exception — capital "generally…
  well-deployed and, in some cases, brilliantly so."
- [1989] A new tool enters the kit: **convertible preferreds** ([[Gillette]], [[USAir]],
  [[Champion International]], joining [[Salomon]]) — a way to deploy large sums in
  businesses Buffett can't fully forecast, yielding "moderately above… most fixed-income
  portfolios" with equity upside. He ranks the options explicitly: these preferreds
  trail both "a business with wonderful economic prospects… unappreciated by the market"
  and his **favorite form of deployment, "the acquisition of 80% or more of a fine
  business with a fine management"** — but those "are rare… in a size befitting our…
  resources." The **deferred-tax math** (see [[Permanent Ownership]]) reinforces the bias
  toward holding over churning.
- [1994] **The acquisition-folly essay.** Allocation decisions "must… increase per-share
  intrinsic value and avoid moves that decrease it" — yet managers obsess over whether a deal
  is **EPS-accretive**, which Buffett calls "equally silly": a non-earning MBA student could
  boost his "earnings" by merging with a day laborer. Berkshire instead follows **Wayne
  Gretzky**: "Go to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is." Most big acquisitions
  enrich the acquiree's holders, the acquirer's management, and the bankers while *reducing*
  the acquirer's wealth — John Medlin's "**running a chain letter in reverse**." The cause is
  partly biological: CEOs with "an abundance of animal spirits and ego," egged on by advisors
  ("like asking your interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug"), need no push to deal
  — one insurer CEO's candid rationale: "**Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one.**" The
  positive model is the **[[Scott Fetzer]]** example (see [[Intrinsic Business Value]]): a fine
  business throws off far more cash than it can use, so a good allocator routes it to per-share
  value, not empire-building. The **money's-not-free** discipline links allocation to pay (see
  [[Executive Compensation]]).
- [1999] **Buffett grades himself a "D."** Opening his worst year, he frames the whole job in one
  line: "**My 'one subject' is capital allocation, and my grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D**" —
  the failure being the **equity portfolio's** poor showing (his responsibility, "leaving aside the
  small piece … run by [[Lou Simpson]]"). The constructive counterpoints: a strong preference for
  **cash acquisitions** (Jordan's and MidAmerican both bought for cash — see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]), and the **share-repurchase test** finally applied to Berkshire's own stock (only
  buy below conservative intrinsic value — see [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]). On where *not* to
  allocate, he reaffirms the **[[Circle of Competence]]**: Berkshire owns no tech stocks because it
  can't identify which firms have "a truly durable competitive advantage," and "there are a great
  many business areas in which Charlie and I have no special capital-allocation expertise."
