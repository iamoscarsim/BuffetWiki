# Institutional Imperative

Buffett's name, coined in the [[1989]] "Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years" essay,
for "an unseen force" he calls "**my most surprising discovery**" in business: the
tendency of organizations, regardless of the intelligence or good intentions of their
managers, to behave irrationally. "In business school, I was given no hint of the
imperative's existence… I thought then that decent, intelligent, and experienced
managers would automatically make rational business decisions. But I learned over time
that isn't so. Instead, rationality frequently wilts when the institutional imperative
comes into play."

He lists four manifestations:

1. **Resistance to change** — "As if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion, an
   institution will resist any change in its current direction."
2. **Make-work for idle capital** — "Just as work expands to fill available time,
   corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up available funds."
3. **Rationalized leadership cravings** — "Any business craving of the leader, however
   foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies
   prepared by his troops."
4. **Mindless peer imitation** — "The behavior of peer companies, whether they are
   expanding, acquiring, setting executive compensation or whatever, will be mindlessly
   imitated."

"Institutional dynamics, not venality or stupidity, set businesses on these courses,
which are too often misguided." Buffett says he made "expensive mistakes" by ignoring
its power and has since organized Berkshire to **minimize** the imperative's influence
(extreme [[Decentralization]], no budgets or peer-driven targets, owner-aligned managers)
and to invest in companies "alert to the problem." The concept underlies much of his
critique of [[Capital Allocation]], [[Premium Takeovers]], and [[Executive Compensation]].

## Evolution
- [1989] Coined and enumerated (the four manifestations) in the 25-year mistakes essay;
  framed as the force Berkshire's structure and investing are designed to resist.
- [1990] Applied to **banking**, explaining the era's wave of bank failures: most bank
  troubles trace to manifestation #4 — "the tendency of executives to mindlessly imitate
  the behavior of their peers." "In their lending, many bankers played follow-the-leader
  with lemming-like zeal; now they are experiencing a lemming-like fate." It's why
  Berkshire bought the *well-managed* [[Wells Fargo]] (managers immune to the imperative)
  rather than a "cheap" troubled bank.
