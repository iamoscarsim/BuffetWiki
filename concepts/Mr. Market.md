# Mr. Market

Benjamin Graham's allegory of the manic-depressive business partner who shows up
every day offering to buy your interest or sell you his at wildly varying prices —
and whose moods you should exploit, not follow. The investor's job is to treat
market quotations as opportunities (buy when Mr. Market is despondent, ignore him
when euphoric), never as a verdict on value. It is the emotional discipline that
makes [[Stocks as Fractional Ownership]] workable.

Precursors run through the early letters before the allegory is named: the [[1977]]
welcome of lower prices on sound businesses; the [[1982]] description of the auction
market set by "an army of manic-depressive lemmings," and the warning that "the
market does not forgive those who know not what they do" and that net worth "could
shrink significantly" in down years without distress — "we simply will add to our
holdings at even more favorable prices."

The **definitive telling** comes in the [[1987]] letter. Mr. Market is "a remarkably
accommodating fellow… your partner in a private business" who "appears daily and names
a price at which he will either buy your interest or sell you his." He has "incurable
emotional problems": euphoric one day (a high price, lest you snap up his interest),
despondent the next (a low price, terrified you'll dump yours on him). His saving
graces: "he doesn't mind being ignored," and "transactions are strictly at your
option" — so "the more manic-depressive his behavior, the better for you." The warning,
Cinderella-style: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his
pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful." And: "if you aren't certain
that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't
belong in the game… As they say in poker, 'If you've been in the game 30 minutes and
you don't know who the patsy is, you're the patsy.'" Buffett pairs it with Graham's
**"In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing
machine,"** and applies it to the October 1987 crash and **portfolio insurance**.

## Evolution
- [1982] Precursor imagery: "manic-depressive lemmings" set auction prices; market
  volatility is opportunity, not threat. (Full Graham allegory comes in 1987.)
- [1987] **The full allegory**, attributed to Ben Graham: the manic-depressive partner
  whose quotes you exploit, not follow; "his pocketbook, not his wisdom"; the patsy
  line; "voting machine… weighing machine." Applied to the 1987 crash and **portfolio
  insurance** ($60–90B on a "hair trigger" selling more as prices fall) — volatility
  driven by speculators "will offer the true investor more chances," hurting him "only
  if he is forced… to sell at untoward times." Delayed recognition can even be "an
  advantage: it may give us the chance to buy more of a good thing at a bargain price."
- [1990] The **net-buyer** corollary, via [[Wells Fargo]]'s panic-driven ~50% fall.
  Investors "who expect to be ongoing buyers of investments throughout their lifetimes"
  should welcome falling prices, just as they welcome falling **food** prices — "it's the
  seller of food who doesn't like declining prices." Since Berkshire "will be buying
  businesses… year in, year out… declining prices benefit us, and rising prices hurt us."
  "The most common cause of low prices is **pessimism**… we want to do business in such an
  environment… because we like the prices it produces. It's **optimism that is the enemy
  of the rational buyer**." But not reflexively contrarian: "a contrarian approach is just
  as foolish as a follow-the-crowd strategy. What's required is thinking rather than
  polling" (Bertrand Russell: "Most men would rather die than think. Many do.").
- [1993] **Volatility as the investor's friend, against beta.** In the risk essay (see
  [[Risk and Volatility]]) Buffett re-invokes Graham's Chapter 8 Mr. Market: "the more
  manic-depressive this chap is, the greater the opportunities," because "a wildly fluctuating
  market means that irrationally low prices will periodically be attached to solid businesses."
  This is his rebuttal to equating risk with price volatility — "after we buy a stock… we would
  not be disturbed if markets closed for a year or two." He also reprises the **voting/weighing
  machine** with [[Coca-Cola]]'s history: a 1919 IPO at $40 was beaten below $20 by 1920, yet
  one share with dividends reinvested was worth $2.1M by 1993.
- [1997] **"How We Think About Market Fluctuations" — the net-saver argument in full.** A short
  quiz: a lifelong hamburger-eater (not a cattle producer) should want *lower* beef prices; a
  periodic car-buyer should want *lower* car prices — "these questions … answer themselves." So
  too: "**If you expect to be a net saver during the next five years, should you hope for a higher
  or lower stock market?** … Prospective purchasers should much prefer sinking prices." Berkshire
  owners "are automatically saving" (all earnings retained), so "they should … rejoice when markets
  decline"; and investees that **repurchase shares** ([[Coca-Cola]], [[Washington Post]], [[Wells Fargo]]) help more at low prices. "Smile when you read a headline that says 'Investors lose as
  market falls.' Edit it … to '**Dis**investors lose as market falls — but investors gain'" ("Every
  putt makes *someone* happy"). The 1970s–80s low prices, "hostile to investment transients [but]
  friendly to those taking up permanent residence," are the proof. (Pairs with the eroded **[[Margin of Safety]]** Buffett flags amid 1997's "cheery consensus.")
- [2013] **The "moody farm-neighbor" retelling — via the "two small investments" essay.** Buffett
  recasts Graham's Mr. Market through his own 1986 Nebraska **farm** and 1993 **NYU retail property**:
  if "a moody fellow with a farm bordering my property yelled out a price every day" varying "wildly…
  depending on his mental state," the owner could "only be benefited by his erratic behavior" — buy
  when the shout-out is absurdly low, ignore (or sell) when absurdly high. The investor's edge over the
  farm-owner is precisely those minute-to-minute quotes — yet "owners of stocks too often let the
  capricious… behavior of their fellow owners cause them to behave irrationally as well," turning
  liquidity "from the unqualified benefit it should be to a curse." The lesson pairs with [[Circle of Competence]] (judge an asset by its future productivity, not its quote): "**games are won by players
  who focus on the playing field — not by those whose eyes are glued to the scoreboard**"; "a climate
  of fear is your friend; a euphoric world is your enemy."
