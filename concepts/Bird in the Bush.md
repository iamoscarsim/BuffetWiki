# Bird in the Bush

Buffett's most compact statement of how *all* assets are valued, set out in the **[[2000]]**
letter. The formula for valuing anything bought for financial gain "has been unchanged
since it was first laid out by a very smart man in about 600 B.C." — **Aesop**, whose
"enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was '**a bird in the hand is
worth two in the bush**.'" To complete it, you must answer three questions:

1. **How *certain* are you that there are birds in the bush?** (How sure are the future cash
   flows?)
2. ***When* will they emerge and *how many* will there be?** (Timing and amount.)
3. **What is the *risk-free interest rate*?** (The yield on long-term U.S. bonds.)

Answer those and "you will know the maximum value of the bush — and the maximum number of
the birds you now possess [dollars] that should be offered for it." This is simply the
discounted-cash-flow definition of [[Intrinsic Business Value]] (the [[John Burr Williams]]
formula) rendered as a fable. Its power is its universality: it "applies to outlays for
farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants … neither
the advent of the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity nor the creation of the
automobile changed the formula one iota — **nor will the Internet**."

Three corollaries Buffett draws:

- **Growth is just a component of value, not a rival to it.** Common yardsticks (dividend
  yield, P/E, P/B, growth rates) matter only as "clues to the amount and timing of cash
  flows." "Growth can **destroy** value if it requires cash inputs … that exceed the
  discounted value of the cash those assets will generate." Hence "market commentators …
  who glibly refer to '**growth' and 'value' styles** as contrasting approaches … are
  displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication" (see [[Value and Growth]]).
- **The Inefficient Bush Theory (IBT).** Usually the range of possible bird-counts is "so
  wide that no useful conclusion can be reached" — but occasionally "even very conservative
  estimates … reveal that the price quoted is startlingly low in relation to value." The
  investor "does not need brilliance nor blinding insights," only business sense and
  independent thinking (and the discipline to stay inside the [[Circle of Competence]]).
- **Investment vs. speculation.** When even the most brilliant investor "can't muster a
  conviction about the birds to emerge" — common with "new businesses and rapidly changing
  industries" — "any capital commitment must be labeled **speculative**." Speculation
  ("the focus is … on what the next fellow will pay") is "neither illegal, immoral nor
  un-American. But it is not a game in which Charlie and I wish to play. We bring nothing
  to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?"

**The dot-com application.** The 2000 letter deploys the fable against the bubble: "Nothing
sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money," so speculators behave like
Cinderella, planning to leave "just seconds before midnight" in "a room in which the clocks
have no hands." A December-1999 survey's 19% expected returns were "irrational"; valuations
had "**decoupled from the values of the businesses that underlay them**"; "**value is
destroyed, not created, by any business that loses money over its lifetime**," and the
bubble enabled a massive **wealth transfer** via "the old-fashioned chain letter." "**A pin
lies in wait for every bubble.**" Buffett's grandsons' update of Aesop: "A girl in a
convertible is worth five in the phonebook."

## Evolution
- [2000] Aesop's "bird in the bush" presented as the timeless, internet-proof valuation
  formula (certainty × amount/timing × risk-free rate); growth recast as a component of
  value (not a style); the IBT; the investment/speculation line; and the dot-com-bubble
  warning ("clocks have no hands"; "a pin lies in wait for every bubble").
