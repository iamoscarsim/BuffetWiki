# Derivatives

Buffett's view of derivatives — instruments calling for money to change hands at a future date
based on reference items (rates, prices, indices) — is set out in the famous [[2002]] essay, where
he and Munger call them "**financial weapons of mass destruction**, carrying dangers that, while
now latent, are potentially lethal," and "**time bombs**, both for the parties that deal in them
and the economic system."

The essay grew out of a concrete problem: **[[General Re Securities]]**, a derivatives dealer that
came with the 1998 [[General Re]] purchase. Buffett "didn't want" it, "failed in [the] attempts to
sell" it, and was **winding it down** — discovering how hard exit is ("Like Hell, both [reinsurance
and derivatives] are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit").

**The dangers Buffett names.** (1) **Mark-to-model → "mark-to-myth"**: with no real market, distant
settlement, and multiple reference items, counterparties book paper profits on "fanciful
assumptions"; marking errors are "**not symmetrical**," favoring the bonus-seeking trader or the
option-rich CEO. (2) **Collateral-call spirals**: a credit downgrade can force sudden collateral
posting, draining liquidity and triggering further downgrades — "a corporate meltdown." (3)
**Daisy-chain/linkage risk**: credit risk concentrates in a few big dealers trading with each
other, so "the troubles of one could quickly infect the others" (the analogy to pre-Fed bank
runs). The **LTCM** 1998 near-collapse — a firm of a few hundred people whose total-return swaps
threatened market stability — is the cautionary case. Berkshire's posture is to be a "**fortress
of financial strength**" ([[Financial Strength]]) and to **minimize links of any kind** (the same
reason it retains, rather than retrocedes, reinsurance — see [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]).

Buffett is careful to note the instruments have legitimate micro uses ("at Berkshire, I sometimes
engage in large-scale derivatives transactions") — a caveat that matters because Berkshire would
itself later write **large equity-index put and credit-default contracts** (collateral-light, no
counterparty-margin triggers), defended in the [[2008]]–[[2009]] letters even as he reaffirmed the
systemic warning.

## Evolution
- [2002] The landmark essay: "financial weapons of mass destruction"; mark-to-myth; collateral-call
  spirals; daisy-chain linkage; LTCM; the [[General Re Securities]] wind-down. Foreshadows the 2008
  crisis with uncanny accuracy.
- [2003] The [[General Re Securities]] runoff continues at a cost — cumulative losses on the
  wind-down mount — vindicating the decision to exit but underscoring how slowly derivatives books
  unwind.
- [2005] **"A canary in this business coal mine."** A further **−$104M** ([[General Re Securities]]
  cumulative −$404M; down to 741 contracts) prompts Buffett's most pointed systemic warning yet:
  derivatives outstanding worldwide are now "**a multiple of what existed in 1998**" (the LTCM year),
  and Berkshire — a minor player exiting in a *benign* market with conventional accounting — still
  couldn't get out cleanly. "The time to have considered… the reliability of New Orleans' levees was
  before Katrina." Mark Twain's cat-by-the-tail: "he would opt for cats."
- [2006] **The Gen Re Securities exit ends — and Berkshire writes its own.** The wind-down concludes
  (cumulative −$409M; "**Good Until Reached For**"; "All's well that ends"). In the same letter
  Buffett discloses, seemingly paradoxically, that *he* **personally writes derivatives** — **62
  contracts**, "free of counterparty credit risk," entered because "derivatives… are sometimes
  wildly mispriced," and so far producing "**hundreds of millions** of dollars" of pre-tax profit.
  The distinction: Berkshire's contracts carry no collateral/margin triggers and are held to
  maturity — the first hint of the equity-index puts and CDS contracts detailed in 2008.
- [2007] **The book grows to 94 contracts, in two families.** (1) **54 credit-default (CDS)
  contracts** on high-yield bond indices (premiums **$3.2B** received; $472M of losses paid; worst
  case +$4.7B; liability $1.8B; expiring 2009–2013). (2) **Equity-index put options** on four indices
  (S&P 500 + three foreign), original terms **15–20 years**, **European-style** (exercisable only at
  expiry, 2019–2027), struck at-the-market: premiums **$4.5B** received, liability $4.6B. Both
  **"hold the money" (no counterparty risk)** but must be **marked to market quarterly**, so reported
  earnings will swing "**$1 billion or more in a quarter**" — accepted as "the same philosophy" as
  super-cat: trade short-run earnings volatility for long-run gains.
- [2008] **Berkshire's own derivatives, defended.** With the financial system in crisis, Buffett
  details Berkshire's two big derivative bets — **equity-index puts** (on the S&P 500 and three
  foreign indices, European-style, settling 2019–2028) and **credit-default obligations** — taken
  in for **billions of up-front premium** Berkshire holds and invests, and structured to **avoid
  the counterparty-collateral triggers** he warned of. He restates the systemic critique and adds a
  jab at **Black-Scholes** for overvaluing very-long-dated options. (See [[Financial Strength]].)
- [2009] The derivative contracts are marked to market (large swings), but Buffett reiterates that
  what matters is the cash held and ultimate payout, not the mark — and that the **2002 warning
  stands**: the crisis was, in part, the toxicity he predicted made "clear."
