# The Great, the Good and the Gruesome

Buffett's [[2007]] framework classifying businesses by the relationship between **growth, capital
intensity, and returns** — the maturation of [[Owner Earnings]], [[Economic Moat]] and [[Return on Equity]] into a single lens for what makes a business worth owning.

**The Great.** A business with an **enduring moat** that earns **high returns on little capital** —
so its earnings can be largely extracted and redeployed elsewhere. The prototype is **[[See's Candies]]**: bought for $25M when it earned <$5M pre-tax on $8M of capital (~60% pre-tax returns), it
produced **$1.35 billion** of cumulative pre-tax profit on only **$32M** of added capital. "**It's
far better to have an ever-increasing stream of earnings with virtually no major capital
requirements** (ask Microsoft or Google)." Truly great businesses "can't for any extended period
reinvest a large portion of their earnings internally at high rates" — so the cash flows out to buy
more businesses ("**there's no rule that you have to invest money where you've earned it**").

**The Good.** A business with a real competitive advantage that nonetheless must **"put up more to
earn more."** **[[FlightSafety International]]** is the example: a $509M incremental investment lifted
pre-tax earnings $111M → $270M — "an excellent but not extraordinary business." Berkshire's
**regulated utilities** fall here too: "we will earn considerably more… ten years from now, but we
will invest many billions to make it." A satisfactory home for capital, but not a compounding
machine.

**The Gruesome.** A business that "**grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the
growth, and then earns little or no money**." **Airlines** are the archetype — "a durable
competitive advantage has proven elusive ever since… Kitty Hawk… a farsighted capitalist… would have
done his successors a huge favor by **shooting Orville down**." Buffett's own [[USAir]] purchase is
the confessed scar.

The summary metaphor — three **"savings accounts"**: the **great** one pays a high and *rising* rate;
the **good** one pays a fair rate on added deposits; the **gruesome** one pays an inadequate rate
*and* demands you keep adding money.
