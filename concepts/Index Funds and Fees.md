# Index Funds and Fees

Buffett's long-running argument that, for the overwhelming majority of investors, a **low-cost S&P 500
index fund** beats the parade of high-fee "helpers" — because in aggregate active investors must earn the
market's gross return and then lose by the size of their costs. It is the public, retail-facing corollary
of his private discipline ([[Circle of Competence]], [[Margin of Safety]]): if you cannot identify the
rare manager worth the fee — and Buffett says he has spotted "only ten or so" in his lifetime — own the
index and **sit still**.

**The arithmetic.** Split the investing universe into Group A (active) and Group B (do-nothing). B, by
definition, earns the average before costs; therefore A must too. "**Whichever group has the lower costs
will win.**" Stacked fees — a hedge fund's "2 and 20" plus a fund-of-funds' extra layer — turn a small
edge into a large, compounding drag. Hence Bill Ruane's progression, quoted in [[2016]]: "from the
**innovators to the imitators to the swarming incompetents**."

**"The Bet" (2008–2017).** In Berkshire's 2005 report Buffett argued passive would
beat active net of fees; he then publicly wagered **$500,000** that an S&P index fund would beat any five
funds-of-funds an investment pro could pick over ten years. Only **[[Ted Seides]]** of Protégé Partners
took the other side. Reported in the [[2016]] letter at the nine-year mark, the **Vanguard index fund had
gained 85.4% (7.1% compounded) vs. the five funds-of-funds' average of just 2.2%** — with roughly **60% of
the funds-of-funds' gains consumed by two layers of fees** ("**Fees never sleep**," as Gordon Gekko might
say). Buffett estimated the "financial elites" wasted **>$100 billion** over the decade chasing
hyper-helpers, and named **[[Jack Bogle]]** — the index-fund pioneer — the "hands-down choice" for a statue
honoring American investors. The bet's charity proceeds went to Girls Inc. of Omaha.

The lesson reconciles with the rest of Buffett's creed via the **know-nothing / know-something**
distinction ([[Concentration]]): the investor who cannot value businesses should index; only the rare
investor who genuinely can should concentrate.

## Evolution
- [2005] Buffett lays out the active-vs-passive, fees-drag argument in the annual report and issues the
  public challenge.
- [[2016]] **"The Bet"** reported at nine years: index +85.4% vs. funds-of-funds avg +2.2%; the
  fees/"hyper-helper" critique and the **[[Jack Bogle]]** tribute. ~$100B of "elite" waste estimated.
- [[2017]] **"The Bet" concludes — and yields a bonus lesson on bonds.** Final tally: the S&P index fund
  gained **125.8% (8.5%/yr)** vs. the five funds-of-funds' final gains of 21.7%, 42.3%, 87.7%, 2.8%, and
  27.0% (averages 0.3%–6.5%/yr) — "in every one of the nine years that followed [2008], the funds-of-funds
  as a whole trailed the index fund." **Girls Inc. of Omaha received $2,222,279** (not the planned $1M),
  because in **November 2012** Buffett and **[[Ted Seides|Protégé]]** had switched the staked zero-coupon
  Treasuries (then yielding a "pathetic" **0.88%**, priced at "more than 100 times earnings") into **11,200
  Berkshire B shares** — "a really dumb investment compared to American equities." Lessons: "**Performance
  comes, performance goes. Fees never falter**"; for long horizons, supposedly "risk-free" long-term bonds
  can be **riskier than stocks** (see [[Risk and Volatility]]); and "**stick with big, 'easy' decisions and
  eschew activity**" — Buffett and Protégé made "only one investment decision" in ten years vs. the hedge
  funds' "tens of thousands."
