# Financial Strength

Buffett's insistence on a fortress balance sheet — abundant liquidity, moderate and
properly structured debt, and capital strength well beyond regulatory or
operational need — even at the cost of a somewhat lower [[Return on Equity]]. The
purpose is survival and *optionality*: the ability to act decisively when others
can't.

The [[1980]] letter states it directly. Discussing the bond-loss crisis facing
much of the insurance industry, Buffett notes Berkshire likely has "the strongest
[net worth] relative to premium volume among all large property-casualty
stockholder-owned groups." This strength "doesn't remove us from the hostile pricing
environment... it just gives us more staying power and more options." In the
financing section he explains why Berkshire sold $60M of long-term notes despite no
immediate need: "we want to have plenty of financial firepower" for opportunities
that "may present themselves at a time when credit is extremely expensive — or even
unavailable." He closes: "we plan to operate with plenty of liquidity, with debt
that is moderate in size and properly structured, and with an abundance of capital
strength. Our return on equity is penalized somewhat by this conservative approach,
but it is the only one with which we feel comfortable." This principle later
underpins Berkshire's role as lender/insurer of last resort in crises (1998, 2008).

## Evolution
- [1980] Stated as policy: liquidity, moderate debt, surplus capital; "financial
  firepower" to act when credit is scarce, accepting a modest ROE penalty.
- [1984–85] The payoff materializes: in the hard insurance market, Berkshire's
  unmatched capital let it triple premium volume, write large net lines others
  couldn't, and win a "flight to quality" — turning conservatism into a decisive
  competitive weapon (see [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]]).
- [1987] The **debt creed**, stated in the Financing section: adhere to policies that
  "achieve acceptable long-term results under extraordinarily adverse conditions, rather
  than optimal results under a normal range of conditions." Not debt-phobic ("far from
  believing there is no fate worse than debt"), but borrow only what poses "no threat…
  on a worst-case basis," refusing to "risk what is important… for some extra returns
  that are relatively unimportant." And **finance ahead of need**: tight money (costly
  liabilities) coincides with the best acquisition opportunities, so act on the two
  sides of the balance sheet independently — "if you want to shoot rare, fast-moving
  elephants, you should always carry a loaded gun." The $250M debenture issue's negative
  carry (~$160K/week) is dismissed as the price of readiness. Financial strength is also
  what enables Berkshire's defaulted-bond and [[Arbitrage|arbitrage]] commitments.
- [1988] S&P raised Berkshire to **AAA** — its highest rating, held by only ~16 U.S.
  industrials/insurers (down from 28 in 1980). Buffett contrasts Berkshire with the
  bondholder "**event risk**" pervading a takeover-era world of leveraged
  recapitalizations: "Charlie and I promise bondholders the same respect we afford
  shareholders." The fortress also underwrites Berkshire's willingness to write huge
  insurance lines and run a float-to-premium ratio ~3× the industry's.
- [1989] The fortress converts directly into underwriting capacity: Berkshire's insurance
  net worth (~**$6 billion**, "the second highest amount in the United States") lets it
  write and **retain** up to $250M of catastrophe coverage — risking a ~$165M after-tax
  single-quarter loss that "would be a blow only to our pride, not to our well-being"
  (see [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]]). The same year, Berkshire issued $902.6M of
  **[[Zero-Coupon Bonds|zero-coupon convertible debentures]]** (~$400M cash proceeds) — a
  cash-flow-positive financing — while Buffett's 25-year review defends "consistently-
  conservative financial policies" as *not* a mistake: "a small chance of distress or
  disgrace cannot… be offset by a large chance of extra returns" (he wouldn't take even
  99:1 leverage odds).
- [1990] Financial strength becomes the explicit *competitive product* in
  **[[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]**: buyers "seek financially strong sellers… and here
  we have a major competitive advantage — in the industry, our strength is unmatched."
  The same fortress let Berkshire be the aggressive buyer of **[[Wells Fargo]]** during the
  1990 bank panic — happy to "love… businesses… that produced no return for a year" if
  they could then earn 20% on growing equity. Buffett's risk preference, stated flatly:
  "we always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."
- [1998] **Financial strength as the rationale for the [[General Re]] acquisition.** Buffett calls
  Berkshire "a **Fort Knox of capital**" — and that, paired with a temperament that "**happily
  accepts volatility**," is "the perfect structure for writing, and *retaining*, reinsurance in
  virtually any amount." A *publicly-held* reinsurer is penalized (in P/E and credit ratings) for
  the very earnings swings reinsurance produces, forcing it into costly retrocessions or rejected
  business; Berkshire's balance sheet removes that constraint, letting General Re retain far more of
  what it writes. Net worth reached **$57.4B** (the largest of any U.S. corporation), funding a
  $22.76B float and an elephant-hunting war chest (>$15B cash at year-end). The credo recurs: "we
  prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."
- [2008] **The fortress pays off in the crisis — and a perverse twist.** Goal #1 is restated as
  "maintaining Berkshire's **Gibraltar-like financial position** — huge amounts of excess liquidity,
  near-term obligations that are modest, and dozens of sources of earnings and cash." That strength
  let Berkshire be the crisis **"buyer of choice,"** writing $14.5B of fixed-income-plus-equity
  checks ([[Goldman Sachs]], [[General Electric]], [[Wrigley]]) when leveraged rivals were
  retrenching, and funding them by selling other holdings rather than drawing down cash: "**we never
  want to count on the kindness of strangers**… I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance
  of extra profits." But Buffett flags a galling distortion: with government guarantees flowing to
  banks, AAA Berkshire's borrowing spreads hit record levels — "**it is much better to be a financial
  cripple with a government guarantee than a Gibraltar without one**" — squeezing [[Clayton Homes]]'s
  lending arm. He also warns of a **U.S. Treasury bond bubble** and reaffirms refusing leverage that
  could ever threaten the fortress.
- [2014] **The "three rules of financial staying power" (50th-anniversary essay).** Berkshire is "the
  **Gibraltar** of American business," kept so by three strengths maintained "under all circumstances":
  (1) a **large, diversified earnings stream**; (2) **massive liquid assets — at least $20 billion** in
  **U.S. Treasury bills** ("not other substitutes… When bills come due, only cash is legal tender. Don't
  leave home without it"); and (3) **no significant near-term cash demands** — no large short-term debt,
  **no more collateral-posting derivatives** (newly-written contracts now require full collateral, so
  "that ended our interest"), and no cash-out insurance. "**Cash… is to a business as oxygen is to an
  individual: never thought about when it is present, the only thing in mind when it is absent.**" The
  creed: "It is **madness to risk losing what you need in pursuing what you simply desire**" — Berkshire
  "will never play financial Russian roulette," even with a 100-chamber gun and one bullet.
- [[2018]] **The four funding sources — and "The Big One."** In the "[[The Five Groves|groves]]" letter
  Buffett lays out Berkshire's funding (the balance sheet's right-hand side): **debt** (used sparingly,
  "Rational people don't risk what they have and need for what they don't have and don't need"; most of it
  sits at the asset-heavy, recession-resistant **BNSF/BHE** and is **not guaranteed** by Berkshire);
  **equity** of **$349 billion** ("unmatched in corporate America," the fruit of retaining all earnings
  since 1965); **[[Insurance Float|float]]** ("more utility… than an equivalent amount of equity"); and
  **deferred taxes** of **$50.5B** — an interest-free "loan" ($14.7B from unrealized equity gains accrued
  at 21%, $28.3B from accelerated depreciation). The $20B-Treasury-bill **cash floor** is reaffirmed
  ("Berkshire will forever remain a financial fortress… I will never risk getting caught short of cash"),
  alongside the recurring warning that "**The Big One**" — a mega-catastrophe dwarfing Katrina, possibly
  even "a cyber attack having disastrous consequences" — will come: "we will get our share of the losses
  and they will be big — very big… [but] we will be looking to add business the next day."
- [[2021]] **"$144 billion?" — the cash fortress, and a $30B floor.** Berkshire held **$144B of cash/
  equivalents** (ex-BNSF/BHE), **$120B in U.S. Treasury bills** maturing within a year ("financing about
  ½ of 1% of the publicly-held national debt"). Charlie and Buffett **pledge to always hold >$30 billion**
  in cash/equivalents — "we want your company to be financially impregnable and never dependent on the
  kindness of strangers (or even that of friends)." The pile reflects not patriotism but a "**failure to
  find**" enough businesses meeting the long-term criteria; Buffett notes such cash-heavy stretches are
  "never pleasant… also never permanent."
- [[2023]] **"Berkshire is built to last."** With GAAP net worth at **$561 billion** (the largest of any
  U.S. company), Buffett restates the fortress pledge: no dividend, "100% discretionary" buybacks, immaterial
  debt maturities, and "a cash and U.S. Treasury bill position far in excess of what conventional wisdom
  deems necessary." In a panic Berkshire aims "to function as an **asset to the country** — just as it was
  in a very minor way in 2008-9 — and to help extinguish the financial fire rather than… ignite the
  conflagration." Its strength flows from "a **Niagara of diverse earnings**" (after real D&A — "EBITDA is
  a banned measurement at Berkshire"). "Extreme fiscal conservatism is a corporate pledge we make."
- [[2024]] **A record cash pile — but equities still the preference.** After heavy stock sales (marketable
  equities **$354B → $272B**), Berkshire's Treasury-bill hoard swelled to a record (widely noted as
  ~$300B+). Buffett pushes back on "what some commentators… view as an extraordinary cash position": "the
  **great majority of your money remains in equities**… Berkshire will never prefer ownership of
  cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses." The fortress logic now carries a
  **currency caveat**: "**Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails**… Fixed-coupon
  bonds provide no protection against runaway currency" — hence the lifelong reliance on equities. The
  CEO-as-Chief-Risk-Officer creed and owner-aligned, option-free compensation are reaffirmed ("if you
  lose money, so do we").
