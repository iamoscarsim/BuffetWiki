# Executive Compensation

Buffett's philosophy on paying managers — reward performance the manager actually
controls, measured against the cost of capital — and his recurring critique of
conventional stock options. The fullest early statement is the [[1985]] letter.

His critique of **ten-year fixed-price options**: they reward managers simply for
*retaining* earnings, since retained earnings automatically build value (the
savings-account-with-a-trustee analogy — a trustee with an option profits "just from
having held on to most of your earnings"). They ignore the **cost of capital**, are
"blind to individual performance" (the sluggard is rewarded like the star — "a
managerial Rip Van Winkle... could not wish for a better incentive system"), and are
falsely sold as putting managers "in the same boat" as owners — though "an owner
must weigh upside potential against downside risk; an option holder has no downside."
If options are used, he argues, they should: go only to those with overall
responsibility, carry a retained-earnings/carrying-cost factor, and be priced at true
business value. (He concedes some admired managers run fine option cultures — "if it
ain't broke, don't fix it.")

**Berkshire's system**: reward key managers for results "in their own bailiwicks,"
*not* tied to Berkshire's stock price; uncapped and non-hierarchical (a small unit's
manager can out-earn a big unit's); indifferent to seniority/age; "performance"
defined relative to each business's tailwinds or headwinds (".350 hitter," ".150
hitter"). Managers are free to buy Berkshire stock with their bonuses — bearing the
real risks and carrying costs of ownership.

## Evolution
- [1985] Full essay: the case against indiscriminate fixed-price options; Berkshire's
  bailiwick-based, uncapped, stock-price-independent bonus system.
- [1988] The **CEO-accountability** essay (a companion to the comp critique): "the
  supreme irony of business management is that it is far easier for an inadequate CEO to
  keep his job than… an inadequate subordinate." A typist or salesperson is measured
  against a clear standard and let go if they miss; a CEO faces no such standard ("the
  boss shoots the arrow of managerial performance and then hastily paints the bullseye
  around the spot where it lands"), reports to a board that "seldom measures itself," and
  benefits from a norm where criticism is "the social equivalent of belching." Buffett
  contrasts this with the manager-owners of Berkshire's three permanent holdings, who
  "think like owners" — the standard against which fixed pay and entrenchment look worst.
- [1991] The **[[H. H. Brown]] capital-charge model** — Buffett's favorite real-world
  example of the principle. Brown's key managers draw a token **$7,800 salary plus a
  percentage of profits *after a charge for the capital employed***, so "these managers truly
  stand in the shoes of owners." It operationalizes his core complaint: conventional plans are
  "long on carrots but short on sticks" and "almost invariably treat equity capital as if it
  were cost-free." By making managers pay for the capital they use, Brown's system aligns them
  with owners better than options ever could. Buffett's aphorism: "Managers eager to bet
  heavily on their abilities usually have plenty of ability to bet on."
- [1992] **The plea to expense stock options** — the accounting companion to the 1985 comp
  critique, and one of Buffett's most-quoted campaigns. Even properly structured options
  "are accounted for in ways that make no sense," because "much of the business world has
  waged war against accounting rulemakers" to keep option costs out of reported profits. He
  dismantles the excuses: options are *too hard to value* (he offers to **buy** any
  executive's restricted, even out-of-the-money option for cash), and they're *not a cost
  because no cash leaves the company* (the Council of Institutional Investors' "no cash-no
  cost" line — to which he offers to **sell insurance in exchange for options**). The logic
  reduces to three questions: "**If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If
  compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the
  calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?**" He notes accounting already
  estimates hard-to-pin costs (aircraft depreciation, bank loan losses, P&C reserves) and
  warns the "business elite risks losing its credibility… when it advocates the incredible on
  issues of significance to itself." (See [[Owner Orientation and Candor]] for the broader
  accounting-honesty theme — the Lincoln "tail/leg" riddle and the post-retirement-health
  rule.)
- [1994] **The [[Ralph Schey]]/[[Scott Fetzer]] model in full.** Schey is paid on Scott
  Fetzer's results, *not* Berkshire's — "since he's responsible for one operation but not the
  other" — with a **high charge for incremental capital employed and an equal credit for
  capital released**, so "it pays Ralph — and pays him well — to send to Omaha any cash he
  can't advantageously use" (the **money's-not-free** approach linking pay to [[Capital Allocation]]). True **alignment** "means being a partner in both directions, not just on the
  upside"; many "alignment" plans are "artful forms of '**heads I win, tails you lose**.'"
  Ordinary ten-year options, which don't step up the strike for retained earnings, can hand
  "lush gains to a manager who has done no more than tread water." The Schey deal "was worked
  out in about five minutes… without the 'help' of lawyers or compensation consultants" and
  "has never been changed." Buffett and Munger want little for themselves — "if we were not
  paid at all… [we] would be delighted with the cushy jobs we hold."
- [1996] **The [[GEICO]] plan as a model.** Installed right after the buyout, it ties bonuses
  for [[Tony Nicely]] and dozens of executives (and the company-wide profit-sharing pool) to
  exactly **two variables**: growth in **voluntary auto policies** and underwriting profit on
  **"seasoned" (>1-year) business**. The three design principles: goals (1) **tailored** to the
  specific business's economics, (2) **simple** enough to measure easily, and (3) **directly
  tied** to participants' daily activities. Berkshire "**shun[s] 'lottery ticket'
  arrangements**, such as options on Berkshire shares, whose ultimate value … is totally out of
  the control of the person whose behavior we would like to affect" — quixotic payoffs are both
  "wasteful for owners" and may "**discourage the focused behavior we value**." The chart
  measuring the payout "literally went off the chart" in 1996 (record 16.9% / $40M); Buffett's
  rule: "we never greet good work by raising the bar." [[Lou Simpson]]'s investment bonus is set
  on a separate four-year track, so great underwriting can't be "neutralized by bad work" on
  investments and vice versa ("If you bat .350 at Berkshire, you can be sure you will get paid
  commensurately even if the rest of the team bats .200").
- [1998] **"Accounting — Part 1": the General Re option-to-cash swap as a live demonstration.**
  Replacing **[[General Re]]**'s longstanding **stock-option plan** with an *economically identical*
  **cash plan** added **$63M** of reported compensation expense — pure proof that GAAP "offer[s]
  management a choice: Pay employees in one form and count the cost, or pay them in another form and
  **ignore the cost**" ("small wonder … the use of options has mushroomed"). The thought experiment:
  if Berkshire paid GEICO's $190M of advertising in ten-year at-the-market options, "would anyone …
  argue that Berkshire had not borne a cost?" (Bishop Berkeley's unseen tree). Buffett's practice:
  when valuing an option-issuing company he **marks reported earnings down** by what the options
  would fetch if sold publicly — "frequently cut[ting] the … figures by 5%, with 10% not all that
  uncommon," sometimes enough to "make a sale or … pass on a stock purchase." Options "if properly
  structured, can be … even ideal," but are "more often wildly capricious … inefficient as
  motivators, and inordinately expensive for shareholders." The three questions of 1992 are
  repeated, "to which we have not yet received an answer."
- [1997] "**We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets.**" The GEICO plan delivered a record
  **26.9% / $71M** profit-sharing payout off its two variables (policy growth + seasoned-business
  underwriting profit) — "In none of Berkshire's subsidiaries do we relate compensation to our
  stock price, which our associates cannot affect in any meaningful way … When [performance] is
  terrific … there is nothing Charlie and I enjoy more than writing a big check." The year also
  applies the principle to **acquisitions**: when Berkshire buys an option-issuing company, it
  "promptly substitute[s] a cash compensation plan having an economic value equivalent to that of
  the previous option plan," so the acquiree's "true compensation cost is … **brought out of the
  closet and charged, as it should be, against earnings**" — the practical sequel to the 1992
  expensing campaign (this is why an acquiree's *reported* costs may *rise* under Berkshire even
  as its true costs don't). See [[International Dairy Queen]] (option-substitution applied).
- [2002] **The expensing war recounted — and "More!" → "Less!".** Buffett narrates the political
  history: Arthur Andersen's elite Professional Standards Group ruled in 1992 that options should be
  expensed; "rainmaking" partners reversed it; FASB then voted **7-0** to expense; CEOs and auditors
  "stormed Washington," and the **Senate voted 88-9 against** (Levitt later called his forced
  retreat the act of his SEC chairmanship "**he most regrets**"). Under FASB's resulting "honor
  system," **498 of the 500** S&P companies chose *not* to expense — "Let FASB have the honor; they
  had the system." *(See [[2005]] for the "Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo" / "Fred Futile" sequel and
  the [[Jim Kilts]] counter-example.)* On pay levels he reframes [[Corporate Governance]]'s "acid
  test": from Samuel
  Gompers's labor cry "**More!**" (adopted by 1990s CEOs) to the directors' needed retort
  "**Less!**" — "there's nothing wrong with paying well for truly exceptional business performance.
  But, for anything short of that… directors [should] shout 'Less!'" Berkshire **expenses options**
  and pays directors "a pittance" with no D&O insurance.
- [2005] **Both sides of the ledger — Jim Kilts vs. "Fred Futile."** Buffett pairs his critique with
  a rare paean: **[[Jim Kilts]]** revived a struggling [[Gillette]] (whose **Duracell** purchase had
  destroyed billions) and "earned every penny" — "it's difficult to **overpay the truly
  extraordinary CEO** of a giant enterprise. But this species is rare." The dark side: "the deck is
  stacked against investors," with a mediocre CEO "aided by his handpicked VP of human relations and
  a consultant from the ever-accommodating firm of **Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo**." His
  fixed-price-option parable — "**Fred Futile, CEO of Stagnant, Inc.**" — shows why an option-laden
  CEO will "skip dividends entirely" and buy back stock regardless of merit.
