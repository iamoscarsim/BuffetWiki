# Sainted Seven

"The Sainted Seven" is Buffett's affectionate name, coined in the [[1987]] letter, for
Berkshire's seven largest **non-financial** operating units of the era:
[[Buffalo Evening News]], [[Fechheimer Brothers]], [[Kirby]], [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]],
the [[Scott Fetzer]] Manufacturing Group, [[See's Candies]], and [[World Book]]. (In
later letters the group, as Berkshire added businesses, becomes "the Sainted Seven Plus
One," etc.) It is less a formal concept than a vivid demonstration of the economics
Buffett prizes — high returns on tangible capital, little debt, modest reinvestment
needs — bundled into one number.

The 1987 arithmetic is the point. Together the seven earned **$180 million of operating
earnings before interest and taxes**, with net interest expense of only $2 million, so
essentially all of it was return on the **~$175 million of equity capital** employed.
On an after-tax basis that is roughly **$100 million — about a 57% return on equity** —
a figure Buffett notes almost no large company reaches (a *Fortune* study of the 1,000
biggest companies found the best decade-long ROE was Commerce Clearing House's 40.2%).

Buffett draws three inferences: (1) the units' **business value far exceeds** both
their historical book value and their carrying value on Berkshire's balance sheet (they
were bought at ~$222M above underlying asset value); (2) because they need so little
capital, they can **grow while sending almost all their earnings to Omaha** for
redeployment — the engine of Berkshire's compounding; and (3) they are run by
"truly extraordinary managers" (the Blumkins, the Heldmans, [[Chuck Huggins]],
[[Stan Lipsey]], [[Ralph Schey]]). Importantly, the *managers* should be judged on
returns on the **underlying** assets they work with — "what we pay for a business does
not affect the amount of capital its manager has to work with." The Seven embody
[[Economic Moat]], [[Economic Goodwill]] and [[Owner Earnings]] all at once.

## Evolution
- [1987] Coined. The seven non-financial units earned ~$180M pre-tax on ~$175M equity
  (~57% after-tax ROE); the basis for Buffett's "doing ordinary things exceptionally
  well" thesis and the *Fortune* "25 superstars" comparison.
- [1988] "**The Saints came marching in**" — combined with several smaller units, the
  group earned about **67% on average equity capital**, again unleveraged. Buffett:
  "Without this help Berkshire would not have a chance of achieving our 15% goal… the
  question mark in our future is whether Charlie and I can effectively employ the funds
  that they generate." (The early-1989 [[Borsheim's]] purchase begins the group's
  expansion.)
- [1989] Renamed "**The Sainted Seven Plus One**" with [[Borsheim's]] added (the title
  challenge: "both alliterative and appropriate. We failed"). The group earned **57% on
  average equity** with no net leverage — managers "show up at the ballpark because they
  like to hit home runs." Naming them (the Blumkin, Friedman and Heldman families,
  [[Chuck Huggins]], [[Stan Lipsey]], [[Ralph Schey]]) gives Buffett "the same glow that
  Miller Huggins must have experienced when he announced the lineup of his **1927 New
  York Yankees**."
