# Economic Goodwill

The subject of Buffett's celebrated [[1983]] appendix, "Goodwill and its
Amortization: The Rules and The Realities." **Economic Goodwill** is the capitalized
value of a business's ability to earn returns on tangible assets "considerably in
excess of market rates of return" — the worth of an intangible franchise. It is
entirely different from **accounting Goodwill** (the excess of purchase price over
fair value of net identifiable assets, mechanically amortized over up to 40 years).

The driving example is [[See's Candies]]: bought in 1972 for $25M when it had only
~$8M of net tangible assets and earned ~$2M after tax — i.e., **25% on tangible
assets**, far above normal. "Businesses logically are worth far more than net
tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings... considerably in
excess of market rates of return." Sources of such Goodwill: a **consumer franchise**
(letting product *value*, not cost, set price), government franchises (TV stations),
and an enduring low-cost position.

Two crucial consequences:
- **Accounting Goodwill shrinks while economic Goodwill grows.** See's accounting
  Goodwill is amortized down each year, yet by 1983 it earned $13M on ~$20M of
  tangible assets — economic Goodwill had ballooned. Amortization charges are "not
  true economic costs."
- **Asset-light franchises are the best inflation hedge.** Inflation forces every
  business to pour more dollars into receivables, inventory, and fixed assets just to
  stand still; a See's-type business needs far less such reinvestment than an
  asset-heavy "mundane" business, so far more nominal gain accrues to owners.
  "During inflation, Goodwill is the gift that keeps giving" — debunking "In Goods We
  Trust." (See [[Inflation]], [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]].)

Buffett also coins **"No-Will"** for the spurious accounting Goodwill created when "an
overexcited management purchases a business at a silly price." For analysis he
prescribes ignoring amortization when judging operating economics, but counting
Goodwill at *full intrinsic cost* (not recorded value) when judging an acquisition's
wisdom — "a good business is not always a good purchase."

## Evolution
- [1983] Full appendix. Economic vs accounting Goodwill; the See's illustration; why
  economic Goodwill grows with inflation; "No-Will"; the two analytical perspectives.
- [1985] Quantified across the "three very good businesses" (NFM, See's, Buffalo
  News): pre-tax earnings rose from ~$8M to **$72M** while invested capital grew only
  ~$40M — versus the "average American business" needing ~$5 of capital per $1 of new
  earnings (~$300M would have been required). The asset-light franchise lets Berkshire
  redeploy nearly all the cash these businesses throw off.
- [1986] Two refinements. (1) Manager-built goodwill: Buffett credits his operators
  with raising earnings "while... utilizing little additional capital," building
  economic Goodwill that "does not show up in... net worth" — a "substantial"
  unrecorded gain in 1986. (2) Accounting Goodwill mechanics get the full
  [[Owner Earnings]] treatment via [[Scott Fetzer]]: the $24.3M of accounting Goodwill
  (and other write-ups) amortizes against GAAP earnings without being a real economic
  cost or tax-deductible. Also, in the Taxation essay, the franchise/Goodwill link to
  **who keeps a tax cut**: strong unregulated franchises pocket rate cuts, while
  price-competitive businesses pass them to customers (see [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]).
- [1999] **The argument resurfaces in the purchase-vs-pooling accounting fight.** Buffett agrees
  with managers that **goodwill amortization is "usually spurious"** — "economic goodwill does not,
  in many cases, diminish … in a great many instances … it actually **grows in value over time**."
  His image: economic goodwill "is much like **land**: The value … is sure to fluctuate, but the
  direction … is in no way ordained. At **See's** … economic goodwill has grown, in an irregular
  but very substantial manner, **for 78 years**." But he refuses to bless **pooling** as the escape
  (it pretends there is no acquirer/acquiree — "just ask employees severed from their jobs which
  company was the conqueror"). His reform: record the purchase at fair value, carry the goodwill
  asset **without amortization**, and write it down **only if impaired** — applied retroactively.
  Meanwhile Berkshire's ~**$500M/year** of (non-economic) goodwill amortization mechanically widens
  the gap between book and [[Intrinsic Business Value]].
- [2000] **Goodwill written *down* to zero — at [[Dexter Shoe]].** The mirror image of the See's
  story: when economic goodwill genuinely *vanishes*, the accounting should follow. Buffett charged
  off **all remaining accounting goodwill** from the Dexter purchase, conceding "we clearly have
  [no economic goodwill] at present" — the impairment-write-down he had proposed in 1999 as the
  right alternative to amortization, now applied to his own worst deal. Meanwhile **FASB resolved
  the purchase-vs-pooling fight** along lines Buffett endorsed: ending routine amortization of
  intangibles (write down only on impairment), so Berkshire's "reported earnings will more closely
  reflect economic reality" — "though none of this will have an effect on … intrinsic value."
