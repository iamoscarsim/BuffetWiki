# Good Business at a Fair Price

The maxim that crystallizes Buffett's evolution away from Benjamin Graham's
"cigar-butt" bargain-hunting toward the [[Charlie Munger]] philosophy of paying up
for quality: **"It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a
fair company at a wonderful price."** The [[1979]] letter contains the pivot in
near-final form.

Reflecting on the **Waumbec Mills** mistake (a textile maker bought below working
capital — a textbook statistical bargain — that never came good), Buffett
concludes: "'Turnarounds' seldom turn, and... the same energies and talent are much
better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor
business purchased at a bargain price." He contrasts textiles with a network TV
station, where "it is virtually impossible to avoid earning extraordinary returns
on tangible capital" — assets that rightly sell for "one thousand cents or more on
the dollar." His verdict: "the 'easy' business may be the better route to go,"
even at a fancy price.

This idea, paired with [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]], becomes the backbone
of Berkshire's acquisition strategy for the next forty years.

## Evolution
- [1979] Stated via the Waumbec mistake: turnarounds seldom turn; prefer a good
  business at a fair price over a poor business at a bargain price.
- [1980] The crystallized maxim, via GEICO/turnaround discussion: **"when a
  management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation
  for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains
  intact."** [[GEICO]] only *looked* like an exception — it was a sound franchise
  with "a localized excisable cancer," not a genuine turnaround.
- [1987] The principle restated and personally confessed: "we try to buy… an
  **outstanding business at a sensible price, not a mediocre business at a bargain
  price**… making silk purses out of silk is the best that we can do; with sow's ears,
  we fail." Buffett admits it took him "20 years to recognize how important it was to
  buy good businesses," after an "education in the economics of short-line farm
  implement manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile
  manufacturers." The *Fortune* "25 superstars" study (mundane, low-leverage franchises
  earning >20% ROE for a decade) supports the thesis; see [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [1989] The fullest confession, in "Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years." The
  **cigar-butt** approach — buying a poor business cheap for "one puff" of profit — is
  "foolish… unless you are a liquidator": "no sooner is one problem solved than another
  surfaces — there is never just one cockroach in the kitchen," and a low-return business
  erodes any bargain. The maxim: "**Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the
  enemy of the mediocre.**" "Good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on
  broken-down nags." And the operating creed — "**easy does it**": Buffett and Munger
  succeed "because we concentrated on identifying one-foot hurdles that we could step over
  rather than… clear[ing] seven-footers." See [[Mistakes Ledger]] (Hochschild Kohn) and
  [[Institutional Imperative]].
