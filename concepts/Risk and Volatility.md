# Risk and Volatility

Buffett's definition of investment risk — and his frontal attack on the academic equation
of risk with **beta** (price volatility) — is set out at length in the [[1993]] letter. He
defines risk, "using dictionary terms, as **the possibility of loss or injury**": specifically,
the chance that "aggregate after-tax receipts from an investment… will [not], over [the]
prospective holding period, give… at least as much purchasing power as [at] the beginning,
plus a modest rate of interest."

**Against beta.** Academics treat a stock's past volatility relative to the market as risk,
"compute with precision the 'beta'… and then build arcane investment and capital-allocation
theories around this calculation." Buffett: "It is **better to be approximately right than
precisely wrong**." The theory "produces absurdities" — it labeled [[Washington Post]]
*riskier* after its 1973 price crash, when the cheaper price made it manifestly *safer*. The
true investor *welcomes* volatility ([[Mr. Market]]), since a manic-depressive market attaches
"irrationally low prices… to solid businesses." A beta purist "may even prefer not to know
the company's name" — whereas Buffett "would not be disturbed if markets closed for a year or
two" after buying.

**The five real factors.** To judge risk, assess:
1. the certainty with which the business's **long-term economics** can be evaluated;
2. the certainty with which **management** can be evaluated — its ability to realize the
   business's potential and wisely deploy cash;
3. the certainty that management will **channel rewards to shareholders**, not itself;
4. the **purchase price**;
5. the **taxes and inflation** that will erode the purchasing-power return.

These resist any database, but "the difficulty of precisely quantifying these matters does
not negate their importance" — Justice Stewart on obscenity, "I know it when I see it." A
[[Coca-Cola]] (44% of world soft drinks) or [[Gillette]] (60% of blades) plainly carries less
business risk than "any computer company or retailer," yet shares a similar beta with
"run-of-the-mill companies" — proof the metric is meaningless. Beta "has no mechanism for
differentiating the risk inherent in… a single-product toy company selling **pet rocks**…
from… **Barbie**." Concentration in a few understood, advantaged businesses (see
[[Concentration]], [[Circle of Competence]]) *reduces* this real risk; wide diversification
is only rational for the know-nothing investor (an index fund) or for inherently risky,
mutually-independent bets like [[Arbitrage]] (the casino's roulette wheel).

## Evolution
- [1993] Full essay: risk = possibility of permanent loss of purchasing power, not beta;
  the Washington Post absurdity; the five qualitative factors; "approximately right vs.
  precisely wrong"; pet rock vs. Barbie; concentration as risk-*reducing*.
- [[2017]] **The argument extended to bonds — and to leverage.** Two reinforcements of the 1993 thesis.
  (1) **Leverage is the real danger.** A table of Berkshire's **four major drawdowns** (−59.1% in
  1973–75, −37.1% in 1987, −48.9% in 1998–2000, −50.7% in 2008–09) is "the strongest argument I can
  muster **against ever using borrowed money to own stocks**" — "there is simply no telling how far
  stocks can fall," and even small borrowings can rattle the mind into bad decisions; declines instead
  "offer extraordinary opportunities to those who are not handicapped by debt" (capped by Kipling's
  "**If**"). (2) **Over long horizons, bonds can be riskier than stocks.** Defining risk as the chance
  that an investment fails to preserve future purchasing power, Buffett argues (via [[Index Funds and Fees|"The Bet"]]'s bonds-to-Berkshire switch) that "purportedly 'risk-free' long-term bonds in 2012
  were a far riskier investment than… common stocks" — "**it is a terrible mistake for investors with
  long-term horizons… to measure their investment 'risk' by their portfolio's ratio of bonds to stocks.
  Often, high-grade bonds in an investment portfolio increase its risk.**"
