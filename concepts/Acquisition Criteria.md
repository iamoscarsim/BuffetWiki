# Acquisition Criteria

Buffett's four tests for buying a business — applied identically whether
purchasing an entire company or "small pieces of a company; i.e., marketable
common stocks." Stated in the [[1977]] letter: we want a business that is

1. **one that we can understand** (see [[Circle of Competence]]),
2. **with favorable long-term prospects**,
3. **operated by honest and competent people**, and
4. **available at a very attractive price.**

The criteria express the unity at the heart of his method: there is no real
distinction between investing in stocks and acquiring businesses — only between
buying a fraction and buying the whole. Buffett notes that "pro-rata portions of
truly outstanding businesses sometimes sell in the securities markets at very
large discounts from the prices they would command in negotiated transactions
involving entire companies," so the four criteria often find better bargains
through the stock market than through whole-company deals. These tests recur, in
nearly identical wording, throughout the letters and later harden into Berkshire's
published acquisition "want ad."

## Evolution
- [1977] Four criteria stated; explicitly applied to both whole businesses and
  marketable stocks.
- [1981] Berkshire will buy whole businesses "at prices that will make sense, even
  should the future... develop much along the lines of its past" — and "will not
  normally pay a lot... for what we are supposed to bring to the party." Prefers
  "10% of Wonderful Business T at X" to "100% of T at 2X." Currently finds best value
  in open-market fractional stakes of franchises with honest, able managers. The
  flip side — overpriced takeovers — is treated under [[Premium Takeovers]].
- [1982] The formal **acquisition "want ad"** debuts (and recurs for decades), seeking:
  (1) large purchases (≥$5M after-tax earnings); (2) demonstrated consistent earning
  power (no projections, no turnarounds); (3) good ROE with little/no debt; (4)
  management in place ("we can't supply it"); (5) simple businesses ("if there's lots
  of technology, we won't understand it" — see [[Circle of Competence]]); (6) an
  offering price stated up front. No hostile deals; "a very fast answer... within five
  minutes." Cash preferred; stock only on the terms in [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]].
- [1986] The want ad **pays off**: [[Fechheimer Brothers]] came in when Chairman Bob
  Heldman read it and wrote to Buffett. Re-run with the **minimum after-tax earnings
  raised from $5M to $10M** (criterion 1), the rest unchanged. Buffett adds that
  stock-financed deals "may be quite feasible" after Berkshire's share-price rise, and
  separately invites **negotiated purchases of large, non-controlling stock blocks**
  (as with [[Capital Cities]]) when comfortable with the economics and the people.
- [1991] The want-ad is **re-run after the [[H. H. Brown]] purchase** (criteria unchanged:
  ≥$10M after-tax earnings, consistent earning power, good ROE with little debt, management
  in place, simple business, a stated price). Buffett laments that of four major acquisitions
  whose sellers used prominent investment banks, the bank thought of Berkshire in only **one**
  case — "we would love to see an intermediary earn its fee by thinking of us." His
  best-loved deal shape is restated: the **[[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]/[[Fechheimer Brothers]]/[[Borsheim's]] pattern**, where owner-managers want liquidity (for themselves or
  inactive family) yet keep running the business. Two memorable lines: on off-target pitches,
  "if you advertise an interest in buying collies, a lot of people will call hoping to sell you
  their cocker spaniels"; and the country-song brush-off for turnarounds and auctions, "When
  the phone don't ring, you'll know it's me." He repeats: no unfriendly takeovers, complete
  confidentiality, an answer "customarily within five minutes."
- [1992] **[[Central States Indemnity]]** (82%, Dec) is held up as "a **prototype** of what we
  now look for" — the resolution of the toads-and-princesses lesson (see [[Premium Takeovers]]):
  buy "**good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices**"
  ("Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent"). Buffett also formalizes a
  second, lower-risk channel — **add-on acquisitions** by existing subsidiaries (five in 1992,
  the largest being [[H. H. Brown]]'s Lowell Shoe deal): "we enlarge the domain of managers we
  already know to be outstanding — a low-risk and high-return proposition." Separately, the
  equity-selection creed is restated with one change: market conditions and Berkshire's size
  now compel substituting "**an attractive price**" for the original "*a very* attractive
  price."
- [1993] **[[Dexter Shoe]]** exemplifies the criteria — superb economics plus managers
  ([[Harold Alfond]], [[Peter Lunder]]) Buffett wants as partners — and showcases his pitch to
  **owners of large private businesses** weighing a sale: Berkshire offers tax-free
  diversification, easy gifting/liquidity, a permanent "home," and the promise that "we do not
  tell **.400 hitters** how to swing." Buffett disclaims any top-down strategy: "we have no view
  of the future that dictates what businesses or industries we will enter… it's usually poison…
  if [a corporate giant] embarks upon new ventures pursuant to some grand vision" — better to
  focus on business economics and manager quality "and then hope we get lucky in finding the two
  in combination." ("Five years ago we had no thought of getting into shoes… So much for
  strategic plans.")
- [1994] **"Price, not time."** Berkshire bought NICO (1967), See's (1972), Buffalo News
  (1977), NFM (1983), and Scott Fetzer (1986) simply "because those are the years they became
  available" at acceptable prices — pondering "what the business was likely to do, not what the
  Dow, the Fed, or the economy might do." The same logic governs stock purchases: "Why scrap an
  informed decision because of an uninformed guess?" Buffett also elevates **adding to existing
  holdings** ("if a business is attractive enough to buy once, it may well pay to repeat the
  process") — he enlarged [[Coca-Cola]] and [[American Express]] in 1994. Two memorable framings:
  "the truly big investment idea can usually be **explained in a short paragraph**," and the
  scorecard "is not computed using Olympic-diving methods — **degree-of-difficulty doesn't
  count**." With a "fat wallet" the size threshold is now **$100M deployable** per idea, but the
  standards stay (Ted Williams's "happy zone").
- [1995] A bumper year (three deals) reaffirming, not relaxing, the skepticism. Buffett restates
  the **double-barrelled** strategy — buy whole businesses *and* part-interests via the stock
  market, "always mentally compar[ing] any move… with dozens of other opportunities" (Woody Allen
  on eclecticism). He never reads sellers' projections ("when he's walking fine, sell him"; the
  limping horse "peddled as Secretariat") and cites Drucker on why bad deals happen ("dealmaking
  beats working… romantic, sexy"). Berkshire's edges: **no strategic plan**, and a **stock backed
  by an extraordinary collection of businesses** that lets a seller defer taxes and trust the
  "buying to keep" promise. The criteria threshold is now **"$25 million or more before tax."**
  The **[[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]]** and **[[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]** deals exemplify
  "**ABWA — acquisitions by walking around**" (street-corner and Blumkin-network introductions),
  and the [[GEICO]] purchase shows the appetite for great economics + great management.
- [1996] Two deals "both possessing exactly the qualities we seek — **excellent business economics
  and an outstanding manager**" — arrived by **serendipity**, illustrating Buffett's "ABWA"
  sourcing: **[[Kansas Bankers Surety]]** came from a chance birthday-party seatmate (a KBS
  director), closed at $75M two days after the financials arrived; **[[FlightSafety International]]** (~$1.5B) came from a shareholder who spotted the fit and wrote to [[Salomon]].
  The want-ad is re-run ("if you own a large business with good economic characteristics … give me
  a call"). FlightSafety also marks a first: Berkshire's appetite now extends to a **capital-
  intensive** business when the economics and manager are right.
- [1997] The **"any more at home like you?"** referral method bears fruit: asked after the 1983
  [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]] purchase, the Blumkins named three furniture standouts; that chain ran
  to [[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings|R.C. Willey]] ([[Bill Child]], 1995) and then to **[[Star Furniture]]** (1997), with **[[International Dairy Queen]]** added the same year. Both Star and IDQ
  "fully meet our criteria: They are understandable; possess excellent economics; and are run by
  outstanding people," and both were sealed on integrity rather than due diligence ("I knew I was
  dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted"). The cash-vs-stock preference hardens
  (see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]): both IDQ and the prior FlightSafety deal used a
  **cash-tilted** option to steer sellers away from Berkshire stock.
- [1998] **Elephant hunting.** With net worth at $57.4B, "popcorn stands just won't do" — earning
  15% means finding "big ideas," and Buffett vows to "stay focused on the hunt" for elephants even
  though "today's markets are not friendly to our search." The year landed two: **[[General Re]]**
  ($22B) and **[[Executive Jet]]** ($725M). The pitch to public-company CEOs is reframed as the
  **"GEICO-like environment"** — freedom from boards, analysts, and quarterly-earnings pressure — for
  any "large, profitable business that will thrive" in it ("I promise a fast answer and will mention
  your inquiry to no one except Charlie"). Both deals again show the **referral/relationship**
  sourcing (EJA via H.H. Brown's [[Frank Rooney]]; General Re a long-standing reinsurance
  counterparty).
- [2000] **The biggest single-year haul: eight businesses (~$8B, 97% cash).** "Our acquisition
  technique … is simplicity itself: **We answer the phone**" — and it "rings a bit more often now,
  because owners and/or managers increasingly wish to join … with Berkshire." Each 2000 deal arrived
  by phone or fax and was sealed fast: CORT (a broker's fax), U.S. Liability (a [[Ron Ferguson]]
  intro), Ben Bridge ([[Barnett Helzberg]]'s recommendation), Justin (a stranger's fax), Shaw (a
  declined asbestos policy), Benjamin Moore (a director's call, "$1 billion cash offer **on the
  spot**"), Johns Manville (a Monday call after the LBO collapsed). Two tailwinds: sellers feared
  near-term slowdowns, and the **junk-bond market dried up**, so LBO bidders couldn't fund deals —
  "Because we analyze purchases on an all-equity basis … we became considerably more competitive."
  The deeper edge is being the **buyer of choice**: sellers who "love[] [their] company, not just
  the money" trust Berkshire's promises (to keep Justin in Fort Worth, not to merge the Bridges'
  jeweler). "How much better … for the 'painter' of a business **Rembrandt** to personally select
  its permanent home … We'll leave the auctions to others" (see [[Permanent Ownership]]).
