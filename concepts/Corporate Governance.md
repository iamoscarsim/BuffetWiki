# Corporate Governance

Buffett's framework for the role of directors, set out in the [[1993]] letter, distinguishes
**three fundamentally different owner/manager situations** — the legal duty of directors is
identical in each, but their *power to effect change* differs sharply. Commentators, he
argues, usually ignore the distinction.

1. **No controlling shareholder** (by far the most common). Directors should act "as if there
   is a single **absentee owner**, whose long-term interest they should try to further." A
   well-functioning board must change mediocre management and "slap the hands" of able-but-
   greedy managers who "dip too deeply into the shareholders' pockets." A dissenting director
   should try to persuade the board, and — if the issue is serious and he fails — feel free to
   make his case to the owners. Boards should be small (≤10), mostly **outside**, and should
   evaluate the CEO against standards **without him present**. Director requisites: "business
   savvy, interest in the job, and **owner-orientation**" — not prominence or diversity (a
   "pleasant but vacuous director need never worry about job security").

2. **Controlling owner who is also the manager** (Berkshire's current mode, often reinforced
   by dual-class voting stock). The board "does not act as an agent between owners and
   management"; outside directors can only **persuade or object**, and "if change does not
   come, and the matter is sufficiently serious, the outside directors should **resign**" — a
   signal of their doubts.

3. **Controlling owner not in management** (e.g. Hershey Foods, Dow Jones). The most effective
   for ensuring first-class management: an unhappy outside director "can go directly to the
   owner," who "can forthwith effect change" and "can readily correct any mistake he makes."

**Berkshire's path.** Berkshire is in mode 2 "for as long as I remain functional," and will
move to **mode 3** after Buffett's death, when his stock passes to **[[Susan Buffett|Susie]]**
or a foundation — a "vitally interested, but non-management, owner." Susie was elected to the
board earlier and son **[[Howard Buffett|Howard]]** joined in 1993 to represent the controlling
interest (the family will not manage). Most other directors are significant owners with "a
strong owner-orientation." "All in all, we're prepared for 'the truck.'" The essay extends
[[Owner Orientation and Candor]] and [[Executive Compensation]] (CEO accountability) into the
boardroom.

## Evolution
- [1993] Full essay: the three owner/manager governance models and a director's leverage in
  each; small, outside, owner-oriented boards; Berkshire's planned move from mode 2 to mode 3;
  Howard Buffett joins the board.
- [2001] **The Enron-era indictment.** Against the backdrop of Enron — "the symbol for shareholder
  abuse" — Buffett sharpens the owner/manager conflict into a moral one: "shareholders have
  suffered billions in losses while the CEOs, promoters, and other higher-ups who fathered these
  disasters have walked away with extraordinary wealth," many "urging investors to buy shares while
  concurrently dumping their own." Such leaders "view shareholders as **patsies, not partners**."
  The emblem is the party joke — a CEO asked what he'd most like, who answers without hesitation,
  "**Reprice my options**." Buffett re-states his own alignment (no cash comp, restricted stock, or
  options; >99% of net worth in Berkshire; never sold a share) and holds up **[[MiTek]]**'s 55
  managers who bought stock with their own cash as the antithesis of option culture (see
  [[Executive Compensation]], [[Owner Orientation and Candor]]).
- [2002] **The full post-bubble essay.** Buffett returns to the 1993 framework and diagnoses why
  "intelligent and decent directors failed so miserably" — not weak law but "**boardroom
  atmosphere**," where it is "like belching at the dinner table" to question a CEO's acquisition or
  pay. He skewers the cult of codified "**independence**" with a **62-year case study**: federal
  law has required mostly-independent mutual-fund boards since 1940, yet they "**failed miserably**"
  at their two real jobs — picking the best manager and negotiating the lowest fee (directors who
  scrutinize their *own* money never switch managers as fiduciaries; his own daughter declined such
  a directorship). The real fix is **big owners acting** ("twenty… of the largest institutions,
  acting together, could effectively reform corporate governance"). The "**acid test… is CEO
  compensation**" — from labor's 1890s cry "**More!**" to the needed "**Less!**". Berkshire will add
  codified-independent directors but values **true purchased ownership**, pays directors "a
  pittance," and carries **no D&O insurance** (so directors' "family's net worth," not fees, drives
  them). A companion **Audit Committee** reform: four questions that put auditors "on the spot."
  See [[Executive Compensation]] (the options-expensing saga) and [[Owner Orientation and Candor]].
- [2003] **The mutual-fund scandal vindicates the critique — and Berkshire's "owner-capitalism."**
  Months after the 2002 essay, Eliot Spitzer's investigations exposed fund managers who "**trampled
  on the interests of fund shareholders**" while their independent directors stayed silent (none, as
  Buffett wrote, had even fired the offending management company). "Boardroom atmosphere almost
  invariably sedates their fiduciary genes." He proposes two annual affirmations independent fund
  directors should sign (we've checked other managers; we negotiated a competitive fee) and praises
  low-cost index funds (Vanguard). **Berkshire's board** is offered as the antidote: 11 directors,
  each with **>$4M** of family stock bought in the market, **no D&O insurance** — "**you win, they
  win big; you lose, they lose big… owner-capitalism**" ("who ever washes a rental car?"). Four
  owner-directors join (**[[David Gottesman]]**, Charlotte Guyman, **[[Don Keough]]**, **[[Tom Murphy]]**), and the board's "**primary job**" is named: selecting Buffett's successor (four
  internal candidates identified). On pay, CEO comp "remains the **acid test**," with GE's Jeff
  Immelt cited as a rare good example "more admired than followed."
- [2009] **The crisis postscript: the CEO must own risk control, and failed bosses need "sticks."**
  After the financial meltdown, Buffett argues "a board of directors of a huge financial institution
  is **derelict if it does not insist that its CEO bear full responsibility for risk control**" — a
  job that "must not [be] delegate[d]" to a Risk Committee or Chief Risk Officer ("If Berkshire ever
  gets in trouble, **it will be my fault**"). He rejects the "bailed-out shareholder" framing:
  owners of the failed firms lost >$500B (90%+ of value in most failures) while "the CEOs and
  directors… have largely gone unscathed." The reform he urges is **meaningful downside** — penalties
  "not reimbursable by the companies they've damaged nor by insurance": "CEOs and… directors have
  long benefitted from oversized financial carrots; some meaningful **sticks** now need to be part of
  their employment picture." (Pairs with the boardroom-courage theme and [[Executive Compensation]].)
- [[2019]] **The "independence" essay — the cocker spaniel, not the pit bull.** Drawing on **21
  public-company directorships over 62 years** (he lists them), Buffett argues the codified definition of
  director "independence" is backwards. Soaring fees ($250,000–300,000 for "a pleasant couple of days six
  or so times a year") make the **non-wealthy director (NWD)** subconsciously dependent on the income — and
  eager for a *second* board, which requires being deemed a "good" (read: compliant) director by the
  current CEO. "When seeking directors, CEOs don't look for pit bulls. **It's the cocker spaniel that gets
  taken home.**" Perversely, "the director for whom fees are important… is almost universally classified as
  '**independent**,'" while big owner-shareholders are deemed *not* independent — he cites a laggard
  company whose eight directors had **never bought a share with their own money**. Other notes: the
  "bedrock challenge" is still to find and keep an able, honest CEO; audit committees remain "no match" for
  CEOs who "game numbers" out of ego; and no CEO ever "bring[s] in an informed and articulate critic to
  argue against" a coveted acquisition — "**Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut.**" Berkshire
  seeks "business-savvy directors who are owner-oriented." (Linked to his **will** disclosure — Berkshire's
  durability rests on directors' "future judgment and fidelity"; see [[The Berkshire System]].)
