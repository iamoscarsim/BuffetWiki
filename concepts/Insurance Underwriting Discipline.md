# Insurance Underwriting Discipline

The principle that an insurer should write business only at adequate prices — and
accept shrinking volume when rates turn inadequate — rather than chase market
share into unprofitable policies. It is the defining discipline of Berkshire's
insurance operations and a recurring theme from the very first letter.

In [[1977]] Buffett explains the mechanics: insurance costs (repairing humans and
property, plus "social inflation" — juries broadening what policies cover) rise at
close to **1% per month**, so unless rates keep pace, underwriting profits shrink.
Pricing mistakes can only be corrected at annual renewal, so the cycle turns
slowly. When competitors retreated after the 1974–75 crisis, [[National Indemnity Company]]
earned excellent margins; but Buffett warns the pendulum will swing back, and then
"unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it runs counter to normal
institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away business — even at
foolish prices."

This willingness to lose volume to preserve underwriting profit is the cultural
foundation that, decades later, lets Berkshire write enormous catastrophe and
super-cat business on its own terms. Profitable underwriting is also what makes
[[Insurance Float]] genuinely "free" or better.

## Evolution
- [1977] Stated as core insurance philosophy: accept reduced volume rather than
  write at inadequate rates; insurance costs rise ~1%/month.
- [1978] Sharper on reinsurance: "It is very easy to fool yourself regarding
  underwriting results in reinsurance... self-delusion in company reserving almost
  always leads to inadequate industry rate levels." When major players don't know
  their true costs, the competitive fallout hits even disciplined insurers. Warns
  the 1979 cycle will compress margins (auto rates +3% vs. loss costs +9%).
- [1979] The discipline in action: [[Phil Liesche]] earned $8.4M underwriting
  profit on ~$82M premiums while *letting volume fall* — "if business makes sense,
  he writes it; if it doesn't, he rejects it." Policy of **not laying off staff**
  during voluntary volume swings ("we would rather have some slack... than keep
  everyone terribly busy writing business on which we are going to lose money").
  Predicts low interest-rate-driven competition will push the industry's tolerated
  combined ratio higher; forecasts a ~105 five-year industry average.
- [1980] Names the mechanism prolonging the soft market: **"cash-flow"** and
  **"asset-maintenance" underwriting** — insurers writing terrible business at any
  price to hold investable assets, partly because recognizing huge unrealized
  *bond* losses (carried at amortized cost) would gut stated net worth. "Right
  behind having financial problems yourself, the next worst plight is to have a
  large group of competitors with financial problems." [[National Indemnity Company]]
  hit all-time-high relative margins with flat volume; Berkshire will accept lower
  1981 volume rather than chase underpriced business.
- [1981] Presents Best's combined-ratio history and forecasts **1982 "the worst year
  in recent history"** for underwriting — guaranteed by present pricing plus the
  ~12-month policy term. Rejects the tidy "underwriting cycle" view: expects "very
  large... underwriting losses [to] be the norm," with future good years looking
  substandard against the past. Notes results have worsened *despite* good luck
  (hurricanes at sea, less driving). Berkshire's managers "swam against the tide,"
  sacrificing volume; its [[Financial Strength]] "could become an operational asset
  of immense value."
- [1982] The structural argument: insurance's historic profitability rested on a
  quasi-administered, regulator-blessed pricing system that is now "gone" — new
  capacity competes hard on price and "customers have learned that insurance is no
  longer a one-price business." Poor profits alone won't shrink supply; only a
  natural or financial **"megadisaster"** will, after which the well-capitalized
  will reap "great opportunities." Berkshire's own underwriting "deteriorated far
  more than... the industry's" in 1982 (combined-ratio table updated; 1982 est.
  109.5). Stars Milt Thornton (Cypress) and Floyd Taylor (Kansas) kept earning
  underwriting profits every year.
- [1984] Berkshire's combined ratio worsened to **134** (industry 117.7) — a third
  straight year far below industry, rooted in pre-Goldberg decisions. But Buffett
  declares the long-awaited turn: with rivals weakened, "the day may have arrived"
  when [[Financial Strength]] becomes a real competitive edge. A hard market looms
  (industry premium growth from <$5B to >$15B). Structured-settlement/loss-reserve
  business (via Columbia Insurance) grows. See [[Loss Reserving]] for his candid
  account of Berkshire's under-reserving.
- [1985] **The hard market arrives** — the long-awaited payoff for [[Financial Strength]].
  Berkshire's premium volume *tripled*; a "flight to quality" drew large buyers
  ("a policy is only an IOU"), and Berkshire's willingness to write big **net** lines
  (risking $10M on a single event) set it apart as reinsurers pulled back. National
  Indemnity's large-risk ad drew 600 replies and ~$50M of premium. Combined ratio
  improved 134 → 111 but still reflected past under-reserving (the "Except-For
  Insurance Company" story; "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor").
- [1983] Berkshire's own combined ratio hit **121** — "absolutely terrible," which
  Buffett attributes to his own decisions of "two or more years back," not to new
  insurance head [[Mike Goldberg]] (insurance's long lead time cuts both ways). Begins
  using [[Financial Strength]] as a competitive weapon in **structured settlements**
  and **loss-reserve assumption** (run by Don Wurster) — businesses where the buyer's
  decades-long creditworthiness is paramount and few rivals match Berkshire.
- [1986] The hard market pays off: Berkshire's combined ratio fell **111 → 103**, and
  it was likely the **fastest-growing of the country's top 100 insurers** over two
  years. Buffett illustrates its deliberate volatility with National Indemnity's
  general-agent business — monthly volume swung from ~$5M (late 1984) to ~$35M (early
  1986) and back toward $20M as rivals returned: "Our large swings in volume do not
  mean that we come and go... we are its most steadfast participant." Restates the
  **commodity** nature of pricing ("what's needed is not more statesmen, but less
  corn") and identifies **social and judicial inflation** — not general inflation — as
  the cost driver, requiring ~10% annual premium growth just to hold profitability.
  The growing **"large risks"** book ($1–3M-premium policies) suits Berkshire's
  capital; structured settlements went near-dormant on inadequate pricing. (Reserving
  still ran short — see [[Loss Reserving]].)
- [1987] Combined ratio **105** (vs. 103), but profits *improved* on far more **float**
  (and the float-to-premium ratio will keep rising — "Berkshire's insurance profits are
  quite likely to improve… even though we expect our combined ratio to rise"). Buffett
  names Berkshire's **two escapes from commodity economics**: (1) **financial strength**
  — useless in personal lines (claims get paid anyway) and ignored in soft markets, but
  a decisive edge when a buyer asks whether a $10M claim will be honored in 5–10 years
  ("hard for an empty sack to stand upright"); and (2) **total indifference to volume** —
  willing to write 5× or ⅕ as much, "price-based-on-exposure, not-on-competition,"
  enabled by three industry traits (market share doesn't drive profitability,
  distribution isn't proprietary, idle capacity is cheap). Berkshire is a "reservoir of
  standby capacity." The anecdote: an insurance-broker shareholder placed his business
  elsewhere (cheaper) *and* bought more Berkshire stock — "had we been really
  competitive… he would not have bought our stock."
- [1988] Combined ratio **104**; favorable reserve development for a second straight
  year. Premium volume keeps falling (the [[Fireman's Fund]] quota-share expires Aug
  1989, a one-time ~−$85M to "premiums written"), but Buffett is content — "we simply
  will not write policies at rates that carry the expectation of economic loss." The
  payoff is **float**: Berkshire's float-to-premium ratio will run **~3× the industry's**
  in 1989–90, so profits should beat peers despite low volume. The team is deepening —
  [[Mike Goldberg]] "with special help from **[[Ajit Jain]]**, Dinos Iordanou, and the
  National Indemnity team." The industry's poor economics and unpopularity get the
  **cereal-vs-auto-insurance** contrast: Kellogg/General Mills earn double the ROIC and
  sell at 5× book while raising prices freely, yet auto insurers are vilified for
  sub-cost increases (Prop 103). "At some point… we will be deluged with insurance
  business" after the next catastrophe or industry under-reserving reckoning.
- [1989] Two milestones. (1) Buffett **demolishes the "underwriting cycle"** as a misnomer:
  its old rhythmic quality came from the cartel era's lagged, regulator-blessed rate-
  setting, now "long gone." With hundreds of sellers of a commodity, the industry earns
  decently *only* during a capacity shortage — and "shortages will occur only when
  insurers are frightened," which "happens rarely." (2) The **super-catastrophe debut**:
  after Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake left CAT reinsurance suddenly short,
  Berkshire offered up to **$250M of catastrophe coverage and retained it** rather than
  laying it off — accepting a possible ~$165M after-tax single-quarter loss because its
  ~$6B insurance net worth allows it and "we simply don't care what earnings we report
  quarterly." This makes Berkshire a deliberate **stabilizing force** ("Adam Smith's
  invisible hand"), adding capacity when it's scarce. Premium volume may fall to ~$150M in
  1990 (from $1B in 1986) — "so be it." Team: [[Mike Goldberg]] with Rod Eldred, Dinos
  Iordanou, [[Ajit Jain]], Phil Urban, Don Wurster.
- [1990] Combined ratio ~109.8 and still rising; "the most important thing to do when you
  find yourself in a hole is to **stop digging**," but managers "continue to dig — sullenly
  but vigorously." Results will turn only when "most insurance managements become so
  fearful that they run from business," and "when that moment arrives, Berkshire will be
  ready — both financially and psychologically — to write huge amounts of business." Two
  framework advances dominate the year: the **cost of float** (see [[Insurance Float]] —
  funds developed below the government's cost in 19 of 24 years) and full-blown
  **[[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]** ("a lumpy 15%… [over] a smooth 12%"). The
  long-tail pricing rule: never target a 110–115 combined ratio — "aiming for 110–115 is
  business suicide." Buffett also owns his pre-Goldberg errors: "in the insurance business,
  there is no statute of limitations on stupidity."
- [2001] **The three rules, codified — and broken at [[General Re]].** The September-11 disaster
  prompts Buffett's most explicit statement of the discipline: the winners "unfailingly stick to
  three key principles" — (1) accept only risks they can **properly evaluate** (staying within
  their circle of competence) that carry "the expectancy of profit," ignoring market share; (2)
  **limit aggregation** so no single event or correlated set of events "will threaten their
  solvency"; and (3) **avoid moral risk** — "no matter what the rate, trying to write good
  contracts with **bad people** doesn't work." General Re "broke each of the three," above all by
  pricing terrorism off **experience rather than exposure** ("all of us in the industry made a
  fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on experience, rather than exposure, thereby
  assuming a huge terrorism risk for which we received no premium"). Buffett's self-rebuke is the
  **Noah rule**: "Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does" — he had recognized the
  terrorism exposure but "didn't convert thought into action." Berkshire's edge remains its
  willingness to "lose $2 billion to $2½ billion in a single event… **if we have been paid
  properly**" — but it "will control [its] total exposure, no matter what the competition does."
  *(This entry also stands in for the 1991–2000 underwriting material not separately logged here.)*
- [2004] **The "Portrait of a Disciplined Underwriter."** The fullest demonstration of the principle:
  a 25-year table of **[[National Indemnity Company]]** showing written premium deliberately falling
  from **$366M (1986) to $55M (1999)** rather than match competitors' inadequate prices — "we
  consistently priced to make a profit, not to match our most optimistic competitor. We never left
  customers — but they left us." Two cultural enablers make it possible: a **no-layoff promise** (so
  employees never "rationalize inadequate pricing… to save their own skins") and the [[Tom Murphy]]
  rule against overstaffing in fat years (an added employee is "a $3 million decision"). It "takes
  real fortitude — embedded deep within a company's culture": NICO's CEO **[[Don Wurster]]** "will
  accept a walk rather than swing at a bad pitch." The flip side, low-cost operation, is **[[GEICO]]**
  ("foot-to-the-floor"); the two strategies bookend Berkshire's escape from commodity insurance.
