# Class B Shares

The 1995 letter proposes the **recapitalization that created Berkshire's two share classes**
([[1995]]). Existing common becomes **Class A**; a new **Class B** share carries **1/30th of
an A's economic interest** but only **1/200th of a vote** and **no eligibility** for the
[[Shareholder-Designated Contributions]] program. Each A is convertible into 30 B (never the
reverse). Berkshire planned to list the B on the NYSE and to make a public offering of **at
least $100 million** of new B shares to seed a liquid market.

**The motive was defensive.** Promoters were preparing **expense-laden unit trusts** — "low-
priced 'clones' of Berkshire" — to be "sold by brokers working for big commissions" and
"marketed *en masse* to unsophisticated buyers… apt to be seduced by our past record." Buffett
judged the inevitable outcome "a multitude of investors destined to be disappointed," and
created the B as "a low-denomination product far superior to Berkshire-only trusts" to make
the clones "unmerchandisable." Crucially, he stressed the offering would **not dilute intrinsic
value**, because the stock was *not* cheap: at $36,000, "**Berkshire is selling at a price at
which Charlie and I would not consider buying it**" — the stock had "outperformed the business"
over the prior five years. The episode is a vivid case of [[Owner Orientation and Candor]]
(protecting prospective shareholders from high-commission salesmanship) and of Buffett's
preference that Berkshire's price "precisely track its intrinsic value." It also gave
would-be splitters "a do-it-yourself method" via the A→B conversion.

## Evolution
- [1995] Recapitalization proposed: Class B = 1/30 economics, 1/200 vote, no SDC; A↔B
  one-way conversion; ≥$100M offering. Created to block expense-laden Berkshire "clone" unit
  trusts; Buffett warns the stock is not undervalued at $36,000.
- [1996] **The recapitalization is executed.** In May, Berkshire sold **517,500 Class B shares**
  for **$565M** net through [[Salomon]], with deliberate anti-speculative design: a record-low
  **1.5% underwriting commission** (vs. a typical new issue) and an **open-ended** offering size
  to repel IPO "flippers." Trading volume just after the offering — "a rough index of 'flipping'"
  — ran far below the norm, and Berkshire added ~**40,000 shareholders** (roughly doubling its
  count), "most of whom … understand what they own and share our time horizons." The move
  "arrested the sale of the [clone] trusts" while giving small investors a low-cost, direct
  entry. The larger holder base prompted moving the Annual Meeting to the Aksarben Coliseum.
  Buffett also issued (via Salomon) a $500M note **exchangeable into Salomon shares**.
