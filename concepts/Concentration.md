# Concentration

Buffett rejects broad diversification in favor of putting meaningful money behind
his best ideas. Stated plainly in the [[1978]] letter: "Our policy is to
concentrate holdings. We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are
only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are convinced as to
attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts."

The principle follows from [[Stocks as Fractional Ownership]] and the
[[Circle of Competence]]: if good businesses at attractive prices are rare, the
right response to finding one is to buy a lot of it, not to spread thinly across
mediocre alternatives. It stands in deliberate contrast to the diversification
orthodoxy of institutional investors (whom Buffett needles in the same letter for
piling into equities at peaks and abandoning them at bottoms).

## Evolution
- [1978] Stated as explicit policy: concentrate in high-conviction holdings rather
  than diversify into lukewarm ones.
- [1984] Defended via the WPPSS bet (see [[WPPSS Bonds]]). Concentration "makes sense
  only because our insurance business is conducted from a position of exceptional
  [[Financial Strength]]" — most insurers can't withstand a big error. Billy Rose on
  over-diversification: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any
  of them very well." Acting as owners (47% of stock), Buffett and Munger will make
  "intelligent-but-with-some-chance-of-looking-like-an-idiot" decisions that
  conventional managers (fearing the pink slip) avoid.
- [1988] Applied to the new **[[Coca-Cola]]** and **[[Freddie Mac]]** stakes: "We
  continue to concentrate our investments in a very few companies that we try to
  understand well. There are only a handful of businesses about which we have strong
  long-term convictions. Therefore, when we find such a business, we want to participate
  in a meaningful way." Mae West supplies the motto — "**Too much of a good thing can be
  wonderful**" — paired with the holding-forever doctrine (Peter Lynch's "cutting the
  flowers and watering the weeds"; see [[Permanent Ownership]]).
- [1991] The **[[John Maynard Keynes]] endorsement.** Defending the "Rip Van Winkle"/
  stay-put portfolio, Buffett quotes Keynes's August 15, 1934 letter to F. C. Scott: "As time
  goes on, I get more and more convinced that the right method in investment is to put fairly
  large sums into enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the management
  of which one thoroughly believes. It is a mistake to think that one limits one's risk by
  spreading too much between enterprises about which one knows little… there are seldom more
  than two or three enterprises at any given time in which I personally feel myself entitled
  to put *full* confidence." Buffett reframes the logic with a thought experiment — if limited
  to private Omaha businesses, he'd buy into "a few of the best," never "an equal part of every
  business in town," so why behave differently in public markets? He skewers index-like
  institutional trading: calling active traders "investors" is "like calling someone who
  repeatedly engages in one-night stands a romantic." Motto: "If at first you do succeed, quit
  trying."
- [1993] **Concentration as risk-*reducing*.** "We'll now settle for one good idea a year."
  Against "standard diversification dogma," Buffett argues a concentrated policy "may well
  *decrease* risk if it raises, as it should, both the intensity with which an investor thinks
  about a business and the comfort-level he must feel with its economic characteristics before
  buying" (see [[Risk and Volatility]]). The split advice: the **know-nothing** investor should
  buy a low-cost **index fund** and space out purchases — "when 'dumb' money acknowledges its
  limitations, it ceases to be dumb"; the **know-something** investor who can find "five to ten
  sensibly-priced companies that possess important long-term competitive advantages" should
  reject diversification, which "is apt simply to hurt your results and increase your risk" —
  "why… put money into a business that is his 20th favorite rather than… his top choices?"
  ([[Circle of Competence]]). Mae West again: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful." (The
  exception: inherently risky, mutually-independent bets like [[Arbitrage]] *do* require wide
  diversification.)
- [1996] The **college-basketball-stars** image. A sound investment strategy "will often result in
  its practitioner owning a few securities that will come to represent a very large portion of his
  portfolio" — like buying 20% of the future earnings of several college basketball stars: "A
  handful … would go on to achieve NBA stardom, and the investor's take from them would soon
  dominate his royalty stream." To sell down such winners merely because they dominate the
  portfolio is to "trade Michael Jordan because he has become so important to the team" (see
  [[Permanent Ownership]]). When you find a business "whose earnings are virtually certain to be
  materially higher five, ten and twenty years from now," Buffett says, "you should buy a
  **meaningful amount** of stock."
- [[2022]] **"The Secret Sauce" — a dozen decisions in 58 years.** Buffett quantifies concentration's
  payoff with **[[Coca-Cola]]** and **[[American Express]]** (each ~$1.3B, bought in the mid-1990s; each
  now ~5% of net worth and worth $22–25B, throwing off $300–700M of annual dividends). The general law:
  "**The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to
  work wonders**" — Berkshire's "satisfactory results have been the product of **about a dozen truly good
  decisions — that would be about one every five years**." A flat $1.3B bond, by contrast, would now be a
  trivial 0.3% of net worth. The corollary to [[Permanent Ownership]]: hold the winners; the losers shrink
  to irrelevance on their own. (Munger's companion line: "You don't… need to own a lot of things in order
  to get rich.")
