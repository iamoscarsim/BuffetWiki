# Unconventional Commitments

When Berkshire cannot find its favorite commitment — "a well-run and sensibly-priced
business with fine economics" — it usually parks new money in "very short-term instruments
of the highest quality." But "**sometimes … we venture elsewhere**." The **[[1997]]**
letter gives this category its name and its clearest statement, distinguishing it sharply
from buying a wonderful business: with a great business at a fair price "we *know* that we
are going to make money — the only question being when"; with an unconventional commitment
"we *think* that we are going to make money," while "recogniz[ing] that we will sometimes
realize losses, occasionally of substantial size."

The three 1997 positions, all carried at market (so their swings hit reported results):

- **Oil derivatives** — 14.0M barrels remained of a 45.7M-barrel position established in
  1994–95; the 31.7M barrels already settled yielded a **$61.9M** pre-tax gain, with
  $11.6M unrealized at year-end. Bought when oil for future delivery "seemed modestly
  underpriced"; by 1997 Buffett had "no opinion as to its attractiveness."
- **Silver** — **111.2 million ounces** purchased (a **$97.4M** mark-to-market pre-tax
  gain in 1997). Buffett had watched the metal's fundamentals since buying it ~30 years
  earlier (anticipating U.S. demonetization); falling bullion inventories now meant "a
  higher price would be needed to establish equilibrium between supply and demand."
  Notably, "**inflation expectations … play no part** in our calculation of silver's
  value."
- **Long-term zero-coupon U.S. Treasuries** — **$4.6 billion** at amortized cost, a
  **macro bet on falling rates** (zeros' prices move violently with rates). Rates fell, so
  Berkshire booked a **$598.8M** unrealized pre-tax gain — reflected in year-end book
  value. See [[Zero-Coupon Bonds]] for the mechanics (this is the benign Treasury-STRIPS
  use, distinct from the junk-zero abuses Buffett condemned in 1989).

Buffett's framing of the risk is candid: "A macro-based commitment such as this never has
anything close to a 100% probability of being successful. However, you pay Charlie and me
to use our best judgment — not to avoid embarrassment … Try to think kindly of us when we
blow one." The Munger family had >90% of its net worth in Berkshire, the Buffetts >99% —
"we will be feeling your pain."

## Evolution
- [1997] The category named and illustrated by three positions — oil derivatives, 111.2M
  oz of silver, and $4.6B of long-term zero-coupon Treasuries — all alternatives to cash
  when no wonderful business is available at a fair price. A reminder that these carry only
  *probable*, not certain, profit (contrast [[Circle of Competence]], [[Margin of Safety]]).
- [1998] Buffett **reverts to a "never-comment" policy.** The 1997 disclosure was exceptional: the
  silver position was acknowledged because regulators "wished us to," and the zero-coupon bonds
  because their size could materially affect net worth. Absent such reasons, "we see no advantage in
  talking about specific investment actions," so he will not report on unconventional holdings going
  forward "unless we again take a position that is particularly large." He notes only that Berkshire
  "**eliminated certain of the positions discussed last year and added certain others**." The
  rationale is owner-friendly, not coy: "Your Berkshire shares would be worth less if we discussed
  what we are doing" — and media speculation about Berkshire's moves "continues in most cases to be
  incorrect."
