# 2018 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

A pivotal letter for *how* Berkshire reports itself. GAAP net income was just **$4.0 billion** — but
Buffett dissects it into four parts to show the figure is meaningless: **$24.8 billion of operating
earnings** (a record, +41% over 2016's prior high of $17.6B), a **−$3.0B non-cash impairment** (almost
entirely Berkshire's **[[Kraft Heinz]]** stake), **+$2.8B of realized gains**, and a **−$20.6B "loss"
from the reduction in unrealized gains** forced into earnings by the [[Operating Earnings vs GAAP Net Income|new mark-to-market rule]]. The rule produced exactly the "wild and capricious swings" he warned of
in 2017 — quarterly GAAP losses of $1.1B (Q1) and **$25.4B** (Q4) bracketing profits of $12B and $18.5B.
"**Our advice? Focus on operating earnings, paying little attention to gains or losses of any variety.**"

**Abandoning book value.** "For nearly three decades, the initial paragraph featured the percentage change
in Berkshire's per-share **book value**. It's now time to abandon that practice." Book value has "lost the
relevance it once had" for three reasons: Berkshire has **morphed from stocks into operating businesses**
(carried far below worth); the new accounting rules whipsaw the figure; and Berkshire will increasingly
**repurchase shares above book but below intrinsic value** — which *raises* per-share intrinsic value
while *lowering* per-share book value, making the book scorecard "increasingly out of touch with economic
reality." Going forward Buffett will focus on **market price** (this is book value's "farewell appearance"
on the cover table: 2018 book +0.4%, market +2.8%, S&P −4.4%). See [[Intrinsic Business Value]].

**"Focus on the Forest — Forget the Trees": the five groves.** Buffett offers a new framework for
appraising Berkshire as **five "groves"**: (1) the **non-insurance controlled businesses** (the most
valuable grove; earned **$16.8B** after *all* real costs); (2) the **equity portfolio** (~$173B; "5–10%
interests" in great businesses); (3) a **quartet of jointly-controlled companies** (26.7% [[Kraft Heinz]],
50% [[Berkadia Commercial Mortgage|Berkadia]] and Electric Transmission Texas, 38.6% [[Pilot Flying J]] —
~$1.3B); (4) **cash & Treasuries** ($112B + $20B other fixed income, with a pledged **$20B floor**); and
(5) **insurance/float** (whose value is less obvious). The unifying point: "**the whole is greater —
considerably greater — than the sum of the parts**," because one entity can allocate capital seamlessly,
eliminate enterprise risk, fund cheaply with float, and minimize overhead (see [[The Five Groves]],
[[Capital Allocation]]). On the **EBITDA** dodge: stock-based comp and restructuring *are* real costs
("If you call a dog's tail a leg, how many legs does it have? Four — calling a tail a leg doesn't make it
one"). Amortization ($1.4B) is *not* a real cost; depreciation ($8.4B) *understates* true cost; record
capex **$14.5B** (89% U.S.).

**Retained earnings of investees.** With a table of the five largest holdings (Amex, Apple, BofA, Coke,
Wells Fargo), Buffett shows Berkshire's share of their **dividends ($2,966M)** vs. their far larger
**retained earnings ($6,837M)** — value GAAP ignores but which "over the years… delivered capital gains
to Berkshire that totaled more than one dollar for each dollar these companies reinvested." The
**[[American Express]]** example: with shares unchanged for eight years, Berkshire's ownership rose
**12.6% → 17.9%** purely on Amex's buybacks, so its $1.2B share of Amex's 2018 earnings nearly equals the
$1.3B originally paid for the whole stake.

**Repurchases and reporting.** With the **120%-of-book cap scrapped in mid-2018**, Berkshire may now buy
back stock whenever it trades **below intrinsic value** (cash staying above the $20B floor) — benefiting
continuing holders, "obviously… price-sensitive" (see [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]). Berkshire prepares
**no monthly reports, no company-wide budget, no quarterly "number" to hit** — a deliberate guard against
the "cookie-jar reserve"/"trade-loading" slide from "innocent fudge" into "full-fledged fraud."

**Taxes — the "AA shares."** Non-insurance pre-tax income rose to **$20.8B (+24%)**; *after-tax* it jumped
**47%** thanks to the new 21% rate. Buffett frames the Treasury as holder of a special "**AA share**" class
that collects tax "dividends": the 2017 cut handed **40% of the government's stake (14/35ths) back to
shareholders, free of charge**, lifting Berkshire's (and its investees') intrinsic value.

**Insurance.** Float grew to **$122,732M** (now including life/annuity/health float). Berkshire earned an
underwriting profit in **15 of the past 16 years** ($27B pre-tax over the span; **+$2B in 2018**), the
2017 hurricane loss the lone exception. The funding section names Berkshire's **four sources of capital**:
debt (used sparingly, mostly at the asset-heavy BNSF/BHE and unguaranteed), **equity ($349B, "unmatched in
corporate America")**, **float**, and **deferred taxes ($50.5B — $14.7B from unrealized equity gains,
$28.3B from accelerated depreciation; an interest-free "loan")**. The recurring warning: "**The Big One**"
mega-cat (hurricane, quake, or even a cyber attack) will come and "they will be big — very big," but "we
will be looking to add business the next day."

**GEICO and Tony Nicely.** A tribute essay: **[[Tony Nicely]] retired as CEO on June 30, 2018**, handing
the job to long-time partner **[[Bill Roberts]]** (Nicely stays Chairman). The arc — joined 1961 at 18;
[[Jack Byrne]]'s 1976 rescue; Berkshire's ⅓→½ stake for **$47M**; Nicely made CEO 1993 (1.9M policies, 7th
in sales); the 1995 buyout of the rest for **$2.3B (50× the first half)**. Today GEICO is **America's #2
auto insurer**, sales **+1,200%** since 1995, **$15.5B of pre-tax underwriting profit** since purchase,
float **$2.5B → $22.1B** — "Tony's management… increased Berkshire's intrinsic value by more than **$50
billion**."

**Investments.** **[[Apple]] is now Berkshire's largest holding** (255.3M shares, 5.4%, cost $36.0B /
market **$40.3B**); **[[Bank of America]]** built to 918.9M shares ($22.6B); plus Wells Fargo, Coca-Cola,
Amex, US Bancorp, Moody's, the airlines, Goldman, BNY Mellon, USG, Charter, and a **new [[JPMorgan Chase]]**
position ($5.0B). Total cost **$102.9B** / market **$172.8B**; the portfolio earns ~**20% on net tangible
equity** with little debt — "mind-blowing… compared against… 3% or less on 30-year U.S. Treasury bonds."
Berkshire bought **~$43B** of equities and sold only $19B in 2018, finding stocks cheaper than whole
companies ("prices are sky-high for businesses possessing decent long-term prospects") — though it still
hopes for an "elephant-sized acquisition."

**The American Tailwind.** The letter's capstone essay. Buffett recalls his **first investment on March
11, 1942** — $114.75 (3 shares of Cities Service preferred), age 11, amid wartime gloom. Had that $114.75
gone into a no-fee S&P 500 index fund with dividends reinvested, it would be worth **$606,811** by Jan 31,
2019 (**5,288-for-1**); a $1M tax-free institution would have **$5.3 billion** — but **only $2.65 billion**
after a mere 1%/yr of "helper" fees (11.8% → 10.8%). Over the same span the national debt rose **400-fold
(40,000%)**, yet gold-as-protection turned $114.75 into only ~$4,200: "**the magical metal was no match
for the American mettle.**" Prosperity came "in a bipartisan manner" (7 Republican + 7 Democratic
presidents); household wealth is now **$108 trillion**; the engine is **retained earnings / national
savings**. With humility ("the tidy rows of simple white crosses at Normandy should shame those" who claim
to have "done it alone"), Buffett credits **"The American Tailwind"** — "we are lucky — gloriously lucky —
to have that force at our back" (see [[The American Tailwind]]).

**Succession affirmed.** "Really good news… not reflected in our financial statements": the early-2018
elevation of **[[Ajit Jain]]** (insurance) and **[[Greg Abel]]** (everything else) means "**Berkshire is
now far better managed than when I alone was supervising operations.**"

## Key figures

- **GAAP net income $4.0B** = operating **$24.8B** (record) − $3.0B Kraft Heinz impairment + $2.8B
  realized gains − **$20.6B** unrealized-gain reduction (the new rule). **Book value +0.4%** (its last
  cover appearance); market +2.8%; S&P −4.4%. 1964–2018 gain: book 1,091,899%, market 2,472,627%.
- **Five groves:** non-insurance controlled (earned **$16.8B** after-tax); equities (~$173B); jointly-
  controlled quartet (~$1.3B); cash $112B + $20B fixed income ($20B floor); insurance/float.
- **Non-insurance pre-tax $20.8B (+24%)**; after-tax +47% (21% tax rate). Two "redwoods" **BNSF + BHE
  (90.9%)** earned **$9.3B pre-tax (+6%)**. Next five (Clayton, IMC, Lubrizol, Marmon, PCC) $6.4B; next
  five (Forest River, JM, MiTek, Shaw, TTI) $2.4B; rest $3.6B. Capex record **$14.5B**.
- **Insurance:** **+$2B underwriting profit** (15 of 16 years; 16-yr total $27B). **Float $122,732M**.
  Funding: equity **$349B**, float, deferred taxes **$50.5B**. **GEICO**: Nicely → **[[Bill Roberts]]**;
  #2 insurer, $15.5B pre-tax UW profit since 1996, float $2.5B→$22.1B.
- **Equities** (cost $102.9B / mkt **$172.8B**): **Apple now #1** ($40.3B, 5.4%), BofA $22.6B (9.5%), WFC
  $20.7B, Coke $18.9B, Amex $14.5B (17.9%), new **JPMorgan Chase** ($5.0B). Bought ~$43B / sold $19B.
- **American Tailwind:** $114.75 (1942) → **$606,811** in an index fund (5,288-for-1); gold → ~$4,200.
