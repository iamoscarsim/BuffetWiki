# 2019 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

The first letter with the **book-value column gone** from the cover table — now just Berkshire's
**market value (+11.0%)** vs. the **S&P 500 (+31.5%)**. Over 1965–2019 Berkshire compounded **20.3%** (vs.
the S&P's 10.0%); overall 1964–2019 gain **2,744,062%** (S&P 19,784%). GAAP net income was a headline-
grabbing **$81.4 billion** — but Buffett again dismantles it: **$24B operating earnings + $3.7B realized
gains + $53.7B from rising unrealized gains** (the [[Operating Earnings vs GAAP Net Income|mark-to-market rule]]). The contrast with 2018's $4B GAAP figure was "a crazy **1,900% increase**" with no economic
meaning. "**Focus on operating earnings… ignore both quarterly and annual gains or losses from
investments.**"

**The Power of Retained Earnings.** The letter's centerpiece essay. Buffett revives **Edgar Lawrence
Smith**'s 1924 *Common Stocks as Long Term Investments* — a book that "began… with a confession" (its
preconceived theory failed) and discovered that **retained earnings compound** ("the failure of facts to
sustain a preconceived theory"). He quotes Smith's early reviewer, **John Maynard Keynes**: well-managed
companies retain part of their profits, so "**there is an element of compound interest operating in favour
of a sound industrial investment**." Applied to Berkshire's stock holdings, this explains a "**standout
omission**": GAAP records only the **dividends** Berkshire receives, not its share of investees' far
larger **retained earnings**. A table of the **10 largest holdings** quantifies it — Berkshire's share:
**$3,798M of dividends vs. $8,332M of retained earnings** — and "from the group we will realize capital
gains at least equal to… the earnings… they retained. Mr. Smith got it right." (See [[The Power of Retained Earnings]], [[Look-Through Earnings]].)

**Non-insurance operations.** Quoting director **[[Tom Murphy]]**'s advice — "**To achieve a reputation as
a good manager, just be sure you buy good businesses**" — Buffett reviews his "uneven record" with the
**acquisitions-as-marriage** analogy. The redemptive mechanic: "poor" businesses stagnate and shrink as a
share of capital, while "good" ones grow and absorb more — so winners become an ever-larger portion (the
**[[Berkshire Hathaway Textiles|textile]]** original being the extreme). The **two "lead dogs," [[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]] and [[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]** (91% owned), earned a combined **$8.3B (+6%)**;
the next five (Clayton, IMC, Lubrizol, Marmon, PCC) **$4.8B**; the next five (Berkshire Hathaway
Automotive, Johns Manville, NetJets, Shaw, TTI) **$1.9B**; the rest **$2.7B** — total controlled
non-insurance net income **$17.7B (+3%)**. A wry aside: a September 2019 **fire at a French [[Lubrizol]]
plant** was insured in large part by… a Berkshire-owned insurer ("Let not the left hand know what the
right hand doeth").

**Insurance and float.** Float grew to **$129,423M**; Berkshire earned an underwriting profit in **16 of
the last 17 years** (17-year pre-tax gain **$27.5B**; **+$400M** in 2019). Buffett warns the **value of
float has fallen industry-wide** because low rates cut what bond-heavy insurers earn on it ("once… 5 or 6
cents on each dollar of float, they now take in only 2 or 3 cents") — Berkshire's edge is its flexibility
to invest float beyond high-grade bonds. The recurring "**The Big One**" mega-cat caveat repeats ("we will
most certainly not have an underwriting profit in 16 of the next 17 years"). A profile of **[[Guard Insurance|GUARD Insurance]]** (Wilkes-Barre): bought by [[Ajit Jain]] for $221M in late 2012, its premium
grew **379% to $1.9B** under CEO **Sy Foguel** — "Wilkes-Barre may well deliver a similar surprise" to
1967 Omaha.

**Berkshire Hathaway Energy at 20.** A milestone tour: Iowa residential rates (8.8¢/kWh in 2000) have
risen <1%/yr, with **no base-rate increases promised through 2028** — vs. another Iowa utility now charging
**61–70% more**. BHE will reach **wind self-sufficiency in Iowa by 2021** (25.2M MWh of wind vs. 24.6M MWh
of customer need) — drawing high-tech plants to the state. BHE (91% owned, with [[Walter Scott Jr]] and
[[Greg Abel]]) has **never paid Berkshire a dividend**, retaining **$28B** — and stands "ready, willing and
able" for utility projects "requiring investments of **$100 billion or more**."

**Investments.** The portfolio (cost $110.3B / market **$248.0B**) is now dominated by **[[Apple]]
($73.7B, 5.7%, by far #1)** and **[[Bank of America]] ($33.4B, 10.7%)**, then Coca-Cola, Amex (18.7%),
Wells Fargo, US Bancorp, JPMorgan, Moody's, Delta, BNY Mellon, Goldman, Southwest, United, a new
**[[Visa]]** stake, and — in "Others" — a **$10 billion [[Occidental Petroleum]]** preferred-plus-warrants
investment (financing its Anadarko purchase). The holdings earn **>20% on net tangible equity** with
little debt — "mind-blowing… compared to… 2½% or even less on 30-year U.S. Treasury bonds." Buffett's
forecast: if rates and tax rates stay low, "**equities will over time perform far better than long-term,
fixed-rate debt instruments**" — for the investor "who does not use borrowed money and who can control his
or her emotions. Others? Beware!" **[[Kraft Heinz]]** (equity-method): GAAP $13.8B but market only
**$10.5B** — now *below* its $9.8B cost.

**"The Road Ahead."** Buffett (89) and Munger (96) are "in the urgent zone," but "**your company is 100%
prepared for our departure**," resting on five factors (diverse high-return businesses; the single-entity
advantages; unbreakable financial strength; skilled devoted managers; owner-oriented directors). Buffett
discloses his **will**: 99% of his net worth is Berkshire stock, never to be sold; executors are directed
**not to sell**, converting A→B shares and distributing to foundations over **12–15 years** — a bet on
Berkshire's durability and its directors' "future judgment and fidelity."

**Boards of Directors.** A pointed governance essay drawing on Buffett's **21 public-company directorships
over 62 years**. The "bedrock challenge" is to find and keep an able, honest CEO. His critique of director
**"independence" theater**: soaring fees ($250–300k for a few days a year) make the **non-wealthy director**
(NWD) dependent on the income and on being invited onto a *second* board — so CEOs seek "cocker spaniels,"
not "pit bulls," and "the director for whom fees are important… is almost universally classified as
'independent'" while big owner-shareholders are deemed *not* independent. He prefers directors who bought
shares "with their savings" over those who only received grants. (See [[Corporate Governance]].)

**Short subjects.** Buybacks: Berkshire repurchased **$5 billion (~1% of the company)** in 2019 — "neither
of us feels any urgency to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real **95 cents**" (see [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]). Taxes: **$3.6B** sent to the Treasury, **1.5% of all U.S. corporate income tax**.
The 2020 annual meeting will give **[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]]** more exposure (fielding questions).
