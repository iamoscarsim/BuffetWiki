# 1996 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$6.2 billion (36.1%)**, but per-share book value rose **31.8%** (to
**$19,011**) because the share count increased — Berkshire issued stock for
[[FlightSafety International]] and sold new [[Class B Shares|Class B]] stock. Over 32
years book value compounded **23.8%** ($19 → $19,011). An accounting wrinkle opens the
letter: because **[[GEICO]] became a wholly-owned subsidiary on January 2, 1996** (a
"step acquisition"), rules forced Berkshire to **write down** its original 51% by
**$478.4M** — even though the stake's *economic* value rose. Buffett stresses, as
always, that **intrinsic value, not book value, is what counts**, and points readers to
the newly-published **Owner's Manual** (the economic principles, reproduced in full in
the report for the first time).

**The two-column intrinsic-value table.** For the second year, Buffett supplies a table
tracing two indices of Berkshire's value per share: **investments per share** (cash +
securities) and **pre-tax operating earnings per share, excluding all income from
investments**. In 1996 investments hit **$28,500/share (+29.0%)** and operating
earnings **$421.39/share (+63.2%)**. The goal is to push both columns ahead at a
"reasonable (or, better yet, unreasonable) pace," tempered by two realities: past growth
rates can't be matched (Berkshire's capital is now larger than all but ~10 U.S.
companies — "an abundance of funds tends to dampen returns"), and progress won't be
smooth (market swings move column one; super-cat profitability swings column two).
**Costs matter:** Berkshire's after-tax HQ expense is **under two basis points** of net
worth, vs. ~100 bp for equity mutual funds — "We are here to make money *with* you, not
*off* you."

**Intrinsic value vs. market price.** A year earlier, with the stock at $36,000, Buffett
warned its market gain had outstripped its (still-excellent) business gain and that he
didn't consider it undervalued. In 1996 intrinsic value rose sharply (largely on
GEICO) while the stock barely moved — so the stock **underperformed the business**, and
the price/value relationship is now "more appropriate." His fairness doctrine: managed
like a family partnership, "fairness prevails when market price and intrinsic value are
in sync"; the longer an owner holds, the more business results (not entry/exit premiums)
govern his experience. Berkshire "probably ranks number one among large American
corporations in the percentage of its shares held by owners with a long-term view."

**Two acquisitions.** **[[Kansas Bankers Surety]]** (KBS) — a bank-insurance specialist
in 22 states run by **[[Don Towle]]**, bought for **$75M** and placed with
**[[Wesco Financial|Wesco]]** — came via Buffett's serendipitous seatmate at a birthday
party (Roy Dinsdale, a KBS director). **[[FlightSafety International]]** — the world
leader in pilot training, run by 79-year-old founder **[[Al Ueltschi]]** — was far
larger, **~$1.5 billion**, sourced by a long-time shareholder (Richard Sercer) who saw
the fit and wrote to [[Salomon]]'s Bob Denham. The structure encouraged tax-indifferent
holders to take cash (51% cash, 41% Berkshire A, 8% B). FlightSafety is unusually
**capital-intensive** (175 simulators, some costing $19M). "It's difficult to teach a
new dog old tricks" — many Berkshire managers are past 70.

**Insurance & float.** A terrific year. With GEICO consolidated, **float vaulted to
$6,702.0M** (from $3,607M); it has grown **22.3%/yr since 1967** and, in a fourth
straight underwriting-profit year, again **cost less than zero**. In **super-cat**,
another lucky (loss-free) year: Berkshire wrote an Allstate Florida-hurricane contract
(then "the largest single catastrophe risk ever assumed by one company") and a ~**$1
billion** layer for the new **California Earthquake Authority** (effective 4/1/1997,
exposing more than twice the Florida loss). After-tax worst-case from a mega-cat is "no
more than **$600 million**" (<3% of book, 1.5% of market). The three super-cat edges —
certain payment, capacity reserved for long-standing clients, and unmatched limits —
recur; computer models' "precision … is a chimera" (echoing "portfolio insurance").
Pricing aims for ~90% of premiums to be paid out eventually. Charlie's inversion: "All I
want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there."

**GEICO's stunning year.** Voluntary auto policies grew **10%** (best-ever prior year was
8%, hit once) and accelerated, led by the nonstandard market; underwriting targets were
beaten. The strategy is not to widen margins but to **enlarge the price advantage**
(the **virtuous circle**: low cost → low price → retention → 1M+ referrals/yr ≈ half of
new business → lower acquisition cost). A focused **compensation plan** ties bonuses to
just two variables — growth in voluntary auto policies and underwriting profit on
"seasoned" business — and shuns "lottery-ticket" options; the profit-sharing payout hit
a record **16.9% ($40M)**. **[[Lou Simpson]]** beat the S&P by 6.2 points. Buffett
recalls GEICO was valued at **$7M** when he found it in 1951; Berkshire paid **$2.3B**
for the last 49% in 1995. The smaller insurers shone too: **[[National Indemnity Company|National Indemnity]]** combined ratio **74.2** (Don Wurster), homestate **87.1**
(Rod Eldred), workers' comp (Brad Kinstler, now in six more states), Central States
(John Kizer), plus KBS — "an underwriting record virtually unmatched in the industry."

**Taxes.** Berkshire mailed **$860 million** in federal income tax — "if an equal amount
had been paid by only 2,000 other taxpayers, the government would have had a balanced
budget in 1996." Buffett deems large tax payments fitting: "Berkshire prospers in
America as it would nowhere else."

**The Inevitables.** The conceptual centerpiece. **[[Coca-Cola]]** and **[[Gillette]]**
are labeled **"The Inevitables"** — companies certain to "dominate their fields
worldwide for an investment lifetime." For every Inevitable "there are dozens of
**Impostors**"; to the Inevitables Berkshire adds a few **"Highly Probables."** Buffett
favors businesses "unlikely to experience major change" ("We applaud the endeavor but
prefer to skip the ride"); warns that even great businesses can be overpaid for (overpay
risk "may now be quite high"), and that the deadliest danger is a great company **losing
focus** (Coke once grew shrimp; Gillette explored for oil). Inactivity is intelligent
("we continue to make more money when snoring than when active"); Michael Jordan
shouldn't be traded just because he dominates the team. For most investors, a **low-cost
index fund** is the best vehicle; for stock-pickers, only two courses matter — "How to
Value a Business" and "How to Think About Market Prices" — and "If you aren't willing to
own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes."

**USAir — a reprieve.** The [[USAir]] preferred, written down 75% to $89.5M in 1994 and
offered for sale at a loss, recovered: under CEO Stephen Wolf the airline turned
profitable in H2 1996 and paid **$47.9M** of arrears (the contract's prime+5% "penalty
dividends" had compounded the unpaid amounts at 13.25–14%). The preferred is now worth
roughly its **$358M par**; Berkshire has collected $240.5M of dividends (incl. $30M in
1997). Buffett's verdict on his own analysis: "superficial and wrong" — "I again failed
in my attempt to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory."

**Financings & misc.** Four checks to [[Salomon]], all worth it: the FlightSafety deal,
a small finance-sub debt placement, the **May Class B sale** (517,500 shares, **$565M**
net, a record-low **1.5% commission**, open-ended to repel flippers — ~40,000 new
holders, doubling the shareholder count), and a **$500M Salomon-exchangeable note**
issue ($447.1M proceeds; 3% yield, ~1.1% if exchanged into Salomon stock). Berkshire
"decided to enter the 20th Century": reports will now be posted at
**berkshirehathaway.com**. SDC reached **$13.3M** (97.2%, 3,910 charities). [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] is **103**; NFM did a record **$265M**. The meeting moves to the
Aksarben Coliseum (capacity ~10,000) after 5,000 attended in 1995.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $19,011, per-share **+31.8%** (net worth **+36.1%, +$6.2B**);
  32 years, 23.8% compounded. Stock ~$36,000 a year earlier (now "more appropriate").
- **Two-column table:** investments **$28,500/share** (+29.0%); pre-tax operating
  earnings (ex-investment income) **$421.39/share** (+63.2%). Long-run rates since 1965:
  33.4% (investments) and 14.7% (operating earnings).
- **GEICO step-acquisition writedown:** $478.4M (rules forced a markdown of the original
  51% on moving to 100%).
- **Insurance:** **4th straight underwriting profit** ($222.1M pre-tax / $142.8M to
  Berkshire); average float **$6,702.0M** at negative cost (22.3%/yr growth since 1967).
  NICO traditional CR **74.2**; homestate **87.1**.
- **GEICO:** voluntary auto policies **+10%**; profit-sharing **16.9% ($40M)**; valued
  at $7M in 1951 vs. $2.3B paid for the last 49% in 1995.
- **Super-cat:** ~$1B California Earthquake Authority layer (effective 4/1/1997);
  worst-case after-tax mega-cat loss **~$600M** (<3% book, 1.5% market).
- **Operating earnings:** $1,221.4M pre-tax / $883.1M to Berkshire (vs. $814.7M / $600.2M
  in 1995). **Total earnings** $3,705.9M / $2,488.6M (incl. $1,605.5M securities gains).
- **Look-through earnings:** **$1,522M** (operating $954M + $661M investee retained −
  $93M hypothetical tax at 14%).
- **Common stocks:** total cost $7,910.2M / **market $27,750.6M**. Coca-Cola $10,525M
  (200M sh post-split), Gillette $3,732M, Amex $2,794.3M (10.5%), Wells Fargo $1,966.9M,
  Freddie Mac $1,772.8M, Disney $1,716.8M (24.6M sh), McDonald's $1,368.4M (new), WaPo
  $579M (15.8%).
- **Acquisitions:** Kansas Bankers Surety ($75M, → Wesco); FlightSafety International
  (~$1.5B; 51% cash / 41% A / 8% B).
- **Taxes:** $860M federal income tax. **SDC:** $13.3M; 97.2%; 3,910 charities.
