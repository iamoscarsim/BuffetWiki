# 2020 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

The COVID-year letter. GAAP net earnings were **$42.5 billion**, broken into four parts: **$21.9B
operating earnings** (down **9%** as the pandemic bit), **$4.9B realized gains**, a **$26.7B** rise in
unrealized gains (the [[Operating Earnings vs GAAP Net Income|mark-to-market]] swing), and an **$11
billion write-down** — "almost entirely the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016." Berkshire's
market return was **+2.4%** (vs. the S&P's +18.4%); over 1965–2020 it compounded **20.0%** (S&P 10.2%);
overall 1964–2020 gain **2,810,526%**. Berkshire made **no sizable acquisitions** but lifted per-share
intrinsic value by **retaining earnings and repurchasing ~5% of its shares**. Marketable stocks ended the
year worth **$281 billion**, and Buffett restates the [[The Power of Retained Earnings|retained-earnings]]
case ("What worked for Carnegie and Rockefeller has… worked its magic for millions of shareholders").

**The Precision Castparts mea culpa.** The $11B write-down was **[[Precision Castparts|PCC]]**: "I paid
too much… I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential," exposed by the aerospace
downturn. "No one misled me… **PCC is far from my first error of that sort. But it's a big one.**" (See
[[Mistakes Ledger]].) CEO **[[Mark Donegan]]** is praised; the misjudgment was Buffett's price, not the
business.

**"Two Strings to Our Bow" — the conglomerate, redefined.** Buffett embraces the "conglomerate" label only
"in part." Classic conglomerates failed because great businesses wouldn't sell, so dealmakers chased
mediocre ones and overpaid — often manufacturing an overvalued stock as acquisition "currency" ("I'll pay
you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats") via "promotional techniques and
'imaginative' accounting." Berkshire instead owns a diverse group of good businesses **whether controlled
or not** — his "20-year struggle with the textile operation" taught him that "owning a non-controlling
portion of a **wonderful** business is more profitable… and far less work than struggling with 100% of a
marginal enterprise." "You are awarded no points in business endeavors for 'degree of difficulty.'" (See
[[The Berkshire System]].)

**The Family Jewels — the "Big Four."** Most of Berkshire's value sits in four businesses: (1) the **P/C
insurance** operation (largest; [[Ajit Jain]]); (2) **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]]** and (3)
**[[Apple]]** (a "toss-up" for 2nd/3rd — Berkshire's **5.4%** stake); and (4) 91%-owned **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]**. **Insurance float reached $138 billion**, deployed in an "equity-heavy" strategy that the
fortress balance sheet uniquely permits — crucial because "**bonds are not the place to be these days**"
(the 10-year Treasury yielded **0.93%**, down 94% from 15.8% in 1981; trillions of sovereign debt at
negative yields). Reaching for yield in "shaky" credit is "not the answer" (the S&L collapse the warning).

**The power of repurchases.** Berkshire bought back a **record $24.7 billion** in 2020 (80,998 A-share
equivalents, **5.2% of the company**) — "increased your ownership in all of Berkshire's businesses by 5.2%
without requiring you to so much as touch your wallet" — buying "**exactly the reverse**" of CEOs who buy
more when prices rise. The **[[Apple]] illustration**: bought late-2016 to mid-2018 for **$36B (a 5.2%
stake)**; ~$775M/yr of dividends; in 2020 Berkshire **sold a small slice for an $11B gain**, yet — thanks
to Apple's own buybacks — **still owns 5.4%** ("costless"). Combined with Berkshire's repurchases,
shareholders "**indirectly own a full 10% more of Apple**" than in July 2018. "Too much of a good thing
can be… wonderful" (Mae West). (See [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]].)

**Investments.** The portfolio (cost $108.6B / market **$281.2B**) shows large 2020 turnover: **[[Apple]]
$120.4B (5.4%)** dwarfs the rest; **[[Bank of America]]** added to ($31.3B, 11.9%); new positions in
**[[Verizon Communications|Verizon]]** ($8.6B), **[[Chevron]]** ($4.1B), Japan's **[[Japanese Trading Houses|Itochu]]** ($2.3B, 5.1%), and a **pharma basket** (AbbVie, Merck — plus Bristol-Myers and Pfizer in
"Others"). Conspicuously **absent: all four airlines** (sold during the spring-2020 crash at a loss; see
[[Evolving Views — Airlines]]) and most of the bank book (Wells Fargo, JPMorgan, Goldman exited). The
**$10B [[Occidental Petroleum]]** preferred (now valued $9B) sits in "Others." **[[Kraft Heinz]]**:
equity-method GAAP $13.3B, market only **$11.3B**.

**"A Tale of Two Cities" (and Omaha and Knoxville).** A founders'-stories essay: **[[See's Candies]]**
(Mary See, 1972) and **[[GEICO]]** (Leo and Lillian Goodwin's $100,000 start in 1936; $238,288 of business
in 1937 → **$35 billion** in 2020); then Omaha's **[[National Indemnity Company]]** (Jack Ringwalt's
$125,000 start in 1940; the **1967 15-minute handshake, no audit**) and **[[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]**
(Rose Blumkin's $2,500 start; net worth $72,264 and $50 of cash in 1946; son Louie's Omaha-Beach/Bulge war
service; the 1983 80% purchase; record 2020 sales despite a six-week COVID closure; "**God Bless
America**"); and Knoxville's **[[Clayton Homes]]** and **[[Pilot Flying J|Pilot]]** (Jim/Kevin Clayton and
"Big Jim"/Jimmy Haslam — each now **>$1B pre-tax**, ~47,000 employees together). The refrain: "**Never bet
against America.**"

**"The Berkshire Partnership."** Buffett recounts the partnership era (BPL from 1956; the 12-partnership
amalgamation in 1962; Munger's 1962 partnership; BPL's 1969 dissolution distributing its **70.5%** of
Berkshire; the 1983 Blue Chip merger) and the founding creed — "**Although our form is corporate, our
attitude is partnership.**" He describes the **five "buckets"** of Berkshire ownership (himself as
"founder"; two institutional buckets — index funds and active managers; active individuals; and his
treasured fifth, the **million-plus long-term individual holders**), illustrated by 100-year-old **Stan
Truhlsen** and the 1959 "Emdee, Ltd." doctors' partnership. **Phil Fisher**'s restaurant analogy returns —
Berkshire has "been serving hamburgers and Coke for 56 years" — alongside the dart-throwing monkey and the
warning that "your expenses are Wall Street's income" (see [[Owner Orientation and Candor]]).

**A surprising number, and the two asset-heavy giants.** Berkshire owns the **most U.S. property, plant
and equipment of any American company — $154 billion** (next: AT&T at $127B). The lesson is not triumph
(asset-light is better) but that **asset-heavy businesses can still be good investments**: **BNSF + BHE**
earned a combined **$4.2B in 2011 → $8.3B in 2020**. **BNSF** (~15% of U.S. non-local ton-miles) has
invested **$41B** in fixed assets since 2010 ($20B over depreciation), 23,000 miles across 28 states, and
paid Berkshire **$41.8B of dividends**; **[[Katie Farmer]] succeeded the retiring [[Carl Ice]] as CEO** at
year-end (Ice and Farmer lifted margins 2.9 points despite a 7% volume drop). **BHE** pays no dividend
(21 years: earnings **$122M → $3.4B**) and is mid-way through an **$18 billion Western transmission-grid**
rebuild (2006 → 2030) to carry remote wind/solar power — "BHE will be a leader in delivering ever-cleaner
energy."

**Annual meeting.** The 2020 meeting was virtual (Greg Abel beside Buffett in an empty Omaha arena); the
2021 meeting would be held in **Los Angeles, with Munger back on stage** plus Abel and Jain. Buffett is
90, Munger 97.

## Key figures

- **Market value +2.4%** (S&P +18.4%); 1965–2020 compounded **20.0%**; 1964–2020 gain **2,810,526%**.
  GAAP net **$42.5B** = operating **$21.9B** (−9%) + $4.9B realized + $26.7B unrealized − **$11B PCC
  write-down**.
- **Record buybacks $24.7B (5.2% of shares).** Apple: sold a slice for an $11B gain, still owns **5.4%**;
  holders indirectly own ~10% more of Apple than in mid-2018.
- **Insurance float $138B**; "Big Four" = insurance, BNSF, Apple, BHE. **BNSF + BHE** earned **$8.3B**
  (vs. $4.2B in 2011). Berkshire owns **$154B** of U.S. PP&E (most of any U.S. company).
- **Equities** (cost $108.6B / mkt **$281.2B**): **Apple $120.4B (5.4%)**, BofA $31.3B (11.9%), Coke
  $21.9B, Amex $18.3B (18.8%), Verizon $8.6B (new), Moody's $7.2B, US Bancorp $6.9B, BYD $5.9B, Chevron
  $4.1B (new), Charter $3.4B, AbbVie/Merck/Itochu (new). **Sold all airlines & most banks.** Kraft Heinz
  mkt $11.3B.
- **BNSF:** ~15% of U.S. non-local ton-miles; $41B fixed-asset investment since 2010; **[[Carl Ice]]→
  [[Katie Farmer]]**. **BHE:** $18B Western grid project (→2030); no dividend; $122M → $3.4B over 21 yrs.
