# 1977 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

The earliest letter in this collection, written March 14, 1978, and the first in
the recognizably modern Buffett style. Berkshire reported operating earnings of
$21.9 million ($22.54 per share), a 19% return on beginning equity capital —
"slightly better than last year and above both our own long-term average and that
of American industry in aggregate." Buffett immediately frames his central
yardstick: not earnings per share (which a "totally dormant savings account" will
grow through compounding) but **[[Return on Equity]]**. He warns that per-share
gains look less impressive once you note beginning capital rose 24%.

The letter tours Berkshire's four operating areas of the era. **Textiles** ([[Berkshire Hathaway Textiles]])
again had a poor year despite two straight years of mistaken optimism; Buffett
defends staying in partly out of loyalty to employees and to [[Ken Chace]]'s
management, while conceding the business is unlikely to earn comparable returns —
an early statement of the "tailwinds vs. headwinds" lesson. **Insurance underwriting**,
led by [[Phil Liesche]] at [[National Indemnity Company]], delivered results
"even better than our optimistic expectations." Buffett recounts entering
insurance in 1967 by buying National Indemnity for ~$8.6 million and growing
premium volume from $22 million to $151 million with no new Berkshire shares
issued. He lays out the cyclical nature of underwriting and the discipline
required to *accept reduced volume* when rates turn inadequate. **Banking** at the
[[Illinois National Bank]] under 80-year-old [[Gene Abegg]] earned ~3x the return
on assets of most large banks. And **[[Blue Chip Stamps]]** (~36.5% owned)
contributed its share of [[See's Candies]] and [[Wesco Financial]].

The investing philosophy is already fully formed. Buffett states the four
**[[Acquisition Criteria]]**: a business (1) we can understand, (2) with favorable
long-term prospects, (3) run by honest and competent people, (4) available at a
very attractive price — applied identically to whole companies and to marketable
stocks. He articulates **[[Stocks as Fractional Ownership]]** ("we select our
marketable equity securities in much the same way we would evaluate a business for
acquisition in its entirety") and the willingness to welcome lower prices on good
businesses. The [[Washington Post]] and [[Capital Cities]] positions illustrate
buying outstanding businesses through the market at "very large discounts" from
negotiated value, and Buffett's striking claim that with Cap Cities "we can obtain
a better management result through non-control than control."

## Key figures
- Operating earnings: **$21,904,000** ($22.54/share), up 37% per share.
- Return on beginning equity: **19%**.
- Insurance premium volume: **$151 million** (from $22M in 1967, ~600% growth).
- Net investment income (Insurance Group): $12.3M pre-tax (from $8.4M in 1975).
- Insurance equity holdings: cost $106.9M, market $181.1M; unrealized gain ~$74M.
- See's pre-tax operating earnings: **$12.6 million** (from $4.2M at 1972 purchase).
- Illinois National Bank earnings: $3.6 million.
- Blue Chip Stamps: ~$12.9M operating earnings; Berkshire owned ~36.5%.
