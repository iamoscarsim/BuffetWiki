# 1986 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written February 1987. Net worth grew **$492.5M (+26.1%)**, lifting book value to
**$2,073.06** (23.3% compounded over 22 years; corporate net worth up 10,600% while
shares outstanding rose less than 1%). Buffett notes that [[Intrinsic Business Value]]
"probably" rose even more than book in 1986, because his managers — the Blumkins,
[[Mike Goldberg]], the Heldmans, [[Chuck Huggins]], Stan Lipsey, [[Ralph Schey]] —
built [[Economic Goodwill]] that never shows on the balance sheet. But he is hard on
himself: "my performance did not match that of our managers." He frames his and
Munger's role as **two jobs** — attract and keep outstanding managers, and allocate
capital (see [[Capital Allocation]], [[Decentralization]]). On the first job, the
David Ogilvy line: hire people bigger than you and become "a company of giants";
managing stars is like managing a golf team with Nicklaus and Palmer — "our main
contribution has been to not get in their way." On the second, 1986 was "tough
work": no new marketable equities met Berkshire's tests, so the main moves were
paying off debt and stockpiling cash.

**One acquisition: [[Fechheimer Brothers]]** — a Cincinnati uniform maker (founded
1842), bought ~84% at a $55M valuation after Chairman Bob Heldman, a longtime
shareholder, read the annual-report "want ad" and wrote in. Buffett re-runs the ad
verbatim (six criteria; minimum after-tax earnings raised from $5M to **$10M**) and
notes Berkshire had never visited Cincinnati — just as [[Chuck Huggins]] had never
visited Omaha — because the evaluation is of economics and people, not plants.

Operating businesses thrived: the **[[Buffalo Evening News]]** (Stan Lipsey; ten
years owned; 50% news hole; #1 weekday penetration of the top 50 U.S. papers; Sunday
penetration 83% vs. the old Courier-Express's 63%); **[[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]**
(Mrs. B at **93**, "outsell and out-hustle any manager I've ever seen"; sales +10.2%
to $132M — "file this fact away... before you mark your ballot at the 2024 annual
meeting"); **[[See's Candies]]** ($30.3M pre-tax; poundage +2%; Christmas stemmed the
same-store decline); and the two **[[Scott Fetzer]]** standouts, **[[World Book]]**
(encyclopedia units +7%, +45% over 1982) and **[[Kirby]]** (units +4%, +33% over
1982).

**Insurance** improved sharply: Berkshire's combined ratio fell 111 → **103**, and
it was likely the fastest-growing of the country's top 100 insurers over two years
(NICO's monthly volume swung from $5M to $35M and back toward $20M as rivals
returned). Buffett restates the **commodity** nature of insurance pricing — "what's
needed is not more statesmen, but less corn" — and names *social and judicial
inflation* as the real cost driver, arguing premiums must grow ~10%/yr just to hold
profitability. He also confesses a **third straight year of under-reserving**: "If
the physiological rules that applied to Pinocchio were to apply to me, my nose would
now draw crowds" (see [[Loss Reserving]]). **[[GEICO]]** (41%-owned) had an
outstanding year — combined ratio 96.9, premium +16%, share count down 5.5% — and
Buffett introduces the **moat** metaphor: GEICO's rock-bottom cost is "a kind of moat
that protects a valuable... business castle," which Chairman **[[Bill Snyder]]**
keeps widening (see [[Economic Moat]]). He prints [[Lou Simpson]]'s seven-year equity
record vs. the S&P 500 — admitting "it's a little embarrassing for me... to chronicle
Lou's performance."

On **marketable securities**, Buffett finds nothing to buy: ~$700M of merely
"mediocre" tax-exempt bonds, plus arbitrage (Lear-Siegler, Chesebrough-Ponds) as a
T-bill alternative. He states his enduring temperament — "be fearful when others are
greedy and... greedy only when others are fearful" — amid Wall Street euphoria, and
reaffirms three **permanent** holdings he'd never sell ([[Capital Cities]]/ABC,
[[GEICO]], [[Washington Post]]), mocking the "institutional investor" oxymoron and
the restructuring fad (see [[Permanent Ownership]]). A long **Taxation** essay
dissects the Tax Reform Act of 1986 (corporate ordinary rate 46% → 34%, but corporate
cap-gains 28% → 34%, and a heavier tax on insurers' investment income): in
franchise/strong businesses a rate cut accrues to owners, in price-competitive ones it
passes to customers — the brain-surgeon's fee won't fall just because tax rates do.
He also bought a used corporate jet for $850,000, mocking himself with Ben Franklin
("So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature...").

The letter's centerpiece is its **Appendix on "owner earnings"** (see
[[Owner Earnings]]): using Scott Fetzer's "old" vs. "new" GAAP statements (identical
economics, different accounting after $142.6M of purchase-price write-ups), Buffett
defines owner earnings as **(a) reported earnings + (b) depreciation/amortization and
other non-cash charges − (c) the capex required to maintain competitive position and
unit volume**, and demolishes the Wall Street "cash flow" figure that adds (b) but
ignores (c). "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong."

## Key figures
- Net worth +$492.5M (**+26.1%**); book value/share **$2,073.06** (22 yrs, 23.3%).
- Operating earnings $195.9M pre-tax / $131.5M to Berkshire (vs. $125.4M / $92.9M).
- Berkshire combined ratio (statutory, ex-structured settlements/financial reins.)
  **103** (down from 111). NICO monthly volume peaked ~$35M (Q1 1986), back to ~$20M.
- **[[GEICO]]** 41%-owned: combined ratio **96.9**, premium +16%; market value of
  6.85M shares **$674.7M** (cost $45.7M); Berkshire's share of GEICO premium >$500M.
- Total marketable equities: cost $655.4M / **market $1,874.0M**. Cap Cities/ABC 2.99M
  sh, market **$801.7M** (cost $515.8M); Washington Post market **$269.5M** (cost $9.7M).
- WPPSS bonds: amortized cost **$218M**, market **$310M**, $31.7M annual tax-exempt income.
- **[[Fechheimer Brothers]]**: ~84% bought at a $55M whole-business valuation (closed 6/3/86).
- NHP, Inc.: ~50% bought in 1985 for **$23.7M** (multi-family housing).
- ~$700M of tax-exempt bonds purchased; corporate jet bought used for $850,000.
