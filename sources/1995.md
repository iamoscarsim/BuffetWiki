# 1995 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth surged **$5.3 billion (45.0%)** — per-share book value up **43.1%** (stock issued
for two acquisitions added 1.3% to shares) — lifting book value to **$14,426** (23.6%
compounded over 31 years). Buffett deflates it immediately: "any fool could make a bundle in
the stock market [in 1995]. And we did… a rising tide lifts all yachts." The year's real news
is **three acquisitions "of exactly the type we desire"** — **[[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]]**
and **[[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]** (both in the 1995 statements) and the **purchase of
the 50% of [[GEICO]] Berkshire didn't own** (closed right after year-end) — which roughly
**double Berkshire's revenues** while barely moving share count or debt; HQ staff grew 11 →
12. The "double-barrelled" strategy (buy whole businesses *and* part-interests via the stock
market) is celebrated with Woody Allen ("the real advantage of being bisexual is that it
doubles your chances for a date on Saturday night"). A ten-year-interval table shows
per-share marketable securities growing 33.4%/yr (to $22,088) and per-share operating
earnings 14.7%/yr (to $258.20) since 1965; the standing disadvantage is **size** ("now we
need good *big* ideas").

**Acquisitions essay.** Despite making three deals, Buffett restates his skepticism: most
deals harm the acquirer (*HMS Pinafore*: "skim milk masquerades as cream"; the limping horse
peddled as Secretariat; Peter Drucker: "dealmaking beats working… that's why you have deals
that make no sense"; the consultant's diversification that took a company from "100%… to
150% of our earnings"). Berkshire's edges: **no strategic plan**, a habit of comparing every
deal against passive stock alternatives, and a **stock backed by an extraordinary collection
of businesses** that lets a seller defer taxes and trust the "buying to keep" promise.

**Helzberg's & R.C. Willey ("ABWA — acquisitions by walking around").** [[Barnett Helzberg]]
Jr. (owner of four Berkshire shares) stopped Buffett on a NYC street corner; the resulting
deal brought in **[[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]]** (founded 1915; 134 stores, 23 states; sales
$10M→$53M→$282M over two decades) via a tax-free stock exchange, run by CEO **[[Jeff Comment]]** — "we would not have bought the business if Jeff had not been there… buying a
retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator."
**[[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]** (Utah's leading home-furnishings chain, >50% market
share, $257M sales) came via Irv Blumkin of [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]], run by CEO **[[Bill Child]]** (who'd grown it from $250K of sales in 1954). The retail lesson: it is a
"have-to-be-smart-**every-day**" business (a sharp contrast to a "have-to-be-smart-**once**"
network TV station, where even "a shiftless and backward nephew" could prosper — though "you'd
do far better… if you put in [[Tom Murphy]]"). "In retailing, to coast is to fail."

**GEICO — the whole company.** Berkshire agreed to pay **$2.3 billion** for the half of
[[GEICO]] it didn't own, completing a **45-year arc**: Buffett discovered GEICO in 1951 as Ben
Graham's student (Graham was chairman), took a Saturday train to its closed DC headquarters,
and got a four-hour tutorial from **[[Lorimer Davidson]]** ("Davy") on the **direct-marketing
cost advantage** that "gave it an enormous cost advantage." He bought 350 shares for $10,282
(>65% of his net worth), sold in 1952 for $15,259 — a stake that grew to ~$1.3M — "a lesson
about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company." Berkshire
bought ~33% in 1976 (backing [[Jack Byrne]]) and rode buybacks to ~50%. Now full ownership
brings two "extraordinary managers": **[[Tony Nicely]]** (insurance) and **[[Lou Simpson]]**
(investments — 22.8%/yr vs. 15.7% for the S&P over 1980–95, and a ready successor-investor
for Berkshire). GEICO's expense ratio fell to **23.6%** — "GEICO's moat widened in 1995."
Davy, now 93, "warmly supported the transaction" though it cost him a large tax.

**Insurance & float.** A **third straight underwriting-profit year**; float grew to
**$3,607.2M** at negative cost (compounding 20.7%/yr since 1967). The new **float-as-equity**
framing: cost-free float "has in effect served as equity" but without dilution — replacing
$3.4B of float with equity would mean "more shares, equal assets and lower earnings." GEICO
adds ~$3B of float. Super-cat had a third lucky year (Opal abated; Kobe was huge but barely
insured) — "the results of years like the past three will be… offset by some truly terrible
year in the future." **[[Ajit Jain]]**, "the guiding genius," also wrote single-risk deals:
**Mike Tyson's life**, **Lloyd's** against >225 of its "names" dying, and the launch+orbit of
**two Chinese satellites**. Berkshire offered a **$1 billion** policy (largest *accepted*:
$400M). "We prefer a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%." A bad reinsurance contract "is like hell:
easy to enter and impossible to exit" — the early-1970s mistakes still bill Berkshire 20+
years on. The smaller units shone: National Indemnity combined ratio **84.2** (Don Wurster),
homestate **81.4** (Rod Eldred), California workers' comp profitable despite walking from
underpriced renewals (Brad Kinstler), Central States premium +23% (John Kizer).

**Common stocks, Disney, and the preferreds.** "Rip Van Winkle" mode — five of the six top
positions untouched; only **[[American Express]]** increased (to ~10%, 49.46M shares, $2.05B).
Itemized over-$600M holdings: Coca-Cola ($7.43B), Cap Cities/ABC ($2.47B), Gillette ($2.50B,
now 48M shares post-split), GEICO ($2.39B), American Express, Wells Fargo ($1.47B), Freddie
Mac ($1.04B). In early 1996, GEICO became wholly owned and Berkshire exchanged its Cap Cities
shares for cash + **[[Disney]]** stock (electing stock for its 20M shares; also buying Disney
in the market) — "happy to be once again a large owner" of Disney, which Buffett had owned in
the 1960s (bought at a split-adjusted 31¢, sold at 48¢, now $66). The **convertible-preferred
post-mortem**: ex-[[Gillette]] the five preferreds returned no more than the fixed-income
issues they replaced. Gillette was "our best holding" *and* "my **biggest mistake**" — taking
the preferred instead of common cost ~$625M of forgone gain. [[Champion International]] was
called (converted, sold to the company; ~19% after tax); [[First Empire State]] will be
called 3/31/96 (convert and keep, backing [[Bob Wilmers]]); [[Salomon]]'s conversion option
"must be graded as very poor" (with $140M redeemed on schedule); [[USAir]] improved (dividends
accruing at prime+5% since June 1994; carried at 60% of par).

**The Class B recapitalization.** Berkshire proposes a new **Class B share** worth **1/30** of
a Class A in economics but **1/200** of a vote and no SDC eligibility; A converts to 30 B (not
the reverse), with a public offering of ≥$100M to list on the NYSE. The motive: to **thwart
expense-laden unit-trust "clones"** being marketed to unsophisticated buyers on Berkshire's
record. Buffett is blunt that the offering won't dilute intrinsic value because the stock
isn't cheap — at $36,000, "**Berkshire is selling at a price at which Charlie and I would not
consider buying it**." The market cap is $43B; the goal is to double per-share intrinsic value
every five years (and the stock has lately "outperformed the business").

**Miscellaneous.** Buffett recounts brokering the **Disney/Cap Cities** merger at Sun Valley
(July 14, 1995) when he, Michael Eisner, and [[Tom Murphy]] crossed paths — and delivers a
glowing **Murphy tribute** ("as fine an executive as I have ever seen… if Murph should elect
to run another business, don't bother to study its value — just buy the stock"), pinned to his
own regret at selling Cap Cities too early ("$635 million versus the $1.27 billion those shares
would bring in the Disney merger"). Three businesses had **declining earnings**: the Shoe
Group (cyclical), the [[Buffalo Evening News]] (newspapers "lost still another notch"), and
[[World Book]] (CD-ROM/online competition). SDC reached **$11.6M** (96.3%, 3,600 charities).
[[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] is **102** and saved the downtown "Rose" theater. No key manager left
Berkshire in 1965–95.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $14,426, per-share **+43.1%** (net worth **+45.0%, +$5.3B**); 23.6%
  compounded over 31 years. Market cap $43B.
- **GEICO:** $2.3B paid for the remaining ~50% (7th-largest U.S. auto insurer, 3.7M cars);
  expense ratio 23.6%; adds ~$3B float.
- **Insurance:** **3rd straight underwriting profit**; average float **$3,607.2M** at negative
  cost (20.7%/yr growth since 1967). NICO combined ratio 84.2; homestate 81.4.
- **Look-through/operating:** operating earnings $814.7M pre-tax / $600.2M to Berkshire;
  total earnings $1,008.8M / $725.2M.
- **Common stocks:** total cost $5,745.1M / market **$22,000.3M**. Coca-Cola $7,425M; Cap
  Cities $2,467.5M; Gillette $2,502M (48M sh); GEICO $2,393.2M; American Express $2,046.3M
  (~10%); Wells Fargo $1,466.9M; Freddie Mac $1,044M.
- **Class B shares:** 1/30 economics, 1/200 vote; ≥$100M public offering planned. Berkshire
  stock at $36,000 ("would not consider buying it").
- **Gillette "biggest mistake":** taking preferred over common cost ~$625M (less ~$70M excess
  dividends).
- **SDC:** $11.6M; 96.3%; 3,600 charities.
