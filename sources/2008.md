# 2008 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth **fell $11.5 billion**, cutting per-share book value **9.6%** to **$70,530** — the
**worst year** for both Berkshire and the S&P 500 (−37.0%) in the 44-year table, though Berkshire
still beat the index by 27.4 points. Written into the teeth of the **2008 financial crisis**, the
letter opens with the credit market turning "non-functional," a "paralyzing fear that engulfed the
country," and a "freefall in business activity… at a pace that I have never before witnessed." The
Treasury and Fed had "gone 'all in'"; "economic medicine… previously meted out by the cupful has
recently been dispensed by the barrel," with "an onslaught of inflation" a likely aftereffect. Yet
Buffett closes the macro section on his enduring optimism: America overcame two world wars, a dozen
panics, a 21½% prime rate and the Depression while real living standards rose ~7-fold and the Dow
went 66 → 11,497. "**America's best days lie ahead.**" The four standing goals: a **Gibraltar-like
financial position**; widening operating **moats**; new earnings streams; and nurturing managers.

**The crisis as opportunity.** "**When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy.**"
The market's disarray gave Berkshire "a tailwind in our purchases." It made **three large
investments on terms unavailable in normal markets** — **$14.5 billion** of fixed-income securities
issued by **[[Wrigley]]**, **[[Goldman Sachs]]** and **[[General Electric]]** (high current yields
*plus* an equity participation "as a bonus") — expected to add ~$1.5B pre-tax annually. To fund
them, Buffett sold parts of [[Johnson & Johnson]], [[Procter & Gamble]] and [[ConocoPhillips]] he'd
have preferred to keep, honoring his pledge to "always run Berkshire with more than ample cash… We
never want to count on the kindness of strangers." Ben Graham: "**Price is what you pay; value is
what you get.**"

**Confessions.** "During 2008 I did some dumb things in investments." The **major mistake of
commission**: buying "a large amount of **[[ConocoPhillips]]** stock when oil and gas prices were
near their peak," costing Berkshire "several billion dollars" ("so far I have been dead wrong"). A
smaller one: **two Irish banks** bought for **$244 million**, written down to **$27 million** (an
89% loss) by year-end — "unforced errors." Plus errors of omission, "sucking my thumb."

**Insurance.** A **sixth consecutive underwriting gain**: the **$58.5B of float cost less than zero**
— Berkshire was **paid $2.8 billion** to hold it. Underwriting profit **$2,792M** ([[General Re]]
$342M, [[Ajit Jain|BH Reinsurance]] $1,324M, [[GEICO]] $916M, Other Primary $210M); float
**$58,488M**. **[[GEICO]]** (Tony Nicely's 48th year) lifted share **7.2% → 7.7%**, rising to the
**#3** auto insurer (behind only State Farm and Allstate; #7 when Berkshire bought control in 1995);
policies-per-employee 299 → 439; motorcycle +23.4%; commercial auto entered. "Tony and I feel like
two hungry mosquitoes in a nudist camp." [[General Re]] (now **Tad Montross** CEO; [[Joe Brandon]]
stepped down in spring) is "the only reinsurer backed by an AAA corporation" — "It's difficult for
an empty sack to stand upright" (Franklin). [[Ajit Jain]]'s 31-person Stamford operation "may be one
of the most remarkable businesses in the world."

**[[Berkshire Hathaway Assurance|Tax-exempt bond insurance (BHAC)]].** Activated early 2008 as the
**monolines** collapsed ("I was Snow White, but I drifted" — Mae West). Berkshire offered to
reinsure $822B of three monolines' tax-exempt books at 1.5% (rejected — "I had severely underpriced
our offer"), then wrote **$15.6B of secondary** insurance at **3.3%** (77% on **already-insured**
bonds — "second-to-pay"; in one case "fourth to pay") and $3.7B of primary. But Buffett is wary: a
universe **fully** insured would behave differently from the historical (mostly uninsured) record —
politicians will choose "pain to a far-away bond insurer" over pain to local citizens (the 1975 NYC
example). Insuring tax-exempts "has the look today of a dangerous business — one with similarities…
to the insuring of natural catastrophes." **"Beware of geeks bearing formulas"**: history-based,
"back-tested" models drove "the stupefying losses in mortgage-related securities."

**Regulated utility ([[MidAmerican Energy]], 87.4%).** Record earnings (**$1,704M** to Berkshire).
Managers **[[David Sokol]]** and **[[Greg Abel]]** (plus partner [[Walter Scott Jr]]). The
**[[Constellation Energy]]** rescue netted $1,092M (a $175M breakup fee + a $917M investment
profit). MidAmerican is #1 among regulated utilities in **wind** capacity (Iowa #1 of all states; it
has held Iowa electric prices flat since 1999 and spent $1.8B on wind in 2008 alone). Berkshire aims
to be the **"buyer of choice"** — of families *and* of regulators — versus "**private equity**" firms
(an "Orwellian" rename of the leverage-loving LBO operators now watching their over-levered wards
near "mortal danger"). [[HomeServices of America]] lost $45M in a "terrible year for home sales."

**Manufacturing, Service & Retailing.** Revenues **$66,099M**; **17.9% on tangible net worth**, 8.1%
on carrying value (the goodwill drag). Many businesses "hit the skids" in Q4; 2009 "looks worse."
**[[ISCAR]]** bought **Tungaloy** (a leading Japanese small-tools maker) in late November —
"winning the Triple Crown" of managers (Wertheimer, Harpaz, Goldman). Tuck-ins also at MiTek,
Benjamin Moore, Acme Brick, Forest River, Marmon and CTB ([[CTB]] now $89M pre-tax on the $140M paid
in 2002).

**Finance — the [[Clayton Homes]] mortgage lesson.** Clayton delivered 27,499 manufactured homes
(34% of the industry) and its **198,888 borrowers kept paying** through the crash — *despite* a
median FICO of 644 (national 723) and 35% "sub-prime" — because they took mortgages "with the
intention of paying… whatever the course of home prices." Delinquency 3.6%, foreclosures 3.0%.
"Jimmy Stewart would have loved these folks." The 1997–2000 manufactured-housing fiasco ("borrowers
who shouldn't have borrowed being financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent") was an ignored
canary for the 2004–07 conventional-mortgage bubble. But Clayton's *lending* is threatened by the
crisis: government-guaranteed funders enjoy minimal money costs while AAA Berkshire's borrowing
spreads hit record levels — "it is much better to be a financial cripple with a government guarantee
than a Gibraltar without one."

**Derivatives.** "Derivatives are dangerous" — they let Fannie and Freddie misstate earnings for
years (OFHEO "totally missed their cooking of the books"); the Gen Re wind-down (23,218 contracts,
884 counterparties) took five years and $400M+. Yet Berkshire holds **251 contracts** because "each
contract we own was mispriced at inception." Its derivatives **"float" is $8.1 billion** (counterparties
pay up front; minimal counterparty risk). Four categories: **equity-index puts** ($37.1B notional on
the S&P 500, FTSE 100, Euro Stoxx 50, Nikkei 225; **$4.9B premiums**; Black-Scholes liability $10B →
a **$5.1B mark-to-market loss**; expiring 2019–2028); **high-yield-index credit** contracts ($3.4B
premiums, ~$100M recorded loss); new **single-name CDS** ($4B on 42 firms, $93M annual premium — the
only book with counterparty risk); and a few tax-exempt-bond derivatives (−$631M mark). The
**Black-Scholes critique**: for a 100-year S&P put the formula yields an "absurd" $2.5M premium, so
long-dated puts are over-stated; Berkshire still uses it ("conventional wisdom"). Both put and CDS
books "hold the money," so the wild reported swings "neither cheer nor bother Charlie and me."

**Investments & odds.** Common stocks (carried at market) cost **$37,135M** / market **$49,073M**.
**[[Moody's]]** and **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe]]** crossed 20% ownership → moved to equity
accounting (Moody's via *its* buybacks; BNSF via continued buying). New: a Chinese plug-in-car maker,
**[[BYD]]** (10% interest), to be unveiled at the meeting, and the **[[Wrigley]]**/Mars partnership.
"Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are usually greeted by
yawns." Buffett also flags a **U.S. Treasury bond bubble** ("almost equally extraordinary" to the
internet and housing bubbles) — clinging to cash/long Treasuries at these yields is "almost
certainly a terrible policy."

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $70,530, **−9.6%** (net worth −$11.5B); 44 years, **20.3% compounded**.
  Overall gain 1964–2008: **362,319%** vs. 4,276% for the S&P. Per-share investments fell $90,343 →
  **$77,793**; non-insurance pre-tax earnings/share $4,093 → **$3,921**.
- **Insurance:** underwriting profit **$2,792M** (Gen Re $342M / BH Reins $1,324M / GEICO $916M /
  Other Primary $210M); **paid $2.8B to hold $58,488M of float** (cost < 0, 6th straight year).
  GEICO share **7.7%**, now #3. BHAC: $15.6B secondary at 3.3% + $3.7B primary ($96M).
- **Crisis capital deployment:** **$14.5B** of fixed-income in **Goldman Sachs, General Electric,
  Wrigley** (high yields + equity sweeteners). **Constellation Energy** rescue +$1,092M (via
  MidAmerican). **Marmon** now 64% owned.
- **Confessed mistakes:** **ConocoPhillips** (bought near peak energy prices; several-$B cost); two
  **Irish banks** $244M → $27M (−89%).
- **MidAmerican (87.4%):** earnings to Berkshire **$1,704M**; #1 regulated-utility owner of wind.
  Clayton finance pre-tax $206M (down from $526M).
- **Mfg/Svc/Retail:** revenues **$66,099M**, **17.9% on tangible net worth** (8.1% on carrying).
  ISCAR bought Tungaloy.
- **Derivatives:** 251 contracts; "float" **$8.1B**. Equity-index puts $37.1B notional / $4.9B
  premiums / **−$5.1B mark**; high-yield credit $3.4B premiums; single-name CDS $4B/42 firms.
- **Common stocks** (cost $37,135M / market **$49,073M**): Coca-Cola $9,054M, Wells Fargo $8,973M,
  P&G $5,684M, ConocoPhillips $4,398M, Kraft $3,498M, Amex $2,812M (13.1%), J&J $1,795M, US Bancorp
  $1,879M, Tesco $1,193M, POSCO $1,191M, Wal-Mart $1,118M, Sanofi $1,404M, WaPo $674M, **Swiss Re
  $530M (new)**. Moody's & BNSF now at equity value.
