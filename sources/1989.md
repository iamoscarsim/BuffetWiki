# 1989 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 1990 — the 25th-anniversary letter, and one of the richest in the
corpus. Net worth grew **$1.515 billion (+44.4%)**, lifting book value to
**$4,296.01** (23.8% compounded over 25 years). Buffett defines [[Intrinsic Business Value]]
crisply (discounted future cash flows — "all businesses, from manufacturers of buggy
whips to operators of cellular phones, become economic equals") and delivers a sober
forecast: past gains enjoyed a **"double-dip"** — the portfolio companies' rising
business value *plus* the market re-rating their once-cheap stocks — but with those
stocks now fully priced, future returns are a **"single-dip."** Carl Sagan's
exponential-bacteria parable illustrates the **size** anchor: from a $4.9B base, 15%
growth is far harder than 23.8% was from $22M.

A **Taxes** essay reframes Berkshire's $1.1B deferred-tax liability as "an **interest-
free loan from the U.S. Treasury** that comes due only at our election" — a "transfer
tax" payable only on selling. The compounding math is famous: a dollar that doubles 20
times *in one holding* grows to ~$1,048,576 and leaves **~$692,000** after a single 34%
tax, versus ~**$25,250** from 20 separately-taxed doubles — a 27:1 edge from deferral
alone. Still, "we have not adopted our strategy… because of these mathematics": the
real reason is that "splendid business relationships [are] so rare and so enjoyable"
("marrying for money — a mistake under most circumstances, insanity if one is already
rich").

**Look-through earnings** are formally elevated as the truest measure of Berkshire's
earning power: 1989 dividends from five major investees were ~$45M, but Berkshire's
share of their *retained* earnings was ~**$212M** — "a-bird-in-the-bush-may-be-worth-two-
in-the-hand." For intrinsic value to grow 15%/yr, look-through earnings must too (see
[[Look-Through Earnings]]).

The operating group is rechristened the **"Sainted Seven Plus One"** after adding
**[[Borsheim's]]** (a stellar first year — sales >2× four years earlier; expense ratio
~⅓ of comparable jewelers; up to 4,000 visitors a day; the "jewels aren't in the safe"
story). The group earned **57% on average equity** with no net leverage — "the same
glow that Miller Huggins must have… [had with] his 1927 New York Yankees." **See's**
poundage rose 8% with first same-store gains in years (helped by tripled newspaper
advertising via Hal Riney). At **NFM**, a sad note: **[[Rose Blumkin]] (96) quit in
May** after a carpet-department dispute — and promptly opened a *competing* carpet-and-
furniture store across the street; the Blumkins' NFM carpet share nonetheless hit
75.3%. The **Buffalo News** notched its **seventh consecutive profit record**, #1
penetration, and a 50.1% news hole; Buffett credits [[Stan Lipsey]] with "five extra
percentage points" of margin. **Fechheimer** dipped on integrating its 1988 deal;
**[[Scott Fetzer]]** ([[Ralph Schey]]) earned more every year, ~40% of non-insurance
profits, "top decile" ROE.

**Insurance**: the combined ratio rose to ~110.4 (Hurricane Hugo and the California
earthquake added ~2 points). Buffett dismisses the rhythmic "underwriting cycle" — the
cartel era is gone; a commodity with hundreds of sellers earns decently *only* during a
capacity shortage, which occurs "only when insurers are frightened." The landmark is
the **CAT-cover/super-catastrophe** debut: after Hugo and the quake left the market
short, Berkshire offered up to **$250 million** of catastrophe coverage and **retained
it** (no lay-off) — a posture possible because its insurance net worth (~**$6 billion**,
second-highest in the U.S.) lets it accept a potential ~$165M after-tax single-quarter
loss: "We are willing to *look* foolish as long as we don't feel we have *acted*
foolishly." The team — [[Mike Goldberg]] with Rod Eldred, Dinos Iordanou,
**[[Ajit Jain]]**, Phil Urban, Don Wurster.

**Marketable securities**: the **[[Coca-Cola]]** essay — Berkshire more than doubled its
stake to 23,350,000 shares (cost $1.024B, market $1.804B), Buffett's largest commitment
yet, made after a 52-year delay during which he sold Cokes as a boy but bought "street
railway companies, windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers" instead — "only in the
summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes." He credits CEO
**[[Roberto Goizueta]]** and **[[Don Keough]]**. **GEICO** crossed $1B in market value;
Cap Cities/ABC reached $1.69B. He warns the portfolio's prices are "clearly vulnerable
to a general market decline" — "almost certain in at least one of the next three years."

Three new **convertible preferreds** join the [[Salomon]] preferred to form the "four
preferreds": **[[Gillette]]** ($600M, 8.75%, convert $50; CEO [[Colman Mockler]]),
**[[USAir]]** ($358M, 9.25%, $60; Ed Colodny), and **[[Champion International]]** ($300M,
9.25%, $38; Andy Sigler). Buffett *understands* Gillette's economics (he's on its board)
but is "agnostic, not atheist" on airlines and paper — hence the preferred structure,
which yields a mediocre return if industries struggle but good results if the common
does well. Sold low-coupon **[[WPPSS Bonds]]** (doubled in price, having paid 15–17%
tax-free) and most medium-term tax-exempts ($51M pre-tax gain). Sat out **[[Arbitrage]]**
("Fools rush in where angels fear to trade").

Berkshire issued **$902.6M of zero-coupon convertible debentures** (44.314% of face;
~$400M proceeds; convertible at a 15% premium) — prompting a withering essay on
**[[Zero-Coupon Bonds]]**: from the benign Series E war bond and Treasury "strips" to
the junk-era abuse of zeros and PIK bonds, "EBDIT" (ignoring depreciation), Galbraith's
"bezzle," and investment bankers who abandoned "bartender morality." Finally, the
celebrated **"Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years"** — cigar-butt investing ("never
just one cockroach in the kitchen"; Hochschild Kohn; "Time is the friend of the
wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre"), good jockeys on good horses, the
**[[Institutional Imperative]]**, errors of omission ("thumb-sucking"), and the verdict
that conservative finance was *not* a mistake ("a small chance of distress… cannot be
offset by a large chance of extra returns").

Also: [[Harry Bottle]] (now 70) added a product line to K&W; the corporate jet was
upgraded to a $6.7M used plane named **"The Indefensible"**; the shareholder-designated
program gave **$5.9M** to 2,550 charities.

## Key figures
- Net worth +$1.515B (**+44.4%**); book value/share **$4,296.01** (25 yrs, 23.8%).
- Operating earnings to Berkshire **$299.9M**; total earnings $447.5M.
- "Sainted Seven Plus One" earned **57% on average equity**, unleveraged.
- Look-through: ~$45M dividends vs. ~**$212M** share of investees' retained earnings.
- Berkshire combined ratio ~110 (Hugo/quake); insurance net worth ~**$6B** (2nd in U.S.).
- Holdings >$100M: Cap Cities/ABC **$1,692.4M**, [[Coca-Cola]] **$1,803.8M** (cost $1,023.9M),
  Freddie Mac **$161.1M**, GEICO **$1,044.6M**, WaPo **$486.4M**.
- New preferreds: [[Gillette]] $600M, [[USAir]] $358M, [[Champion International]] $300M.
- Zero-coupon debentures: $902.6M face at 44.314% → ~$400M proceeds; deferred-tax
  liability ~$1.1B reframed as an interest-free Treasury "loan."
