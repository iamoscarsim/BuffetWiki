# 2017 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$65.3 billion**; per-share book value rose **23%** to **$211,750** (53 years, **19.1%
compounded**). But Buffett immediately deflates the headline: "**2017 was far from standard. A large
portion of our gain did not come from anything we accomplished at Berkshire.**" Only **$36 billion** came
from operations; the other **$29 billion** was "delivered to us in December when Congress rewrote the
U.S. Tax Code" (the **Tax Cuts and Jobs Act** revalued Berkshire's net deferred-tax liabilities at the
new 21% rate). Overall 1964–2017 gain: book **1,088,029%**, market **2,404,748%**, S&P 15,508%.

**A warning about a new accounting rule.** Beginning in 2018, GAAP will require the **net change in
unrealized gains/losses on stocks** to flow through **net income**. With Berkshire holding **$170 billion**
of marketable stocks (ex-Kraft Heinz), whose value "can easily swing by $10 billion or more" in a quarter,
this will "produce some truly wild and capricious swings in our GAAP bottom-line… For analytical purposes,
Berkshire's 'bottom-line' will be useless." Buffett pleads with shareholders and the media to focus on
**operating earnings**, not GAAP net income (see [[Operating Earnings vs GAAP Net Income]]). He will keep
releasing results late Friday/early Saturday to give analysts time to interpret them.

**The four building blocks — and an acquisition drought.** Value comes from (1) stand-alone acquisitions,
(2) bolt-ons, (3) internal growth, and (4) investment earnings. In 2017 the *price* test "proved a barrier
to virtually all deals," as "prices for decent, but far from spectacular, businesses hit an all-time high"
— fueled by "can-do" CEOs ("Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut"; "Spreadsheets never
disappoint") and "extraordinarily cheap debt." Berkshire evaluates deals **all-equity**, factors in **no
synergies**, and refuses leverage: "**It is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what
you don't need.**"

**The one stand-alone deal — Pilot Flying J.** Berkshire bought a **38.6% partnership interest** in
**[[Pilot Flying J]]**, "far and away the nation's leading travel-center operator" (~$20B annual volume,
~750 locations, 27,000 associates), run by the **Haslam family** (founder "Big Jim," now son Jimmy). A
contract steps Berkshire to **80% in 2023**.

**Bolt-ons.** **[[Clayton Homes]]** bought two conventional-home builders (Oakwood Homes in Colorado;
Harris Doyle in Birmingham), more than doubling site-built volume (>$1B expected in 2018); Clayton held
**49% of the manufactured-home market**. **[[Shaw Industries]]** acquired **U.S. Floors** (luxury vinyl
tile), lifting Shaw to **$5.7B** of sales (22,000 employees). **[[HomeServices of America]]**'s "growth
exploded" — buying **Long and Foster** (the #3 brokerage), **Houlihan Lawrence**, and **Gloria Nilson** —
adding 12,300 agents (to **40,950**) and participating in **$127 billion of "sides"** (still only ~3% of
the U.S. market). **[[Precision Castparts]]** bought Germany's **Wilhelm Schulz** ("I don't understand
manufacturing… Mark Donegan is an extraordinary manufacturing executive… Betting on people can sometimes
be more certain than betting on physical assets").

**Insurance — the streak ends.** A premium/float table (1970 → 2017) shows premium volume $60,597M and
**float $114,500M** — boosted by a single huge deal: Berkshire reinsured **up to $20 billion of AIG's
long-tail losses** for a **$10.2 billion premium** ("a world's record and one we won't come close to
repeating"; see [[AIG retroactive reinsurance]]). After **14 consecutive years of underwriting profit
($28.3B pre-tax)**, 2017 brought a **$3.2 billion pre-tax underwriting loss** — Buffett's long-standing
warning made fact — from **Hurricanes Harvey, Irma and Maria** (~$3B of losses, ~$2B after tax; ~3% of a
~$100B industry loss, "less than 1%" of Berkshire's net worth, vs. 7–15% hits at other reinsurers). The
**mega-cat framework**: ~2% annual probability of a **$400B** U.S. insured mega-catastrophe, of which
Berkshire's ~$12B share would be "far below the annual earnings we expect from our non-insurance
activities" — while "perhaps most" of the p/c world "would be out of business." The Lloyd's 1980s near-
collapse is cited as a caution that long-tail risk "can be anything but" predictable.

**Operations summary.** For the first time Buffett gives only "a simple summary" of the non-insurance
businesses (deferring detail to the 10-K). Ex-investment pre-tax income was **$20 billion** (+$950M);
**BNSF and 90.2%-owned [[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]** supplied ~**44%**. The next five (Clayton, IMC,
Lubrizol, Marmon, PCC) earned **$5.5B**; the next five (Forest River, Johns Manville, MiTek, Shaw, TTI)
**$2.1B**; the rest **$3.7B**. Depreciation **$7.6B**, capex **$11.5B**, amortization $1.3B ("in large
part… not a true economic cost"). Year-end **cash $116.0 billion** (from $86.4B) "earns only a pittance
and is far beyond the level Charlie and I wish" — the search is on for "one or more huge acquisitions."

**Investments.** The portfolio (cost $74.7B / market **$170.5B**) reflects 2017's big moves: **[[Apple]]**
built up dramatically to **166.7M shares (3.3%, cost $21.0B / market $28.2B)**; **[[Bank of America]]**
appears as a **common-stock holding for the first time** (700M shares, 6.8%, cost $5.0B / market $20.7B —
the **2011 warrant was exercised** in 2017); and three other new/itemized positions: **[[Bank of New York Mellon]]** (5.3%), **[[General Motors]]** (3.2%), and **[[BYD]]** (now 8.2%, in the top 15). Coca-Cola,
Wells Fargo, Amex, Phillips 66, Moody's, US Bancorp, the airlines ([[Delta Air Lines|Delta]] 7.4%,
[[Southwest Airlines|Southwest]] 8.1%; the others in "Others"), Goldman, and Charter round it out.
**[[Kraft Heinz]]** (equity-method, $9.8B cost / $25.3B market). **[[Todd Combs]] and [[Ted Weschler]]**
each now manage **>$12 billion**. Buffett restates **Owner Principle 6** — undistributed earnings of
investees should deliver "at least equivalent" capital gains over time — and Ben Graham's "**in the short
run, the market is a voting machine; in the long run… a weighing machine**." A table of Berkshire's **four
major drawdowns** (−59.1% in 1973–75, −37.1% in 1987, −48.9% in 1998–2000, −50.7% in 2008–09) frames "the
strongest argument I can muster **against ever using borrowed money to own stocks**," capped by Kipling's
"**If**" (see [[Risk and Volatility]]).

**"The Bet" is over.** The decade-long wager concluded: the S&P index fund gained **125.8% (8.5%/yr)** vs.
the five funds-of-funds' final gains of 21.7%, 42.3%, 87.7%, 2.8%, and 27.0% (averages 0.3%–6.5%/yr).
**Girls Inc. of Omaha received $2,222,279** rather than the originally-hoped $1M, because in **November
2012** Buffett and **[[Ted Seides|Protégé]]** had switched the prize money out of zero-coupon Treasuries
(then yielding a "pathetic" 0.88%, selling at "more than 100 times earnings") into **11,200 Berkshire B
shares** — "a really dumb investment compared to American equities." The lessons: "**Performance comes,
performance goes. Fees never falter**"; over long horizons high-grade **bonds can be riskier than stocks**
("often, high-grade bonds in an investment portfolio increase its risk"); and "**stick with big, 'easy'
decisions and eschew activity**" (see [[Index Funds and Fees]], [[Risk and Volatility]]).

**"I've saved the best for last" — the succession milestone.** Early in 2018 Berkshire's board elected
**[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]] as directors and Vice Chairmen** — Jain "responsible for insurance
operations," Abel overseeing "the rest of our businesses," while "**Charlie and I will focus on
investments and capital allocation**." "Each has been with Berkshire for decades, and Berkshire's blood
flows through their veins" — the long-anticipated handoff foreshadowed by [[The Berkshire System]].

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** **$211,750, +23%** (net worth **+$65.3B**; 53 yrs, **19.1%**) — but **$29B from
  tax reform**, only $36B from operations. 1964–2017 gain: book **1,088,029%**, market **2,404,748%**.
- **New GAAP rule** (2018): unrealized stock gains/losses flow through net income → "useless" GAAP
  bottom-line; **$170B** of marketable stocks.
- **Acquisition:** **Pilot Flying J** (38.6%, → 80% in 2023). Bolt-ons: Clayton (Oakwood, Harris Doyle),
  Shaw (U.S. Floors → $5.7B sales), HomeServices (Long and Foster + 2 → 40,950 agents), PCC (Wilhelm Schulz).
- **Insurance:** **first underwriting loss in 15 years** (**−$3.2B** pre-tax; Harvey/Irma/Maria), ending a
  14-year, $28.3B streak. Premium $60,597M; **float $114,500M** (incl. the **$10.2B AIG** reinsurance).
- **Non-insurance ex-investment pre-tax $20B** (+$950M); BNSF + BHE (90.2%) ~44%. Depreciation $7.6B,
  capex $11.5B. **Cash $116.0B** (from $86.4B).
- **Equities** (cost $74.7B / mkt **$170.5B**): **Apple** 3.3% ($28.2B mkt), **BofA** new common (6.8%,
  $20.7B; warrant exercised), **BNY Mellon** (new), **GM** (new), **BYD** (8.2%). **Combs & Weschler** each **>$12B**.
- **"The Bet" final:** index **+125.8%** (8.5%/yr) vs. funds-of-funds avg 0.3–6.5%/yr; Girls Inc. got $2.22M.
- **Succession:** **Ajit Jain & Greg Abel** named Vice Chairmen (insurance / non-insurance).
