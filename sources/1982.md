# 1982 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 3, 1983. Operating earnings of $31.5M were just **9.8% of beginning
equity** — down from 15.2% and far below the 19.4% of 1978 — hit by deteriorating
insurance underwriting, a larger equity base, and the growing share of earnings
locked inside non-controlled holdings. In a notable move, Buffett **formally
abandons the operating-earnings/equity yardstick** for Berkshire (while affirming it
for most companies), warning against the temptation to "shoot the arrow... into a
blank canvas and then carefully draw the bullseye around the implanted arrow."

The reason is **[[Look-Through Earnings]]**, now reframed as **"economic" vs.
"accounting" earnings**. GEICO illustrates the absurdity: $3.5M of dividends were
counted, while $23M of Berkshire's share of GEICO's *retained* earnings was ignored
— "if GEICO had earned an additional $100 million — and retained it all — our
reported earnings would have been unchanged." Across four major holdings (GEICO,
General Foods, Washington Post, R.J. Reynolds), unrecorded earnings topped $40M,
exceeding total reported earnings. He credits "St. Offset" (Arthur Okun's patron
saint) for over-achieving holdings more than covering laggards, and notes the
auction market's "manic-depressive lemmings" create the bargains — an early sketch
of [[Mr. Market]]. Net worth grew **$208M (40%)** to a book value of **$737.43**
(22.0% compounded over 18 years), though "geometric progressions eventually forge
their own anchors."

The letter's most enduring essay concerns **[[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]**:
"we will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we
give." Because a buyer's stock often trades below intrinsic value while sellers
command full value, paying in undervalued stock means giving "$2 of value to receive
$1." He dissects the rationalizations ("we have to grow"; the 51%-stock/49%-cash
dodge), the misleading focus on EPS "dilution" versus intrinsic-value dilution, and
the "double whammy" to acquirers' shareholders. The pending **Berkshire–Blue Chip
merger** is offered as a true business-value-for-business-value deal.

A parallel essay deepens **[[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]**: industries with
over-capacity and an undifferentiated product (candy bars sell by brand, sugar
doesn't) earn poor returns unless a producer has "a cost advantage that is both wide
and sustainable." Insurance is "a textbook case" — capacity is created instantly "by
capital plus an underwriter's willingness to sign his name." The historic exception
(a quasi-administered, regulator-blessed pricing system) "is gone"; only a
"megadisaster" shrinking supply will restore underwriting profits — at which point
well-capitalized insurers like Berkshire will find "great opportunities." Berkshire's
own 1982 underwriting "deteriorated far more than... the industry's."

**[[GEICO]]** shines as the "high-profit exception" — a wide, sustainable cost
advantage — its market value reaching $309.6M ($79M of the year's $208M gain); Buffett
praises Jack Byrne, Bill Snyder, and introduces **[[Lou Simpson]]** as "the best
investment manager in the property-casualty business." Parent-company insurance
responsibility passed to **[[Mike Goldberg]]**. The letter publishes Berkshire's
formal **acquisition "want ad"** (six criteria; a "five-minute" answer; no hostile
deals), and closes with tributes to two retiring stars: **[[Phil Liesche]]** (65,
National Indemnity) and **[[Ben Rosner]]** (79, Associated Retail Stores) — managers
who ran their businesses as if they owned 100%.

## Key figures
- Operating earnings $31.5M; **ROE 9.8%** (yardstick formally retired for Berkshire).
- Net worth +$208M (**40%**); book value/share **$737.43** (18 yrs, 22.0% compounded).
- Non-controlled undistributed earnings >$40M (vs $14M dividends counted).
- Total common stocks: cost **$424.0M**, market **$945.6M**.
- GEICO market **$309.6M** ($79M of year's gain); ~35% stake ≈ $250M premium volume.
- Washington Post market $103.2M; R.J. Reynolds $158.7M; new: Time Inc., Crum & Forster.
- Industry combined ratio 1982 (est.) 109.5; insurance underwriting loss $(11.3M) after tax.
- Designated contributions: 95.8% participation, $1/share.
