# 1997 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$8.0 billion**, lifting per-share book value (both A and B) **34.1%** to
**$25,488** (33 years, **24.1%** compounded). Buffett refuses to celebrate: in a roaring
bull market "any investor can chalk up large returns," like "the preening duck that
quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm" — and the S&P (which bears no corporate
tax) rose almost as fast. "Our appraisal of 1997's performance, then: **Quack**."
Berkshire paid or accrued **$4.2 billion** of federal income tax (~18% of beginning net
worth), a drag the Index and mutual funds don't carry; the goal is only "a **modest
advantage over the Index** … over time." (The Cubs-fan rejoinder: "Why get upset?
Everyone has a bad century now and then.")

**The two-column intrinsic-value table** (third year): investments hit **$38,043/share
(+33.5%)** and pre-tax operating earnings ex-investment income **$717.82/share (+70.3%)**;
since 1967 the two have compounded **25.6%** and **24.2%**, with pre-tax operating
earnings grown from $1M to **$888M**. A caveat: 1997's operating earnings were flattered
by super-cat luck and an unsustainably high GEICO underwriting margin, and won't be
matched in 1998. The size brake, vividly: in 1951 a $10,000 idea moved Buffett's year by
100 points; today a **$500M** pre-tax profit adds just **one** point. "We weren't smarter
then, just smaller."

**Prices are high — so wait for the fat pitch.** With "relatively little in prospective
earnings when we commit fresh money," Buffett invokes **Ted Williams**'s *The Science of
Hitting* (the strike zone carved into 77 cells; swing only at pitches in the best cell).
Today's business "pitches" are "just catching the lower outside corner" — swing and "we
will be locked into low returns."

**Unconventional commitments.** Lacking its favorite buys, Berkshire ventured into three
non-traditional positions: (1) **oil derivatives** — 14.0M barrels left of a 45.7M-barrel
1994–95 position (31.7M settled for a **$61.9M** pre-tax gain; $11.6M unrealized at
year-end); (2) **silver** — **111.2M ounces** bought (a **$97.4M** mark-to-market pre-tax
gain) on falling bullion inventories needing "a higher price … to establish equilibrium"
(inflation "play[s] no part"); and (3) **$4.6 billion of long-term zero-coupon U.S.
Treasuries**, a macro rate bet that produced a **$598.8M** unrealized gain as rates fell.
"You pay Charlie and me to use our best judgment — not to avoid embarrassment."

**How to think about market fluctuations.** A net buyer should *want* lower prices, just
as a lifelong hamburger-eater wants cheaper beef. Berkshire owners are "automatically
saving" (all earnings retained), so they "should … rejoice when markets decline";
likewise, investees that **repurchase shares** ([[Coca-Cola]], [[Washington Post]],
[[Wells Fargo]]) help Berkshire more at low prices. "Edit [the headline] in your mind to
'*Dis*investors lose as market falls — but investors gain.'"

**Insurance & float.** A fifth straight underwriting profit kept float — now **$7,093.1M**
— at *less-than-zero* cost; it has grown **21.7%/yr since 1967**. "Therein lies an
accounting irony: Though our float is shown … as a liability, it has had a value to
Berkshire greater than an equal amount of net worth." (Float will dip in Q1 1998 as big
contracts expire.) In **super-cat**, another lucky (loss-free) year — but Buffett warns
against the new fad of **"catastrophe bonds"** ("an Orwellian misnomer": they lay the
promise to pay on the *buyer*), illustrated by a dice parable (insuring a 1-in-36 roll
for too little "premium" — a 75.4% chance of going a decade unscathed before going
broke). The "expert puts no money on the table." Cat prices fell, so Berkshire will write
less in 1998; worst-case mega-cat loss remains ~**$600M** after tax (the CEA maximum,
~1% of market value). [[Ajit Jain]] "developed [super-cat] from scratch."

**GEICO is flying.** Voluntary auto policies in-force grew **16%** (vs. 10% in 1996), new
business nearly tripling over five years (354,882 → 913,176). Underwriting profit was
**8.1% of premiums** — above the **~4% target**, so Buffett *cut* rates to pass savings
on. Profit-sharing leapt to **26.9% ($71M)** (from 16.9%) — "we distribute **merit
badges, not lottery tickets**." For 1998, GEICO will floor it: **marketing spend >$100M
(+50%)** against a mere **3% market share** in a $115B industry. **[[Lorimer Davidson]]**
turned 95 — "my friend and teacher for 47 years." The five other primary insurers
aggregated a **15.0% underwriting profit** (NICO 32.9% under Don Wurster; homestate 14.1%
under Rod Eldred; workers' comp a small loss under Brad Kinstler; Central States John
Kizer; [[Kansas Bankers Surety]] Don Towle).

**Two acquisitions agreed.** **[[Star Furniture]]** (Houston; CEO Melvyn Wolff and sister
Shirley Toomin; closed July 1, 1997) — the last of the "**any more at home like you?**"
referrals that ran Blumkins → [[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings|R.C. Willey]] (Bill Child) →
Star. **[[International Dairy Queen]]** (closed early 1998; 5,792 DQ stores in 23
countries, plus Orange Julius and Karmelkorn; John Mooty and CEO Mike Sullivan), bought
on FlightSafety-style cash-or-stock terms (only 45% took cash).

**A confession on issuing stock.** Tallying all stock-only mergers (ex-Diversified
Retailing and Blue Chip), "our shareholders are slightly worse off … **when I've issued
stock, I've cost you money**" — not because sellers underperformed, but because
Berkshire's own roster is "filled with **.380 hitters**" and trading any of them away
"almost never makes sense." Hence the strong preference for **cash** (the great cash buys:
NICO, See's, Buffalo News, Scott Fetzer, GEICO). A public-company premium is justified
only if the buyer's stock is overvalued *or* real synergy exists — usually neither — and
voracious share-issuers run "Wall Street's version of a **chain-letter scheme**." When
Berkshire buys an option-issuing company, it swaps in cash comp so the true cost is
"brought out of the closet."

**Portfolio.** Net sales of ~5% of the beginning portfolio (some trimming, a modest
bond/stock realignment). The market isn't deemed overvalued *if* rates stay low and
corporate ROE stays high — but the "**margin of safety**" has "materially eroded" amid a
"very cheery consensus" (echoing his 1979 *Forbes* "cheery consensus" article).
**[[Roberto Goizueta]]**, Coke's CEO since 1981, **died in October**; Doug Ivester
succeeds ("the same steamroller"). The **[[Salomon]]** saga ended happily: it **merged
into [[Travelers Group]]** (Deryck Maughan and Bob Denham credited with the rescue), and
**[[USAir]]** "borders on the miraculous" — arrears paid with extras, common $4 → $73,
the preferred called for March 15, 1998 with conversion rights now valuable: "Next time I
make a big, dumb decision … *Phone Mr. Wolf*." The **[[American Express]] Percs** story:
a 1994 round of golf with Hertz's Frank Olson convinced Buffett to hold and build to 10%
— now a **$3 billion** gain ("110% luck, the balance skill").

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $25,488, **+34.1%** (net worth **+$8.0B**); 33 years, 24.1%
  compounded. Federal income tax paid/accrued **$4.2B** (~18% of beginning net worth).
- **Two-column table:** investments **$38,043/share** (+33.5%); pre-tax operating earnings
  ex-investments **$717.82/share** (+70.3%). 1967–97 rates: 25.6% / 24.2%.
- **Insurance:** **5th straight underwriting profit**; average float **$7,093.1M** at
  negative cost (21.7%/yr since 1967). GEICO underwriting profit **8.1%** of premiums;
  five other primaries aggregate **15.0%** profit. NICO 32.9%.
- **GEICO:** voluntary in-force **+16%**; profit-sharing **26.9% ($71M)**; 1998 marketing
  >$100M (+50%); 3% market share.
- **Operating earnings:** $1,715.7M pre-tax / $1,194.5M to Berkshire. Capital gains
  $1,111.9M. **Total earnings** $2,827.6M / $1,901.6M.
- **Look-through earnings:** **$1,930M** (reported $1,292M + $743M investee retained −
  $105M tax at 14%).
- **Common stocks:** cost $7,206.9M / **market $36,247.7M**. Coca-Cola $13,337.5M, Gillette
  $4,821M, Amex $4,414M, Freddie Mac $2,683.1M, Wells Fargo $2,270.9M (trimmed), Disney
  $2,134.8M (trimmed), Travelers Group $1,278.6M (from Salomon merger), WaPo $840.6M.
- **Unconventional:** silver 111.2M oz (+$97.4M); oil derivatives ($61.9M realized,
  $11.6M unrealized); $4.6B zero-coupon Treasuries (+$598.8M unrealized).
- **Acquisitions:** Star Furniture (closed 7/1/97); International Dairy Queen (closed
  early 1998).
- **SDC:** $15.4M; 97.7%; 3,830 charities (cumulative 17-yr $113.1M).
