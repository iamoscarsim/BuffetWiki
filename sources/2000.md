# 2000 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$3.96 billion**, lifting per-share book value **6.5%** to **$40,442** (36
years, 23.6% compounded) — "a decent year," with the book-value gain outpacing the S&P 500
(which fell 9.1%) and intrinsic value "moderately exceed[ing]" the book gain. The year's
story is an **acquisition spree**: two 1999-negotiated deals closed and **six more** were
initiated — **eight businesses, ~$8 billion, 97% cash / 3% stock**, with no debt and
shares up just **⅓ of 1%**. Buffett deadpans that Berkshire "embraced the 21st century by
entering such cutting-edge industries as **brick, carpet, insulation and paint**." Two
negatives: **GEICO's policyholder growth "slowed to a halt"** (the stepped-up advertising
he'd promised would pay off "was wrong"), and the **equity portfolio is "only mildly
attractive."**

**The eight acquisitions.** All by phone — "our acquisition technique … is simplicity
itself: We answer the phone." **[[MidAmerican Energy]]** (76%, closed; one-line
consolidation). **[[CORT Business Services]]** (~$386M cash, → Wesco; "rent-to-rent"
furniture leader; Paul Arnold). **[[U.S. Liability]]** (half cash/half stock; excess &
surplus lines; Tom Nerney; via a [[Ron Ferguson]] introduction). **[[Ben Bridge Jeweler]]**
(half cash/half stock; 65-store West Coast jeweler; Ed and Jon Bridge; recommended by
[[Barnett Helzberg]]). **[[Justin Industries]]** (~$570M cash; Western boots + **Acme
Brick**; John Justin). **[[Shaw Industries]]** (87.3%; world's largest carpet maker; Bob
Shaw and Julian Saul — Berkshire's "by far … largest" non-insurance business). **[[Benjamin Moore]]** ($1B cash; 117-year-old paint maker). **[[Johns Manville]]** (~$1.8B all-cash,
after an LBO financing fell through; insulation/roofing). Two tailwinds helped: sellers
feared near-term slowdowns, and the **junk-bond market dried up**, hobbling leveraged
buyers and leaving Berkshire (which "analyze[s] purchases on an all-equity basis") "much
more competitive." Berkshire is increasingly "the **buyer of choice**" — "We'll leave the
auctions to others."

**Insurance & float.** Float grew to **$27,871M**, but a **$1.6 billion underwriting loss**
gave it a **6% cost**. Two unusual items now loom large: "**pain-today, gain-tomorrow**"
deals (1999 $400M, 2000 $482M of the loss — float Berkshire keeps for years), and
**retroactive reinsurance** (assuming a company's *past* losses, always capped; a
"deferred charges" asset amortized into future underwriting losses, in exchange for "lots
of" float). Both are "almost single-handedly the work of **[[Ajit Jain]]** … impossible to
overstate how valuable Ajit is … Don't worry about my health; worry about his." Jain
booked a **$2.4 billion reinsurance premium** (perhaps the largest in history) covering a
U.K. company, plus a disability policy on **Alex Rodriguez** ($252M contract) and the
**Grab.com $1 billion prize** ($170M present value; no one won). **[[General Re]]** "has
turned considerably better" under [[Ron Ferguson]], Joe Brandon and Tad Montross
(repricing the worst business). **[[Brad Kinstler]]** was moved from Cypress to run
[[Fechheimer Brothers]].

**GEICO stumbles.** New auto policies *fell* to **1,472,853** (from 1,648,095), though
in-force still grew to **4,696,842**. The extra advertising "did not produce a
commensurate increase in inquiries," and the conversion rate fell — spiking per-policy
acquisition cost. Four causes: ad-frequency overreach, low-hanging fruit picked, stricter
underwriting + rate increases, and — most important — **State Farm** (19% share) holding
prices down despite an 18%-of-premium auto underwriting loss (a ~23% float cost) vs.
GEICO's 4% (6.1% float cost). Buffett salutes State Farm as "one of America's greatest
business stories" but insists GEICO's **low-cost model** means it "will inevitably increase
its market share." Profit-sharing fell to **8.8% / $40.7M**; "we are in good hands with
[[Tony Nicely]]."

**The Aesop essay — "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush."** The conceptual
centerpiece (see **[[Bird in the Bush]]**). Every asset's value reduces to a 600-B.C.
formula requiring three inputs: how *certain* the birds (cash) in the bush are, *when* and
*how many* will emerge, and the *risk-free rate*. "Neither the advent of the steam engine
… nor … the Internet changed the formula one iota." **Growth is just a component of value**
("Market commentators … who glibly refer to 'growth' and 'value' styles … are displaying
their ignorance"). Applied to the **dot-com bubble**: "Nothing sedates rationality like
large doses of effortless money" (Cinderella at a ball whose "clocks have no hands"); a
Dec-1999 survey's 19% expected returns were "irrational"; "**value is destroyed, not
created, by any business that loses money over its lifetime**"; the bubble was a "wealth
transfer" run on "the old-fashioned chain letter." "A pin lies in wait for every bubble."

**Portfolio.** Itemized over-$1B holdings: Coca-Cola ($12.2B), **Amex ($8.3B**, now
151.6M shares after a 3:1 split), Wells Fargo ($3.07B), Gillette ($3.47B, fell), WaPo
($1.07B, crossing $1B). Berkshire **sold nearly all its Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae**,
took **15% positions in several mid-sized companies**, bought a few high-yield bonds, and
added mortgage-backed securities — "no 'bargains' among our current holdings." Buffett
restates that he'd **rather buy businesses than stocks** (1983 principle #4), partly for
the **tax** advantage of 80%+ ownership (the government is a "partner twice" with a stock
stake, "only once" with control).

**Full and fair reporting.** EBITDA "make[s] us shudder — does management think the tooth
fairy pays for capital expenditures?" Buffett praises SEC chairman **Arthur Levitt**'s
crackdown on **selective disclosure** (Reg FD), warns CEOs against **predicting growth
rates** ("fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies" will hit 15% for 20 years),
and recounts a *Wall Street Journal* front-page error (a falsely-reported Conseco bond
purchase) to argue rumors deserve "a warning label."

**Misc.** **Dexter Shoe** drew the famous confession (below). **[[Ralph Schey]] retired**
after 15 years running [[Scott Fetzer]] ($1.03B distributed to Berkshire on a $230M net
price) — "Berkshire's Hall of Fame." Aircraft Services thrived: **[[FlightSafety International]]** ($272M of simulators; Al Ueltschi, 83) and **[[Executive Jet]]/NetJets**
(recurring revenue +49%; expanding in Europe; >50 new jets in 2001). FASB resolved the
**pooling** fight, ending intangible amortization. Buffett marked **50 years since entering
[[Ben Graham]]'s class** ("a few hours … proved far more valuable … than … ten years of
supposedly original thinking"). SDC reached **$16.9M** (cumulative 20-year $164M).

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $40,442, **+6.5%** (net worth +$3.96B) — beat the S&P (−9.1%). 36
  years, 23.6% compounded.
- **Acquisitions:** eight businesses, ~$8B (**97% cash / 3% stock**); aggregate sales ~$13B,
  58,000 employees; shares +⅓ of 1%. Workforce >112,000.
- **Insurance:** float **$27,871M** (GEICO $3,943M / Gen Re $15,525M / Other Reins $7,805M /
  Other Primary $598M); **$1.6B underwriting loss**, cost of float **6%**. Ajit's **$2.4B**
  retroactive premium.
- **GEICO:** new policies **1,472,853** (down); in-force **4,696,842**; profit-sharing
  **8.8% / $40.7M**. State Farm float cost ~23% vs. GEICO 6.1%.
- **Operating earnings** $1,699M pre-tax / **$936M to Berkshire**; capital gains $3,955M /
  $2,392M; **total $5,654M / $3,328M**.
- **Look-through earnings:** **$2,415M** (reported $1,779M + $740M investee retained − $104M
  tax). MidAmerican $197M pre-tax (first year).
- **Common stocks:** cost $10,402M / **market $37,619M**. Coca-Cola $12,188M, Amex $8,329M
  (151.6M sh post-3:1; 11.4%), Wells Fargo $3,067M (3.2%), Gillette $3,468M, WaPo $1,066M.
  **Freddie Mac & Fannie Mae nearly all sold.**
- **SDC:** $16.9M (97%; cumulative $164M). Ralph Schey retired (Scott Fetzer).
