# 1981 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written February 26, 1982. Operating earnings of $39.7M were **15.2% of beginning
equity** (down from 17.8%), dented slightly by the new shareholder-designated
contributions program. The letter is one of Buffett's most quoted, built around
three set-pieces: a critique of overpriced acquisitions, an inflation-and-cost-of-
capital argument, and the launch of a novel charity program.

**Acquisition behavior** ([[Premium Takeovers]]): Buffett would "rather buy 10% of
Wonderful Business T at X per share than 100% of T at 2X" — the opposite of most
managers. He names three usually-unspoken motives behind high-premium takeovers:
managerial animal spirits, the worship of *size* over profitability, and the
"managerial kiss" delusion (from the fairy tale of the prince trapped in a toad's
body). "Investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors
instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the toad,
those kisses had better pack some real dynamite. We've observed many kisses but very
few miracles." He salutes two kinds of successful acquirers — those who bought
inflation-resistant businesses (pricing power + low capital needs), and managerial
superstars like Henry Singleton (Teledyne) and Tom Murphy ([[Capital Cities]]) — and
candidly places himself outside the superstar category, confessing he "neglected the
Noah principle: predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does."

**Inflation and value-added**: Updating [[Inflation]], Buffett argues that soaring
*passive* returns (1981 long taxable bonds >16%, tax-exempts >14%) raised the
"crossbar" so high that a typical 14%-ROE American business "is no longer worth one
hundred cents on the dollar to owners who are individuals" — equity capital "in
aggregate, produces no value-added for individual investors." He introduces the
**"gigantic corporate tapeworm"** of inflation, which "preemptively consumes its
requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host."
The retention logic follows the parable of the talents: high-return businesses
should retain, low-return ones should pay out — but inflation perversely forces even
"bad" businesses to retain every nickel just to stand still. He applauds Volcker
while staying bearish long-term: "Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable
of maintenance, but not of restoration."

**[[Look-Through Earnings]]** are reaffirmed as now exceeding reported operating
earnings; four holdings alone (GEICO, General Foods, R.J. Reynolds, Washington Post)
will contribute well over $35M of unrecorded earnings in 1982. He notes that lack of
control is "often... an economic plus" because mistakes in marketable securities can
be corrected faster than in controlled subsidiaries. On the long-term scorecard,
GAAP book value reached **$526.02** (from $19.46; **21.1% compounded** over 17 years);
over half of 1981's $124M (~31%) net-worth gain came from **[[GEICO]]** alone, whose
market value leapt to $199.8M.

The **insurance** section presents Best's combined-ratio data and forecasts 1982 as
"the worst year in recent history for insurance underwriting," rejecting the tidy
"underwriting cycle" view in favor of large losses becoming the norm. Berkshire's
managers (Liesche, Lyons, Miller, Taylor, Thornton) "swam against the tide,"
sacrificing volume; its [[Financial Strength]] "could become an operational asset of
immense value" should fear ever grip the industry. Finally, the
**[[Shareholder-Designated Contributions]]** program — [[Charlie Munger]]'s idea —
let owners direct Berkshire's charitable giving; 95.6% of eligible shares
participated, directing $1.78M to ~675 charities. Munger is named Vice Chairman and
"partner"; "to almost a sinful degree, we enjoy our work."

## Key figures
- Operating earnings $39.7M; **ROE 15.2%** (down from 17.8%).
- Book value/share: $19.46 → **$526.02** (17 yrs, **21.1% compounded**, GAAP basis).
- 1981 net-worth gain: $124M (~31%), over half from GEICO alone.
- Total common stocks: cost **$351.7M**, market **$639.2M**.
- GEICO: 7.2M shares, cost $47M, **market $199.8M**.
- See's Candies: pre-tax earnings **$21.9M** (up from $15.5M).
- Washington Post market $58.2M; R.J. Reynolds a major new position (mkt $83.1M).
- Best's industry combined ratio: 1981 = 105.7, rising.
- Shareholder-designated gifts: $1.78M to ~675 charities (95.6% participation).
