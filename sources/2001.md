# 2001 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

The first **down year** of Buffett's tenure since 1990's near-miss is far worse: net worth
**fell $3.77 billion** and per-share book value **dropped 6.2%** to **$37,920** (37 years,
22.6% compounded). It still beat the S&P (−11.9%) by 5.7 points — "relative results are what
concern us" — but Buffett is blunt: "my performance was anything but [satisfactory]." The
year is dominated by **September 11th**, which delivered the insurance industry its
**largest loss in history** and exposed an underwriting failure at **[[General Re]]** that
Buffett takes as his own: "I violated the **Noah rule**: Predicting rain doesn't count;
building arks does."

**The size constraint, restated.** Berkshire now needs **"elephants"** to move the needle —
a $10M idea that once "did wonders" (Washington Post 1973, GEICO 1976) would now add "only ¼
of 1%." Buffett reaffirms the formula: grow the existing businesses, add similar-quality
ones, issue shares "only grudgingly." All 2001 purchases were **for cash**.

**The Enron-era candor.** Against the backdrop of Enron — "the symbol for shareholder
abuse" — Buffett distances himself from CEOs who enriched themselves while owners lost
billions (the "**Reprice my options**" party joke). He and Munger take no cash comp,
restricted stock, or options; he keeps "well over 99%" of his net worth in Berkshire and has
"never sold a share."

**Acquisitions of 2001 (all cash).** The 2000-initiated **[[Shaw Industries]]** and
**[[Johns Manville]]** deals closed (Shaw's 12.7% minority later bought out for 4,740 A-share
equivalents → 100%). Four new businesses: **[[MiTek]]** (world leader in connector plates for
roof trusses; Gene Toombs CEO; 55 managers bought 10%, min $100K each — "true owners" who
"can't reprice"); **[[XTRA]]** (truck-trailer leasing, from Julian Robertson's Tiger Fund
liquidation; Lew Rubin CEO; tender expired Sept 11); **[[Larson-Juhl]]** (U.S. leader in
custom picture frames; Craig Ponzio grew it $3M→$300M; closed early 2002); and **[[Fruit of the Loom]]**, bought out of bankruptcy via its debt — a homecoming, since Buffett's old
Graham-Newman employer P&R had bought Union Underwear (the Fruit licensee) in 1955. Buffett
insisted **John Holland** stay as CEO. A bolt-on: **[[H. H. Brown]]** bought Acme Boot's
inventory and trademarks ($700K) — itself a P&R alumnus. Buffett quotes Graham & Dodd's
Horace epigraph: "Many shall be restored that now are fallen…"

**The economics of P/C insurance.** A teaching essay reframing Berkshire around **float** —
"money we hold but don't own" — and its three determinants: amount, cost, and the long-term
outlook for both. Float jumped to **$35,508M**, but a man-made catastrophe pushed the **cost
of float to a staggering 12.8%** (worst since 1984). Three **underwriting principles**:
(1) accept only properly-evaluable risks carrying a profit expectancy (circle of competence;
ignore market share); (2) limit aggregation so no single event threatens solvency; (3) avoid
**moral risk** ("trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work"). General Re
**broke all three** — pricing terrorism off *experience* not *exposure* — and Buffett admits
he saw the danger but "didn't convert thought into action." Berkshire's edge: a balance sheet
and shareholder base that let it "lose $2 billion to $2½ billion in a single event" if
properly paid — "whatever the world's problems, our checks will clear."

**"Loss development" essay.** "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking." Loss reserves
are a *liability estimate*, not a rainy-day fund; **IBNR** (incurred but not reported) makes
them hard. General Re belatedly recognized **$800M** of prior-year loss costs in 2001 — an
honest error that produced underpricing, overstated profits, excess incentive pay, and
premature taxes. Buffett wants "loss development" and "reserve strengthening" scrapped as
euphemisms (the "**rented suit**" funeral parable; insurance accounting as "a **self-graded
exam**"), and rejects proposals to **discount** P/C reserves (the "16-year-old son… normal
sex life" line) — see [[Loss Reserving]].

**Insurance operations.** **[[Ajit Jain]]**, with 18 associates, wrote post-9/11 jumbo
policies (a South American refinery, airline terrorism cover, a North Sea oil platform, the
Sears Tower, the World Cup, the 2002 Winter Olympics) — "impossible to overstate his value."
Joe Brandon (CEO from Sept) and Tad Montross (president) are charged with restoring General
Re's discipline. **[[GEICO]]** earned a **$221M underwriting profit** (premium +6.6%, float
+$308M to $4.25B) under **[[Tony Nicely]]** ("an owner's dream"), the lone disappointment its
flat policy count (preferred +1.6%, standard/non-standard −10.1%, total −0.8%).

**Operating businesses.** **Dexter** dragged shoe operations to a **$46.2M pre-tax loss**;
Buffett owns three Dexter errors — buying it, paying in stock, and procrastinating — and
hands it to Frank Rooney and Jim Issler at H.H. Brown (see [[Dexter Shoe]], [[Mistakes Ledger]]). **[[MidAmerican Energy]]** added Yorkshire Electric (now 3.6M U.K. customers, #2
there) and houses **[[HomeServices of America]]**, the #2 U.S. residential real-estate broker
(Ron Peltier; ~106,000 transactions). **[[FlightSafety International]]** (Al Ueltschi, 84) grew
2.5% amid a post-9/11 commercial-training slump; **NetJets** ([[Executive Jet]]) sold record
planes but ran a small loss on European expansion.

**Investments.** Common stocks: cost $8,543M / **market $28,675M**. Over-$500M holdings:
**Coca-Cola $9,430M** (200M sh, fell), **American Express $5,410M**, **Gillette $3,206M**,
**Wells Fargo $2,315M**, **Washington Post $916M**, and two new positions — **[[Moody's]]
$957M** (24M sh) and **[[H&R Block]] $715M** (16M sh). Buffett doesn't think the group is
undervalued and is "decidedly lukewarm" on stocks generally: "The Great Bubble ended on
**March 10, 2000**" (NASDAQ's 5,132 peak — the same day Berkshire hit its post-1997 low of
$40,800). The market "no more than parallels business progress" ahead.

**Distressed debt.** A more-active year in "junk": **[[FINOVA Group]]** (the $5.6B
**Berkadia** joint venture with **[[Leucadia National]]** — Berkshire guaranteed 90%), Fruit
of the Loom's senior debt (~15% current yield), plus Chrysler Financial, Texaco and RJR
history. Never a newly-issued junk bond. The FINOVA financing brought a déjà-vu **$6B
FleetBoston** syndication (Chad Gifford echoing the 1969 Illinois National Bank loan).
**[[Lou Simpson]]** quietly out-performed Buffett on GEICO's $2B portfolio again.

**Misc.** SDC reached **$16.7M** (cumulative $181M); subsidiary gifts $19.2M. Workforce
**110,000** (+40,000); HQ "14.8" people in 5,246.5 sq ft. New GAAP ends goodwill
amortization going forward (lifting reported, not economic, earnings).

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $37,920, **−6.2%** (net worth **−$3.77B**) — first decline of the
  modern era, but beat the S&P (−11.9%) by 5.7 pts. 37 years, **22.6% compounded**.
- **Operating earnings to Berkshire: −$47M** (a loss); **total earnings $795M** (incl. $842M
  capital gains). Reinsurance underwriting loss **−$4,318M pre-tax / −$2,824M** after tax.
- **Insurance float $35,508M** (GEICO $4,251M / Gen Re $19,310M / Other Reins $11,262M / Other
  Primary $685M); **cost of float 12.8%** (9/11). GEICO underwriting profit **$221M**.
- **General Re:** $800M of prior-year loss costs belatedly recognized; reinsurance the year's
  great wound.
- **Common stocks:** cost $8,543M / **market $28,675M**. Coca-Cola $9,430M, Amex $5,410M,
  Gillette $3,206M, Wells Fargo $2,315M, WaPo $916M, **Moody's $957M (new)**, **H&R Block
  $715M (new)**.
- **Acquisitions (all cash):** MiTek, XTRA, Larson-Juhl, Fruit of the Loom (via debt); Shaw &
  Johns Manville closed; Shaw → 100%.
- **SDC:** $16.7M (cumulative $181M). Workforce 110,000.
