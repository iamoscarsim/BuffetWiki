# 1983 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 14, 1984. With shareholders swelling from ~1,900 to ~2,900 (largely
via the completed [[Blue Chip Stamps]] merger), Buffett opens with a numbered
**catechism of owner-related business principles** — the seed of the later
"Owner's Manual" (see [[Owner Orientation and Candor]]): *our form is corporate but
our attitude is partnership*; directors "eat our own cooking"; the goal is to
maximize **per-share intrinsic business value**, not size; a two-pronged ownership
approach (whole businesses + marketable stocks); a preference to "purchase $2 of
earnings that is not reportable... than $1 that is reportable"; minimal debt; no
managerial "wish list" at owner expense; the **$1 retention test** on a five-year
rolling basis; issue stock only for equal business value; and a refusal to sell
good businesses or play "gin rummy" management.

The "high point of 1983" is the purchase of 90% of **[[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]**
and partnership with **[[Rose Blumkin]]** ("Mrs. B") and her family. Buffett tells
her story — fleeing Russia at 23, $500 of savings, building a single 200,000-sq-ft
store doing >$100M in sales — and offers the line: "I'd rather wrestle grizzlies
than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny." It is "the ideal business — one built
upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional
economics for its owners."

Book value rose 32% to **$975.83** (22.6% compounded over 19 years), but Buffett
sharply distinguishes **[[Intrinsic Business Value]]** from book value: book value
records "what has been put in"; intrinsic value estimates "what can be taken out"
(the two-children's-college analogy). Berkshire's intrinsic value now far exceeds
book, for two reasons — unrealized gains in non-insurance stocks, and, more
importantly, **[[Economic Goodwill]]** vastly exceeding accounting Goodwill. The
famous **Goodwill appendix** lays out the distinction using See's (bought for $25M
on $8M of tangible assets, earning 25% after-tax on tangibles): true economic
Goodwill is the capitalized value of returns above market rates on tangible assets,
it *grows* with inflation while accounting Goodwill is amortized down, and
asset-light franchises are the best inflation hedges — "During inflation, Goodwill
is the gift that keeps giving" (debunking "In Goods We Trust"). Buffett credits
Keynes ("the difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old
ones") for his own shift away from a tangible-asset bias that had caused "many...
mistakes of omission."

Operating highlights: **[[Buffalo Evening News]]** (now the *Buffalo News*, with a
morning edition) turned solidly profitable — #1 weekday penetration among the 100
largest U.S. papers — after the rival Courier-Express closed. **[[See's Candies]]**
posted record profits but flat poundage; Buffett delivers the maxim "**price is
what you give, value is what you get**." Berkshire's own **insurance** had "absolutely
terrible" results (combined ratio **121**) from his earlier decisions, while
**[[GEICO]]** "shot the lights out" (wrote at 96 vs. industry 111; a $21M special
distribution; market value $398M). Berkshire began leveraging its [[Financial Strength]]
into **structured settlements and loss-reserve assumption** (Don Wurster). A long
essay explains **why Berkshire won't split its stock**: split-chasing buyers would
"downgrade the quality of our shareholder group," and a hyperactive market is "the
pickpocket of enterprise" / "an invisible foot" imposing a chair-changing tax. The
Blue Chip merger left shares outstanding at 1,146,909 vs. 1,137,778 in 1965 —
"virtually no net dilution."

## Key figures
- Book value/share: $737.43 → **$975.83** (+32%); 22.6% compounded over 19 yrs.
- Total common stocks: cost **$566M**, market **$1,306M**.
- GEICO market **$398M** (~1/3 stake ≈ $270M premium volume; wrote at 96 vs ind. 111).
- Berkshire's own combined ratio: **121** (underwriting loss $(18.4M) after tax).
- See's: revenues $133.5M, after-tax profit $13.7M, 24.65M lbs, 207 stores.
- Nebraska Furniture Mart: 90% acquired; >$100M sales from one store.
- Net accounting Goodwill $62M; economic Goodwill "far exceeds" it.
