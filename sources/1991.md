# 1991 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

The 1991 letter opens with an outsized result that Buffett is at pains to deflate.
Net worth rose **$2.1 billion, or 39.6%**, lifting per-share book value to **$6,437**
(a 23.7% compounded rate over the 27 years of present management). But nearly **$1.6
billion of that $2.1 billion gain came from a single phenomenon** — a sharp rise in
the price/earnings ratios of [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Gillette]], not from operations.
Buffett frames this as the **"double-dip"** repeating from [[1989]]: earnings growth
*plus* a market re-rating. He believes the re-rating was warranted but cannot recur
annually — "we'll have to settle for a single dip in the future." He restates, now
explicitly, the goal he and [[Charlie Munger]] have set: a **15% average annual
increase in intrinsic value**, which would require earning $22 billion over the
coming decade. Size, he warns again, makes the old rates impossible: where a $1M idea
once moved the needle five points, Berkshire now needs a $370M idea (over $550M
pre-tax) to do the same.

**Salomon ("A Second Job").** The dominant event of Buffett's year was his August 18,
1991 election as **Interim Chairman of [[Salomon]]** following the Treasury-auction
bidding scandal that forced out CEO [[John Gutfreund]]. Where buying Coke was "putting
our money where my mouth was," taking the Salomon job was "putting my mouth where our
money was." He turns the episode into a point about [[Decentralization]]: that he could
step away from Berkshire for months without its progress skipping a beat is itself a
testament to the operating managers (the Blumkins, the Friedmans, Goldberg, the
Heldmans, Huggins, Lipsey, Schey, and new arrival Frank Rooney). His job is "merely to
treat them right and to allocate the capital they generate." He also announces a change
in format: with the businesses now too numerous to cover individually, he will **rotate
coverage**, discussing one or two in depth each year.

**Look-through earnings.** Reiterating the [[Look-Through Earnings]] framework, Buffett
reports that 1991 look-through earnings **declined 14%** (to ~$516M from ~$602M). Two
foreseen forces drove the drop: the secular decline in **media** earnings, and the
**Gillette preferred conversion** (the old $45M after-tax preferred dividend exceeded
the 1991 mix of three months' preferred plus nine months' common look-through). Two
unforeseen forces also hurt: a break-even result at [[Wells Fargo]] (dividends offset
by negative retained earnings) and lower insurance profits. He urges investors to build
portfolios maximizing their own look-through earnings "a decade or so from now":
"to put runs on the scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."

**The franchise/business essay.** The letter's most-cited passage, "A Change in Media
Economics and Some Valuation Math," sharpens [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]] into a
formal definition. **An economic franchise** arises from a product or service that (1) is
needed or desired, (2) is thought to have no close substitute, and (3) is not price-
regulated — conditions that let a company price aggressively and earn high returns, and
that let it "**tolerate mis-management**." A **business**, by contrast, earns exceptional
profits only as a low-cost operator or amid tight supply, and "can be killed by poor
management." Media properties, once bullet-proof franchises, have begun "to resemble
*businesses* more than *franchises*" as entertainment choices proliferate against a fixed
"500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day." He illustrates the valuation
consequence with stark math: a property whose $1M of earnings is expected to grow 6%
forever (no added capital), discounted at 10%, is worth **$25M** (≈16× pre-tax); shift to
a "**bob-around**" expectation and the same $1M is worth only **$10M** (≈6½× pre-tax). The
intrinsic value of Berkshire's media holdings ([[Buffalo Evening News]], [[Washington Post]], [[Capital Cities]]) "has declined materially" — though moderated by their strong
managements and near-zero debt. (See [[Evolving Views — Newspapers]].)

**Twenty Years in a Candy Store.** Marking two decades since [[Blue Chip Stamps]] bought
[[See's Candies]] (Jan 3, 1972), Buffett reprises the deal as the education that taught
him the value of an [[Economic Moat]] / franchise. The true purchase price was $30M (a $40M
ask less $10M excess cash); he and Munger, "not yet fully appreciative of the value of an
economic franchise," nearly blew it by capping their bid at $25M against $7M of tangible
net worth. From 1972 to 1991, See's pre-tax profit grew from $4.2M to **$42.4M** while
its net worth rose only from $7M to $25M — meaning ~$18M of reinvestment supported the
growth and ~$410M of pre-tax profit was distributed upstream to deploy elsewhere. 1991
sales matched 1990 in dollars (down 4% in pounds, all in the last two months), yet
profits grew 7% to a record **21.6% pre-tax margin**, despite a California recession and a
new "snack food" sales tax (occasion for the famous taxable/non-taxable food table and
the melted-Milky-Way koan).

**H. H. Brown.** The year's one sizable acquisition (closed 7/1/91) was **[[H. H. Brown]]**,
the leading North American maker of work shoes and boots, run by CEO **[[Frank Rooney]]**
(son-in-law of founder **[[Ray Heffernan]]**, who had bought the company for $10,000 in
1927). Brown earned ~$25M pre-tax and had "one of the most unusual compensation systems
I've encountered": key managers draw a $7,800 salary plus a percentage of profits **after
a charge for capital employed**, so they "truly stand in the shoes of owners" — a model
contrasting the usual "carrots but… no sticks" plans that treat equity as cost-free.
Buffett re-runs the acquisition **want-ad** (the six criteria) and notes wryly that of
four major purchases sold by investment banks, the bank thought of Berkshire in only one
case: "When the phone don't ring, you'll know it's me."

**Insurance.** Underwriting swung to a **$119.6M loss** (cost of float **6.31%**, still
below the 7.40% government long-bond yield; Berkshire beat the government's rate in 20 of
25 years). Float grew to ~**$1.895 billion**. Berkshire is "perhaps the largest" writer of
**super-cat** reinsurance ([[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]), priced to pay out ~90% of
premiums over time but wildly volatile year to year. The biggest 1991 catastrophe was not
the Oakland fire or Hurricane Bob but a September **typhoon in Japan** ($4–5B industry
loss, surpassing Hurricane Hugo). Buffett warns that "innocents" — reinsurers who don't
understand the risk — will eventually return and crater pricing.

**Marketable stocks & the Keynes letter.** The over-$100M holdings: Cap Cities/ABC,
Coca-Cola, Freddie Mac, GEICO, Gillette, **[[Guinness]]** (a new position — Berkshire's
first significant investment in a non-U.S. company), Washington Post, and Wells Fargo. The
"Rip Van Winkle"/"stay-put" approach is defended with [[Concentration]] doctrine and a long
1934 **John Maynard Keynes** letter to F. C. Scott on putting "fairly large sums into
enterprises which one thinks one knows something about." Calling active institutional
trading "investing" is "like calling someone who repeatedly engages in one-night stands a
romantic."

**Mistake Du Jour.** Abandoning his plan to wait until 2015, Buffett confesses a fresh
error of omission: in early 1988 he set out to buy 30M shares of **[[Fannie Mae]]**, bought
~7M, balked when the price rose, and then sold even those — forgoing an estimated **$1.4
billion** gain. "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation… But there isn't one."

**Fixed income & USAir.** Portfolio changes: the Gillette preferred was converted; an RJR
issue was eliminated; new holdings in **[[First Empire State]]** (a $40M 9% convertible
preferred, out of "enormous respect" for CEO **[[Bob Wilmers]]**) and an **[[American Express]]** "PERC" ($300M, 8.85%, mandatory conversion); added to **ACF Industries**.
[[USAir]] was marked down sharply as the airline industry was "decimated" (Midway, Pan
Am, America West, then Continental and TWA into bankruptcy); new CEO **[[Seth Schofield]]**
fights for survival amid the "burn-the-furniture" economics of bankrupt carriers. Buffett
restates that no industry, in aggregate, has earned money "since its birth after Kitty Hawk."

**Miscellaneous.** Shareholder-designated contributions reached **$6.8M** (97.7%
participation, 2,630 charities). Director **[[Malcolm Chace]]** Jr. (88) steps down; his son
**Kim Chace** is nominated to continue the Chace family's association with Berkshire (which
dates to the 1965 share block that gave the Buffett partnership control). Nicholas Kenner,
the young shareholder, again needles Buffett over an arithmetic error.

## Key figures

- **Net worth gain 1991:** $2.1 billion, **+39.6%**. ~$1.6B of it from Coke + Gillette P/E
  expansion.
- **Book value per share:** $6,437 (from $19 in 1964) — **23.7% compounded** over 27 years.
- **Equity capital:** $7.4 billion total.
- **Intrinsic-value goal:** 15%/yr (would need ~$22B earned over the next decade).
- **Look-through earnings:** ~$516M (**−14%** from ~$602M in 1990).
- **See's:** sales $196M, pre-tax profit **$42.4M** (vs $4.2M in 1972); net worth $25M;
  record **21.6%** pre-tax margin; 26M lbs sold.
- **H. H. Brown:** ~$25M pre-tax earnings; acquired 7/1/91; contributed $13.6M pre-tax /
  $8.6M to Berkshire in H2.
- **Insurance:** underwriting loss **$(119.6M)**; average float **$1,895.0M**; cost of
  float **6.31%** vs. 7.40% long-bond yield. Industry combined ratio 109.1.
- **Operating earnings:** $400.5M pre-tax / $315.8M to Berkshire. Total earnings (incl.
  $192.5M security-sale gains) $593.0M pre-tax / $439.9M to Berkshire.
- **Major stock holdings (cost → market, $000s):** Coca-Cola 1,023,920 → **3,747,675**;
  Cap Cities/ABC 517,500 → 1,300,500; Gillette 600,000 → 1,347,000; GEICO 45,713 →
  1,363,150; Freddie Mac 77,245 → 343,090; Guinness 264,782 → 296,755; Washington Post
  9,731 → 336,050; Wells Fargo 289,431 → 290,000.
- **Fannie Mae omission:** ~$1.4 billion of forgone gain.
- **Shareholder-designated contributions:** $6.8M; 97.7% participation; 2,630 charities.
