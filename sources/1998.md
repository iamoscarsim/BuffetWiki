# 1998 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$25.9 billion**, lifting per-share book value **48.3%** to **$37,801**
(34 years, **24.7%** compounded) — but Buffett warns it "was not as good as it looks"
(like Wagner, "better than it sounds"). The reason: **most of the 48.3% came from issuing
shares in acquisitions**. Because Berkshire trades at a large premium to book, issuing
stock instantly *raises* per-share book value (more book received than given up) without
any matching gain in per-share **intrinsic value** ("what we give and what we get are
roughly equal"). Intrinsic value grew "very substantially" but well short of 48.3% — and
still far exceeds book. Net worth reached **$57.4B**, the largest of any U.S. corporation;
the **15% intrinsic-value goal** is "at the very peak of possible outcomes" (15% over five
years = +$58B). "Popcorn stands just won't do" — Berkshire hunts **elephants**.

**Two big acquisitions.** **[[General Re]]** ($22B, closed Dec 21) — 100% of the largest
U.S. P&C reinsurer plus 82% of **Cologne Re** (the world's oldest), operating in 124
countries under CEO **[[Ron Ferguson]]**. And **[[Executive Jet]]** (EJA / NetJets, $725M,
closed Aug 7) — the **fractional-jet-ownership** leader created by **[[Rich Santulli]]**.
GEICO "shot the lights out." On the minus side, several major public investees had
"operating shortfalls," so Berkshire's **equity portfolio underperformed the S&P 500** —
problems Buffett believes are "almost certainly temporary."

**The two-column table** (now including General Re pro forma): investments hit
**$47,647/share** (+25.2%), while pre-tax operating earnings per share *fell* 33.9% to
**$474.45** — both moved by General Re, whose huge investments swelled column one while a
1998 underwriting loss dented column two (absent Gen Re, operating earnings would have
risen modestly). Over 1968–98 the two compounded 25.4% and 18.6%.

**Taxes.** Berkshire and General Re paid (or will soon pay) **$2.7B** of 1998 federal
income tax — "all of the U.S. Government's expenses for more than a half-day." Buffett
corrects two misconceptions: corporations pay 35% on capital gains (so Berkshire's tax on
a long-term gain is 75% higher than an individual's), and the dividends-received exclusion
for its insurance subs is **59.5%**, not 70%. A record **$30M SEC fee** went with the Gen
Re share issuance.

**GEICO.** "Combine a great idea with a great manager … there is no one in the business
world who could run GEICO better than **[[Tony Nicely]]**." Marketing spend went from
$33M (1995) to **$143M** (1998, with counselors 652 → 2,162); new auto policies hit
**1,317,761** and in-force **3,562,644**; 1999 marketing will be ≥**$190M** with "no
limit" (the test: ">$1 of value per dollar spent"). The 1998 profit margin of **6.7%** was
"better than we wished," so GEICO cut rates 3.3% (toward the 4% target). Profit-sharing
reached **$103M / 32.3% of salary** for 9,313 associates — "may well [be] the highest
percentage payment at any large company." Market share rose only **3% → 3.5%** — "for
every policyholder we now have, there are another ten." Comp is 50% seasoned-book earnings,
50% policyholder growth. The "commercial to managers": Berkshire frees its CEOs to **"run
your business as if (1) you own 100% of it; (2) it is the only asset your family will ever
have; and (3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century"** — ignoring accounting
considerations entirely.

**Executive Jet / NetJets.** [[Rich Santulli]] "created the fractional ownership industry
in 1986": buy, say, 1/8th of a jet for 100 annual flight hours, summon it like a taxi at
5,500 airports. Largest operator (>1,000 customers, 163 aircraft, ~650 pilots trained by
fellow-subsidiary [[FlightSafety International]]); offers multiple manufacturers (Boeing,
Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna, Raytheon) vs. single-make competitors. Buffett, a delighted
customer (introduced by H.H. Brown's [[Frank Rooney]]), **sold the Berkshire plane**. Aunt
Alice on the fur coat: "*You* aren't buying it; your heirs are."

**General Re — why it fits.** A reinsurer "gets paid for absorbing the volatility that the
client insurer wants to shed," yet a *public* reinsurer is itself graded on smooth results,
forcing costly retrocessions or rejected business. Berkshire — "a **Fort Knox of capital**"
that "happily accepts volatility" — is "the perfect structure for writing, and *retaining*,
reinsurance in virtually any amount." General Re supplies the distribution, technical
skill, and international reach; Berkshire supplies balance-sheet strength and freedom from
quarterly-earnings pressure. "We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." (Gen Re even
"played a key role in the resuscitation of GEICO" in 1976.)

**Float.** The float table (every fifth year + the last) runs from $17M (1967) to **$22,762M
at year-end 1998** — Gen Re is two-thirds of the total. Float has compounded **25.4%/yr**
at a cost "well under zero" over 32 years: "though our net float is recorded … as a
liability, it has had more economic value to us than an equal amount of net worth … float
will continue to outrank net worth in value."

**Portfolio & a confession.** Amex was slightly increased; the other two top holdings left
alone; many smaller positions trimmed or cut. **Selling McDonald's "was a very big
mistake"** — "you would have been better off … if I had regularly snuck off to the movies."
Year-end cash topped **$15B** ("Cash never makes us happy"). Gen Re's equity portfolio was
liquidated on the merger (~250 stocks, $935M of tax) — "we don't back into decisions." On
the prior year's [[Unconventional Commitments]], Buffett reverts to a "never-comment"
policy (the 1997 silver disclosure had been regulator-prompted).

**Accounting (two essays).** *Part 1:* replacing General Re's stock-option plan with an
**economically identical cash plan** added $63M of reported expense — proof that GAAP lets
managers "pay employees in one form and count the cost, or pay them in another form and
ignore the cost" (Bishop Berkeley; the GEICO-advertising-paid-in-options thought
experiment). Buffett marks down option-issuers' earnings by 5–10% when investing. *Part 2:*
restructuring and merger accounting are "even worse" — the "big bath," the golf-scorecard
trick (140, 80, 80, 80, 80), and the P&C **loss-reserve-inflation** ploy ("We'll make it
bigger than that — that's why we're doing the deal"). R.G. Associates counted **1,369
special charges totaling $72.1B** in 1998. Buffett endorses SEC Chairman **Arthur Levitt**'s
campaign against "earnings management" — "Bad accounting drives out good" (Son of Gresham).

**Misc.** Scott Fetzer set a record under **[[Ralph Schey]]** ($96.5M after-tax on $112M
net worth). [[Ajit Jain]] launched online **annuity** products and "over the last decade
has personally accounted for a significant portion of Berkshire's operating earnings." SDC
reached **$16.9M** (97.5%; 18-year cumulative $130M). The Gen Re merger added ~**60,000
shareholders**; the annual meeting needs two venues.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $37,801, **+48.3%** (net worth **+$25.9B**; total **$57.4B**) — but
  most of the gain came from share issuance, *not* intrinsic-value growth. 34 years, 24.7%.
- **Acquisitions:** **General Re** ($22B; closed 12/21/98) and **Executive Jet/NetJets**
  ($725M, equal cash and stock; closed 8/7/98).
- **Two-column table:** investments **$47,647/share** (+25.2%); pre-tax operating earnings
  ex-investments **$474.45/share** (−33.9%, on Gen Re's underwriting loss).
- **Insurance:** year-end float **$22,762M** (Gen Re ⅔); float compounded 25.4%/yr at sub-zero
  cost. GEICO underwriting profit margin **6.7%**; profit-sharing **$103M / 32.3%**.
- **GEICO:** new auto policies **1,317,761**; in-force **3,562,644**; market share 3% → 3.5%;
  1999 marketing ≥$190M.
- **Operating earnings** ~$1,899M pre-tax / **$1,277M to Berkshire**; capital gains $2,415M /
  $1,553M; **total $4,314M / $2,830M**. Scott Fetzer record $96.5M after-tax.
- **Common stocks:** cost $7,044M / **market $37,265M**. Coca-Cola $13,400M, Amex $5,180M
  (50.5M sh), Gillette $4,590M (96M sh post-split), Freddie Mac $3,885M, Wells Fargo $2,540M
  (63.6M post-split), Disney $1,536M (stock fell), WaPo $999M. **McDonald's sold** ("a very
  big mistake").
- **Taxes:** $2.7B federal income (Berkshire + Gen Re); $30M record SEC fee. **SDC:** $16.9M
  (97.5%; cumulative $130M).
