# 2003 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$13.6 billion**, lifting per-share book value **21%** to **$50,498** (39 years,
22.2% compounded); intrinsic value grew "somewhat more." Buffett flags a structural shift: equity
holdings have fallen from an average **114% of net worth in the 1980s to ~50% in 2000–03**, so
stock-market swings now move a much smaller part of Berkshire — and book value is a less precise
single-year yardstick (he prints an operating-earnings growth table: 22.8% compounded 1968–2003).
The hunt for "significantly undervalued stocks" is "greatly accentuated by the mushrooming of the
funds we must deploy" — Berkshire's capital is "underutilized now… a painful condition… but not as
painful as doing something stupid."

**Acquisitions.** Two big ones, both via Buffett's signature serendipity:
- **[[Clayton Homes]]** — the class act of manufactured housing, sourced when **Dr. Al Auxier**'s
  University of Tennessee finance students gave Buffett **[[Jim Clayton]]**'s autobiography; a call
  to son and CEO **[[Kevin Clayton]]** ("able and a straight-shooter") sealed it. Buffett had learned
  the industry's "atrocious consumer-financing practices" the hard way, via [[Clayton Homes|Oakwood Homes]] junk debt (Oakwood went bankrupt; Clayton later bought its assets). A graduation
  ceremony rewarded the students with shares.
- **[[McLane]]** — the **$23 billion**-sales grocery/non-food distributor, bought from **Wal-Mart**
  (sourced by **[[Byron Trott]]** of Goldman Sachs, his third Berkshire deal). Paper-thin ~1% pre-tax
  margins, CEO **Grady Rosier**; a two-hour meeting, a handshake, no due diligence, money in 29 days.

**Taxes.** Berkshire will pay **$3.3 billion** of federal income tax on 2003 income — ~**2½% of all
U.S. corporate income tax** (vs. a ~1% share of all corporate market value). After his *Washington
Post* op-ed against the Bush tax cuts, a Treasury official jabbed at "a certain midwestern oracle…
who has played the tax code like a fiddle." Buffett notes corporate taxes had fallen to **7.4%** of
federal receipts (from 32% in 1952): "If class warfare is being waged in America, **my class is
clearly winning**."

**Governance.** The mutual-fund scandal (Eliot Spitzer's housecleaning) vindicated the prior year's
"independent"-director critique — directors who "routinely rehired… however pathetic" the manager
and "mindlessly approved fees." See [[Corporate Governance]]. **Berkshire's own board** is the
counter-model: 11 directors, each (with family) owning **>$4M** of stock bought in the market (no
options/restricted grants), **no D&O insurance** — "**owner-capitalism**" ("who ever washes a rental
car?"). Four new directors joined: **[[David Gottesman]]**, **Charlotte Guyman**, **[[Don Keough]]**,
and **[[Tom Murphy]]**. The board's "primary job" is choosing a successor — and **four internal CEO
candidates** have been identified.

**Insurance — a "standout."** Float reached a record **$44,220M** at **no cost**, with a **$1.7
billion pre-tax underwriting profit** across all segments.
- **[[General Re]]** "is fixed" (Joe Brandon, Tad Montross); with **[[National Indemnity Company]]**
  it is now one of only two AAA-rated major reinsurers as rivals were downgraded — "there are no
  promises… equaling those offered by Gen Re and National Indemnity."
- **[[Ajit Jain]]** (staff of 23) wrote PepsiCo's **$1 billion** prize-drawing policy (PV ~$250M;
  no winner on Sept 14). His non-retroactive book earned **+$1,434M**.
- **[[GEICO]]**: premiums **$2.2B (1992) → $8.1B**; market share **2.1% → 5.0%**; preferred
  policies +8.2%, standard/non-standard +21.4%; underwriting profit **$452M**. A new **Buffalo**
  service center (2,500 jobs; Gov. Pataki and **[[Stan Lipsey]]** brokered it).

**Regulated utilities ([[MidAmerican Energy]], 80.5%).** UK distribution (Yorkshire + Northern
Electric, 3.7M customers — 3rd-largest UK distributor), Iowa (689,000), and the Kern River +
**[[Northern Natural Gas]]** pipelines (7.8% of U.S. gas). Partners Sokol, **[[Greg Abel]]**, and
**[[Walter Scott Jr]]** (the controlling vote, per PUHCA). A free-enterprise "tidbit": UK
denationalization cut Northern/Yorkshire headcount from 6,800 to 2,539 while serving more
customers. **[[HomeServices of America]]** did **$48.6B** of transactions (16,343 brokers, 16 firms).

**Finance & financial products.** **[[General Re Securities]]** still had 7,580 tickets / 453
counterparties; losses **$99M** (2003) after $173M (2002) — and a notable **confession**: "Charlie
would have moved swiftly… I, however, dithered," costing ~$100M. Freddie Mac's accounting
"shenanigans of mind-blowing size" reinforced the [[Derivatives]] warning. The **Berkadia/[[FINOVA Group]]** $5.6B guarantee was extinguished (Leucadia's Joe Steinberg and Ian Cumming). Clayton's
finance arm will **retain its loans**, funded by Berkshire borrowing and re-lending at cost +1% ("**every
tub on its own bottom**"). Also: **Value Capital** (Mark Byrne, $604M LP stake), leasing (XTRA, CORT,
weak).

**Investments & the dollar.** Common stocks cost $8,515M / market **$35,287M**; new positions
**[[PetroChina]]** ($1,340M, 1.3%) and **HCA** ($665M). Among the six largest holdings, no change
in Coca-Cola since 1994, Amex since 1998, Gillette since 1989, WaPo since 1973 — "Brokers don't love
us." Confession: "I made a big mistake in **not selling** several of our larger holdings during The
Great Bubble." Junk bonds (bought ~$8B in 2002) "now look decidedly unattractive — yesterday's weeds
are today priced as flowers." The headline macro move: Berkshire **entered the foreign-currency
market** (first time ever, 2002) and **enlarged it in 2003 to ~$12 billion of FX contracts** across
five currencies — Buffett "increasingly bearish on the dollar" because the **U.S. trade deficit** is
"force-feeding… claims on America to the rest of the world" (see [[Trade Deficit and the Dollar]]).
FX contracts contributed **$825M** of the year's $4,129M realized gains.

**Designated-gifts program terminated.** After **$197M** over 1981–2002, Berkshire **ended** its
unique [[Shareholder-Designated Contributions]] program in 2003 — abortion-issue boycotts had begun
to hurt **[[The Pampered Chef]]** associates ("we see nothing charitable in harming decent,
hard-working people"). Berkshire now makes **no parent-level contributions**.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $50,498, **+21%** (net worth +$13.6B); 39 years, **22.2% compounded**.
  Equity now ~50% of net worth (vs. ~114% in the 1980s).
- **Insurance:** record **float $44,220M** at no cost; **+$1.7B underwriting profit** (Gen Re +$145M,
  Ajit ex-retroactive +$1,434M / retroactive −$387M, GEICO +$452M, Other Primary +$74M).
- **Taxes:** **$3.3B** (≈2½% of all U.S. corporate income tax).
- **MidAmerican (80.5%):** EBIT $1,076M; $1.578B junior debt to Berkshire at 11%. HomeServices
  $48.6B transactions.
- **Manufacturing/Service/Retail:** revenues **$32,106M** (incl. McLane), **20.7%** on tangible net
  worth / 9.2% on carrying value. Shaw record (+ Dixie Group → ~$5B); Fruit 42.3% of men's/boys'
  underwear.
- **Common stocks:** cost $8,515M / market **$35,287M** (Coca-Cola $10,150M, Amex $7,312M, Wells
  Fargo $3,324M, Gillette $3,526M, WaPo $1,367M, Moody's $1,453M, **PetroChina $1,340M new**, H&R
  Block $809M, M&T $659M, **HCA $665M new**). **~$12B of foreign-currency contracts.**
- **Gen Re Securities:** −$99M (2003). **Designated gifts terminated** (cumulative $197M).
