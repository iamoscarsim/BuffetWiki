# 1990 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 1991. After the prior year's warning, net worth nearly fell — "during
much of 1990's second half we were on the road to… proving that forecast accurate" —
but a late rally left net worth up **$362M (+7.3%)**, lifting book value to
**$4,612.06** (23.2% compounded over 26 years). The "lackluster" year reflected the
flat-to-down prices of the **"permanent four"** (Cap Cities/ABC, Coca-Cola, GEICO,
Washington Post): the two media names fell on "evolutionary industry developments,"
while Coca-Cola rose. Buffett reiterates the 15% intrinsic-value goal and the wish that
Berkshire stock "sell consistently at about intrinsic value" (granddaughter Emily and
"Beemer the Clown" illustrate his modest role: "Gee, I'm really good at this").

A **"Look-Through Earnings"** essay (with Ben Graham's unpublished 1936 accounting
satire as an appendix) refines the measure: take ~$250M of investees' retained
earnings, subtract ~$30M of hypothetical dividend tax, add the $220M to $371M reported
operating earnings → **~$590M of 1990 look-through earnings**. The extreme case:
Berkshire's 17% of Cap Cities earned it $83M, of which GAAP recorded only ~$530K. "We
care not whether the auditors hear a tree fall in the forest; we do care who owns the
tree." When Coca-Cola buys back stock, it raises Berkshire's share of "the most valuable
franchise in the world" more tax-efficiently than a dividend would.

The non-insurance group earned **51% on average equity** (top-20 on the Fortune 500),
unleveraged, "from a group of businesses operating in such prosaic fields as furniture
retailing, candy, vacuum cleaners, and even steel warehousing… Our extraordinary returns
flow from outstanding operating managers, not fortuitous industry economics."
**[[Borsheim's]]** — sales +18% in a weak retail year; likely the largest U.S. jewelry
volume after Tiffany's NY flagship; 18% operating costs vs. ~40% at rivals ("What works
with diapers works with diamonds" — the Wal-Mart analogy); a national mail-order
"honor system." **NFM** — $159M sales; 15% costs vs. Levitz's 40%; customers driving
130 miles from Des Moines; Buffett's "**counterrevelation**" on *synergy* when a See's
candy cart outsold full See's stores. **See's** — record volume (barely; Kuwait dented
mall traffic); the Albuquerque store saved by 263 customer letters (manager Ann
Filkins; [[Chuck Huggins]]'s thank-you ad). The **media** discussion is pivotal:
Buffett concedes media businesses "will prove considerably less marvelous than I, the
industry, or lenders thought" — more ad channels and slower ad-dollar growth have
**permanently reduced** the intrinsic value of the Buffalo News and the media
investees; the **[[Buffalo Evening News]]** held up (–5%) with a 52.3% news hole.
**Fechheimer** — George Heldman (69) retires; 42 stores in 22 states. **Scott Fetzer**
— [[Ralph Schey]]'s finance unit earned a record $12.2M; Kirby's "Generation 3" vacuum
a success; Campbell Hausfeld record $109M.

**Insurance** is the heart of the letter. The combined ratio rose to ~110 and will keep
rising — "the most important thing to do when you find yourself in a hole is to stop
digging," but managers "continue to dig — sullenly but vigorously." Two landmark
essays: (1) **Super-catastrophe reinsurance** — Berkshire has become "the largest U.S.
writer of super-cat business," selling reinsurers protection against rare mega-events;
because it *concentrates* rather than spreads risk, its super-cat combined ratio "will
be close to either zero or 300%," producing "a lumpy 15%… [we] always have preferred
[to] a smooth 12%." (2) **Measuring insurance performance** via the **cost of float**:
a 24-year table of underwriting loss vs. average float shows Berkshire developed funds
**below the government's borrowing cost in 19 of 24 years** (1990: ~$1.6B float at a
~1.6% cost). [[GEICO]] (now 48%-owned) does even better — a cost of float "considerably
less than zero." See [[Insurance Float]] and [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]].

**Marketable securities**: "**Lethargy bordering on sloth** remains the cornerstone of
our investment style" — Berkshire didn't touch five of its six big holdings. The
exception is the year's major new investment: **[[Wells Fargo]]** — ~10% of the bank for
**$290M** (under 5× after-tax earnings) amid a panic over California real estate,
backing managers **[[Carl Reichardt]]** and Paul Hazen (likened to Murphy and Burke at
Cap Cities). Buffett dislikes banking's 20:1 leverage but will "buy into well-managed
banks at fair prices," and welcomes the price drop as a chance to buy more — the classic
**net-buyer** logic ("we will be buying businesses… year in, year out… declining prices
benefit us"; "it's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer"). He also added to
**RJR Nabisco** bonds ($440M) and delivered a withering essay on **junk bonds** — the
"manufactured junk" of the 1980s vs. genuine "fallen angels," the "dagger on the
steering wheel," and Graham's three-word secret: "**Margin of Safety**" (see
[[Margin of Safety]]).

The four **convertible preferreds** are held ([[Salomon]] $700M, [[Gillette]] $600M,
[[USAir]] $358M, [[Champion International]] $300M). Gillette's preferred was called —
converting April 1 into 12M common shares. USAir is candidly marked down: "your
Chairman displayed exquisite timing… an 'unforced error'" — "in a business selling a
commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest
competitor." The section closes with a moving eulogy for **[[Colman Mockler]]**, the
Gillette CEO, who died in January 1991. Also: **[[Ken Chace]]** retires from the board
(75), recalled warmly ("I made no mistake in choosing Ken"); **Susan Buffett** is
nominated to succeed him and would control Berkshire if she outlives Buffett (whose
estate is "aimed at… returning the fortune to society"); the jet is again "The
Indefensible"; the shareholder-designated program gave **$5.8M** to 2,600 charities.

## Key figures
- Net worth +$362M (**+7.3%**); book value/share **$4,612.06** (26 yrs, 23.2%).
- Operating earnings to Berkshire **$370.7M**; total earnings $394.1M; look-through ~**$590M**.
- Non-insurance group earned **51% on average equity**, unleveraged.
- Insurance cost of float ~**1.6%** in 1990 (below gov't cost in 19 of 24 years); ~$1.6B float.
- Holdings >$100M: Cap Cities/ABC **$1,377.4M**, [[Coca-Cola]] **$2,171.6M** (cost $1,023.9M;
  46.7M shares post-split), Freddie Mac **$117.0M**, GEICO **$1,110.6M** (48%-owned),
  WaPo **$342.1M**, **[[Wells Fargo]]** **$289.4M** (~10%, cost $289.4M).
- RJR Nabisco bonds $440M. Four preferreds: $1.958B face (Salomon, Gillette, USAir, Champion).
- Gillette preferred called → 12M common shares (April 1, 1991).
