# 1992 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Book value per share rose **20.3%** in 1992 to **$7,745** (23.6% compounded over 28
years); net worth grew **$1.52 billion**, more than 98% from earnings and portfolio
appreciation and the rest from shares issued on the conversion of Berkshire's
zero-coupon convertible debentures. Those 5½% debentures (issued [[1989]]) were **called
for January 4, 1993 redemption**: of $476M outstanding, $25M converted before year-end,
$46M in January, and $405M was redeemed for cash (conversions at $11,719/share, 6,106
shares issued). Berkshire now has **1,152,547 shares** — strikingly close to the
1,137,778 outstanding on October 1, 1964, when the Buffett Partnership took control.

**Volatility, the S&P, and two predictions.** Buffett uses the year to frame Berkshire's
lumpy results: because GAAP marks the insurers' equity portfolios to market, ordinary
price swings make annual results "gyrate." Comparing Berkshire's per-share gain to the
S&P 500, he flags a **structural tax drag** (an 18% pre-tax S&P gain nets a corporate
holder under 13%) and offers two predictions: that the S&P's next decade will return
"far less" than the past decade's, and that Berkshire's "**expanding capital base**" will
"substantially reduce" its historical edge over the index — "the only open question is
whether we can drag the anchor along at some tolerable, though slowed, pace." He embraces
the volatility his [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance|super-cat]] commitment and
[[Concentration|concentrated]] holdings create: "our performance yardstick is **slugging
percentage, not batting average**."

**The Salomon interlude ends.** Buffett stepped down as [[Salomon]] Interim Chairman in
June 1992 after ten months — "delighted to be back full-time" ("there is no job in the
world that is more fun than running Berkshire"). Salomon ranked second of 311 firms in
*Fortune's* survey for most-improved reputation, and Salomon Brothers posted record
pre-tax earnings (+34%). He singles out executives **Deryck Maughan, [[Bob Denham]], Don
Howard, and John Macfarlane** — without whom "the firm very probably would not have
survived" — and lead lawyer **[[Ron Olson]]** of Munger, Tolles & Olson, who navigated
five federal authorities.

**Acquisitions.** The toads-and-princesses lecture on [[Premium Takeovers]] returns
("they kiss and the toads croak"; "*Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes
permanent*"), with the resolution to buy "**good businesses at fair prices rather than
fair businesses at good prices**." The year's purchase, a "prototype," was **82% of
[[Central States Indemnity]]** (Dec 1992), an Omaha credit-card payment-protection
insurer (~$90M premiums, ~$10M profit) run by **[[Bill Kizer]]**, a 35-year friend whose
family retains 18% — explicitly likened to the 1967 [[National Indemnity Company|National Indemnity]] purchase from [[Jack Ringwalt]]. Five **add-on** acquisitions were made by
subsidiaries; the largest, **[[H. H. Brown]]'s** purchase of **Lowell Shoe** (Nursemates,
$90M sales), is held up as the low-risk way to "enlarge the domain of managers we already
know to be outstanding."

**Look-through earnings** reached **$604 million** in 1992; Buffett sets a public marker —
they "will need to grow to more than $1.8 billion by the year 2000" to hit the 15% goal —
while warning against managers who hide poor results behind "long-term focus" ("It must
come sometimes to jam *today*").

**Insurance & Hurricane Andrew.** Andrew, "the largest insured loss in history," added
~4 points to the industry's 1992 combined ratio (114.8) and exposed insurers whose
catastrophe reinsurance "was far from adequate" — "it's only when the tide goes out that
you learn who's been swimming naked." Berkshire's super-cat book took ~**$125M** of Andrew
losses (≈ its whole year's super-cat premium), leaving a net super-cat result of just
−$2M; investee [[GEICO]] lost ~$50M to Andrew (Berkshire's look-through share ~$25M). Both
1991 and 1992 came in near break-even, which Buffett calls "aberrations," reaffirming his
forecast of "huge annual swings." He details super-cat mechanics (a retention trigger
*plus* an industry-loss threshold, usually $3B+; geographic limits; "second-event"
policies; one contract obliging a $100M payout), notes Berkshire is **second in U.S. P&C
net worth** (behind State Farm) and **rejects more than 98%** of business offered, and
credits **[[Ajit Jain]]** ("simply the best in this business"). Conservative deferred-
revenue accounting means super-cat **profits show only in Q4**. The cost-of-float table now
spans 26 years: 1992's cost of float was **4.76%** on average float of **$2,290.4M**,
again below the 7.39% government long-bond yield (Berkshire beat the government in 21 of
26 years). [[National Indemnity Company|National Indemnity]] (Don Wurster) and the
homestate operation (Rod Eldred) wrote at underwriting profits.

**The value/growth essay & General Dynamics.** Restating the [[1977]] equity-selection
creed (now softening "a *very* attractive price" to "an attractive price"), Buffett
delivers a landmark passage: "**growth and value investing are joined at the hip**" —
growth is simply a variable in the value calculation. "Value investing" is "redundant";
paying more than calculated value in hope of a higher resale is **speculation**. He
invokes **[[John Burr Williams]]'** *Theory of Investment Value* — the value of any asset
is its future cash flows discounted at an appropriate rate — and stresses [[Circle of Competence|sticking to understandable businesses]] ("how realistically they define what
they don't know") and a [[Margin of Safety]] ("the cornerstone of investment success").
The new common-stock holding, **[[General Dynamics]]**, began as an arbitrage on a 1992
Dutch tender but, after Buffett studied CEO **[[Bill Anders]]'** capital-return strategy,
became a 14% long-term position — a notable case of [[Arbitrage]] converting into
investment. (On selling: the traveler's guidebook, "Things to do in Podunk: You're doing
it.")

**Fixed income & USAir.** Berkshire added to ACF debentures, had some WPPSS called, and
sold RJR Nabisco ($80M of 1992 fixed-income gains). Buffett concedes that the negotiated
**preferreds underperformed** open-market purchases — as expected, since the secondary
market ("periodically ruled by mass folly") offers bargains the new-issue market never
will. On [[USAir]]: "even my mother acknowledges" his 1989 confession of ignorance about
airline economics; he praises CEO [[Seth Schofield]]'s "extraordinary" repositioning
(including weathering a fall 1992 strike) and notes British Airways' early-1993 minority
investment, on which Buffett and Munger joined the USAir board (his fifth).

**Accounting essays.** Two new rules: a deferred-tax change (all unrealized appreciation —
$7.6B at year-end — now accrued at 34%, cutting net worth ~$70M in Q1 1993) and mandatory
recognition of **post-retirement health liabilities**, which Berkshire largely avoided
(its 1982 blown deal — see [[Mistakes Ledger]] — would have saddled it with exactly such
obligations; the buyer that took it went bankrupt). The Lincoln riddle: "How many legs
does a dog have if you call his tail a leg? Four — because calling a tail a leg does not
make it a leg." The centerpiece is a **plea to expense stock options**: "If options aren't
a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And,
if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they
go?" He mocks the Council of Institutional Investors' "no cash-no cost" theory (offering
to sell insurance for options) and warns the business elite "risks losing its credibility…
when it advocates the incredible on issues of significance to itself." (See [[Executive Compensation]], [[Owner Orientation and Candor]].)

**Miscellaneous.** Assistant Gladys Kaiser (25 years) and CFO **Verne McKenzie** (30
years; now consultant, succeeded by **Marc Hamburg**) step back. **[[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]]**, at 99, rejoins Berkshire — selling her building/land to [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]
and (this time) signing a **non-compete at 99**. [[Scott Fetzer]] earned a record **$110M
pre-tax on just $116M of equity** under [[Ralph Schey]], distributing >100% of earnings
to Berkshire over seven years. Corporate overhead fell to $4.2M (1991 carried a one-time
pre-1970 textile environmental charge); after-tax overhead is under 1% of operating
earnings, with no legal/PR/HR/strategic-planning departments. Berkshire stock crossed
**$10,000**; the no-split policy is reaffirmed, with gift-tax workarounds offered. SDC
reached **$7.6M** (97% participation, 2,810 charities), and Buffett floats raising future
giving faster than book value.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $7,745, **+20.3%**; 23.6% compounded over 28 years. Net worth
  **+$1.52B**.
- **Shares outstanding:** 1,152,547 (vs. 1,137,778 in Oct 1964).
- **Look-through earnings:** **$604M** (1992); 2000 target >$1.8B.
- **Operating earnings:** $460.7M pre-tax / $347.7M to Berkshire. Total earnings $550.6M /
  $407.3M.
- **Cost of float:** **4.76%** on average float **$2,290.4M** vs. 7.39% long-bond yield;
  beat government in 21 of 26 years. Underwriting loss $(108.96M).
- **Hurricane Andrew:** super-cat losses ~$125M (net super-cat result −$2M); GEICO loss
  ~$50M (look-through share ~$25M); industry CR 114.8 (~4 pts from Andrew).
- **Super-cat:** reject >98% of business; Berkshire 2nd in U.S. P&C net worth.
- **Major stocks (cost → market, $000s):** Coca-Cola (93.4M sh, 2:1 split) 1,023,920 →
  **3,911,125**; GEICO (34.25M, split) 45,713 → **2,226,250**; Cap Cities/ABC 517,500 →
  1,523,500; Gillette 600,000 → 1,365,000; Freddie Mac (16.2M, >doubled) 414,257 →
  783,515; Wells Fargo (6.36M) 380,983 → 485,624; General Dynamics (new, 4.35M) 312,438 →
  450,769; Guinness 333,019 → 299,581; Washington Post 9,731 → 396,954.
- **Scott Fetzer:** record **$110M pre-tax on $116M equity**.
- **Unrealized portfolio appreciation:** $7.6B at year-end.
- **SDC:** $7.6M; 97% participation; 2,810 charities.
