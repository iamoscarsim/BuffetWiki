# 1978 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 26, 1979. The letter opens with accounting housekeeping: the
year-end 1978 merger with **[[Diversified Retailing Company]]** raised Berkshire's
stake in [[Blue Chip Stamps]] to ~58%, forcing full consolidation of Blue Chip's
diverse businesses. Buffett complains that aggregating "textiles, insurance,
candy, newspapers, trading stamps — with dramatically different economic
characteristics" into one balance sheet "tends to obscure economic reality more
than illuminate it," and introduces his **Sources of Earnings** table breaking out
each business's pre-tax and after-tax contribution to Berkshire — a presentation
he keeps for decades.

1978 was a good year: operating earnings were **19.4% of beginning equity**, near
the 1972 record. Buffett stresses that over the prior three years per-share net
worth nearly doubled (~25% annual compounding from operating earnings plus capital
gains), while cautioning that neither rate is sustainable as the insurance cycle
turns down in 1979. **Textiles** earned $1.3 million on $17 million of capital,
and Buffett delivers a now-classic explanation of **[[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]**:
"producers of relatively undifferentiated goods in capital intensive businesses
must earn inadequate returns except under conditions of tight supply or real
shortage." **Insurance underwriting** starred again — [[Phil Liesche]]'s segment of
[[National Indemnity Company]] earned ~$11 million underwriting profit on ~$90
million of premiums — and Buffett credits founder [[Jack Ringwalt]], "whose
operating philosophy remains etched upon the company." He warns the 1979 cycle
will compress margins (auto rates +3% vs. loss costs +9%).

The investing section is rich. Buffett restates the four [[Acquisition Criteria]]
verbatim and reports insurance equities grew to $129.1M cost / $216.5M market. He
introduces the **[[SAFECO]]** holding — "the best run large property and casualty
insurance company in the United States" — bought below book, to argue you can buy
the best business through the market for less than mediocre ones cost in
negotiated deals, and that **passive ownership of excellent management** is no
loss. Most importantly, he articulates the seed of **[[Look-Through Earnings]]**:
the undistributed earnings of well-run investees are "just as real in terms of
eventual benefit to us as the amount distributed," and may even be worth more than
100 cents on the dollar. He states the **[[Capital Allocation]]** test in passing:
retain earnings only if they can be employed at attractive rates; otherwise pay
them out or **repurchase shares**. And he affirms a policy to **concentrate
holdings** ([[Concentration]]). Banking (the [[Illinois National Bank]], now to be
divested by December 31, 1980) and the new retailing operation
([[Associated Retail Stores]] under [[Ben Rosner]]) round out the report, with
Buffett's affectionate note on his cadre of elderly owner-operators — Rosner (75),
Abegg (81), Vincenti (73) — who "instinctively and unerringly think like owners."

## Key figures
- Operating earnings: **19.4% of beginning equity**; total net earnings $39.2M after tax.
- 3-year per-share net worth: ~doubled (~25% annual compounding).
- Insurance underwriting profit (Liesche segment): ~$11M on ~$90M premiums.
- Insurance equities: cost **$129.1M**, market **$216.5M** (from $39.3M cost in 1975).
- 3-year realized + unrealized equity gains: ~$112M (while the Dow fell 852 → 805).
- Textiles: $1.3M earnings on $17M capital.
- Illinois National Bank: ~2.1% return on average assets (~3x major banks).
- Notable new holdings: SAFECO ($23.9M cost / $26.5M market), ABC, Interpublic, Knight-Ridder.
- Washington Post: cost $10.6M, market **$43.4M**.
