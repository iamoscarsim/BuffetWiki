# 1994 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Net worth grew **$1.45 billion (13.9%)** to a per-share book value of **$10,083** (23%
compounded over 30 years); Berkshire's net worth now stands at **$11.9 billion** versus
~$22 million when Buffett and Munger took over. The letter opens with a sober prediction —
"the future performance of Berkshire won't come close to matching… the past" — not because
the formula has stopped working but because "a **fat wallet is the enemy of superior
investment results**." Berkshire now considers only investments where it can deploy **at
least $100 million**, shrinking its universe. Buffett invokes Ted Williams's "happy zone"
(swing only at good pitches), dismisses macro forecasting ("**Fear is the foe of the
faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist**"; "we have usually made our best purchases
when apprehensions about some macro event were at a peak"), and renews the partnership
promise: "if you suffer, we will suffer; if we prosper, so will you," with no upside-only
compensation and the Buffett/Munger fortunes kept in Berkshire stock.

He frames the decade's progress: 1984's insurance securities of $1.7B ($1,500/share) and
~$6M of non-security pre-tax earnings became **$18B of securities ($15,000/share) and ~$384M
of pre-tax earnings** by 1994 (employment 5,000 → 22,000, eleven at HQ). The partial-interest
"look-through" businesses are showcased as "rare gems": Coke's 7.8% stake = ~$200M of
"soft-drink earnings" (280B servings, ~a penny each); Gillette = ~$250M of razor sales;
Wells Fargo's 13% = a "$7 billion Berkshire Bank" earning ~$100M. "It's far better to own a
significant portion of the Hope diamond than 100% of a rhinestone."

**Book value vs. intrinsic value — the Scott Fetzer case.** The letter's centerpiece is a
detailed worked example using **[[Scott Fetzer]]** (bought 1986 for $315.2M against $172.6M
book — a $142.6M premium signaling intrinsic value near *double* book). A nine-year table
shows earnings climbing ($40.3M → $79.3M) while book value *fell* (cash paid out), so ROE
became "truly extraordinary" — had Scott Fetzer been on the 1993 Fortune 500 its return on
equity would have ranked **first** (excluding bankruptcy-windfall companies). The promised
**accounting lesson**: the $142.6M purchase premium is amortized through Berkshire's
earnings (a non-cash, non-economic charge), so Scott Fetzer's carrying value has been marked
*down* to **$148.2M** even as its intrinsic value doubled — proof that book value is
"meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value." Credit to **[[Ralph Schey]]**: "Ben Graham
taught me… it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results."

**Intrinsic value and capital allocation.** Managers must act to raise *per-share intrinsic
value*, not EPS. Focusing on whether a deal is EPS-accretive is "equally silly" — like a
non-earning MBA student boosting his "earnings" by merging with a day laborer. Berkshire
follows Wayne Gretzky ("**Go to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is**"). Most
big acquisitions enrich the acquiree's holders, the acquirer's management, and the bankers,
while *reducing* the acquirer's wealth — John Medlin's "**chain letter in reverse**." CEOs
brimming with "animal spirits and ego" are pushed into deals by advisors ("like asking your
interior decorator whether you need a $50,000 rug"); one insurer CEO confessed the real
rationale: "**Aw, fellas, all the other kids have one**." (See [[Capital Allocation]].)

**Compensation.** Schey is paid on *Scott Fetzer's* results, not Berkshire's — with a high
charge for incremental capital employed and an equal credit for capital *released*, so "it
pays Ralph — and pays him well — to send to Omaha any cash he can't advantageously use."
True **alignment** means "being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside"; many
"alignment" plans are "artful forms of 'heads I win, tails you lose,'" and ordinary
ten-year options reward a manager "who has done no more than tread water." The Schey deal
"was worked out in about five minutes… without the 'help' of lawyers or compensation
consultants" and "has never been changed." Buffett and Munger, the only ones logically
compensable on the whole enterprise, want little — "if we were not paid at all, [we] would
be delighted with the cushy jobs we hold" (Reagan: "hard work never killed anyone, but…
why take the chance"). (See [[Executive Compensation]].)

**Look-through earnings** reached **$1,030M** — "modestly ahead of schedule" toward the
$1.85B-by-2000 goal, though flattered by abnormally high super-cat earnings. New look-through
names: **[[American Express]]** (built up), **Gannett** (new), **PNC Bank** (new); **[[GEICO]]
crossed 50%** ownership (50.2%).

**Insurance.** A **second straight underwriting-profit year** — float grew to **$3,056.6M**
at *negative* cost. But "it is not as good as it currently looks": super-cat was again highly
profitable, and 1994 was "close to a best-case" (only the January California [Northridge]
quake caused significant loss; no major loss expected from the early-1995 **Kobe** quake).
Two competitive edges from financial strength: buyers want cover that is "**absolutely
certain**" to pay after a $50B catastrophe (when many reinsurers would default), and Berkshire
can write **single policies up to $400–500M** same-day, which no rival will. Buffett states the
**worst-case super-cat insurance loss at ~$600M after-tax** — "If you are not comfortable with
this level of exposure, the time to sell your Berkshire stock is now, not after the inevitable
mega-catastrophe." [[Ajit Jain]]'s "value to us is simply enormous." 1995 super-cat volume will
fall as new capital chases premiums.

**Common stocks & the Amex history.** The over-$300M holdings now include **[[American Express]]** (27.76M shares, ~10%, cost $1.36B), Cap Cities/ABC (20M shares post-10:1 split,
$1,705M), Coca-Cola (built to **100,000,000 shares**, $5,150M), Freddie Mac, **Gannett**,
GEICO, Gillette ($1,797M), **PNC Bank**, Washington Post, and Wells Fargo ($984.7M). "The
truly big investment idea can usually be explained in a short paragraph"; investors should
**price, not time**, purchases ("degree-of-difficulty doesn't count… Why scrap an informed
decision because of an uninformed guess?"). Buffett recounts pulling investments "out of past
associations": GEICO (70% of his 1951 portfolio; first stock he sold, to Aunt Alice), the
Washington Post (paper-route money), Coca-Cola ("the real money was in the syrup"), and
**American Express** — 40% of the Buffett Partnership during the 1960s salad-oil scandal, now
built into a ~10% stake (Amex earned $12.5M in 1964, **$1.4 billion in 1994**), plus the IDS
unit he first studied in 1953 at a P/E of 3.

**Mistake Du Jour.** A "vintage year." **Silver medal:** selling **10 million Cap Cities
shares at $63** in late 1993 (worth $85.25 by year-end 1994 — $222.5M of forgone value); "now
I've become a repeat offender… Maybe it's time to get a guardian appointed." **Gold medal:**
the **$358M [[USAir]] preferred**, dividend suspended in September 1994 — an "unforced error"
of "sloppy analysis" (he failed to see that a high-cost carrier in a deregulated commodity
business "must lower its costs to competitive levels or face extinction"). Written down to
**$89.5M (25¢ on the dollar)**, a $269M charge through the income statement; Buffett and Munger
will not stand for reelection to USAir's board. Herb Kelleher: "Bankruptcy court for airlines
has become a health spa." "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it."

**Miscellaneous.** Two retiring CEOs are thanked — **Dan Burke** (Capital Cities/ABC) and
**[[Carl Reichardt]]** (Wells Fargo). SDC reached **$10.4M** (95.7%, 3,300 charities). Only one
minor 1994 acquisition (a small retail shoe chain). After a failed merger with a large
family-controlled business, Berkshire asks shareholders to **authorize preferred stock** for
future deals (only to be used "receiving as much in intrinsic value as we are giving"). [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] is **101**, working daily in "Mrs. B's Warehouse"; NFM opened the **Mega
Mart**.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $10,083, **+13.9%**; 23% compounded over 30 years. Net worth $11.9B.
- **Securities held:** $18B ($15,000/share), up from $1.7B in 1984. Non-security pre-tax
  earnings ~$384M (vs ~$6M in 1984). Employees 22,000.
- **Look-through earnings:** **$1,030M** (1994).
- **Operating earnings:** $839.4M pre-tax / $606.2M to Berkshire. Total earnings (after a
  $268.5M USAir writedown and $91.3M security gains) $662.2M / $494.8M.
- **Insurance:** **underwriting profit** (2nd straight year); average float **$3,056.6M** at
  negative cost; long-bond yield 7.88%. Worst-case super-cat loss ~$600M after-tax.
- **Scott Fetzer:** bought 1986 for $315.2M ($172.6M book); 1994 earnings $79.3M; carrying
  value marked down to $148.2M; ROE would rank #1 on the Fortune 500.
- **Major stocks (cost → market, $000s):** Coca-Cola (100M sh) 1,298,888 → **5,150,000**;
  Cap Cities/ABC (20M sh post-split) 345,000 → 1,705,000; Gillette 600,000 → 1,797,000; GEICO
  (50.2%) 45,713 → 1,678,250; Wells Fargo 423,680 → 984,727; American Express (27.76M, ~10%)
  723,919 → 818,918; Freddie Mac 270,468 → 644,441; Washington Post 9,731 → 418,983; PNC Bank
  503,046 → 410,951; Gannett 335,216 → 365,002.
- **American Express:** earned $12.5M (1964) → $1.4B (1994); Berkshire stake cost $1.36B.
- **USAir:** $358M preferred written down to $89.5M; $269M charge. **SDC:** $10.4M; 95.7%.
