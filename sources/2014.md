# 2014 Shareholder Letter (Golden Anniversary)

## Summary

The **50th-anniversary letter**. Net worth grew **$18.3 billion**; per-share book value rose **8.3%**
to **$146,186** (50 years, **19.4% compounded**). For the first time Buffett adds **stock-price
history** to the scorecard — the **1,826,163% market gain** since 1964 (vs. 11,196% for book; the S&P
22,800-odd%), which he says "roughly equal[s]" the rise in *intrinsic* value. The report includes two
landmark **"Golden Anniversary" essays**, one by Buffett and one by Munger, each written without
seeing the other's.

**The year at Berkshire.** The **"Powerhouse Five"** — **[[MidAmerican Energy|Berkshire Hathaway Energy]]** (renamed), [[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]], **[[ISCAR|IMC]]** (renamed), [[Lubrizol]]
and [[Marmon Group]] — earned a record **$12.4 billion** pre-tax (+$1.6B; +$12B over a decade with
minor dilution). The one blemish: **BNSF service failures** disappointed shippers, prompting a planned
**$6 billion** of 2015 capex ("nearly 50% more than any other railroad has spent in a single year").
Smaller non-insurance units earned **$5.1B**. Insurance logged a **12th straight underwriting profit**
($2.7B; float **$84 billion**; 12-year cumulative $24B). A record bolt-on year: **31 deals for $7.8
billion** — the largest being **[[Duracell]]** (acquired from P&G via a stock exchange, closing 2H
2015, placed under Marmon). And **[[Van Tuyl Automotive]]** (78 dealerships, $9B sales, the #5 U.S.
auto group) made Berkshire "car guys." Capex set a record **$15 billion** (90% U.S.); employment hit
**340,499** (HQ 25). The **Big Four** ([[American Express]] 14.8%, [[Coca-Cola]] 9.2%, [[IBM]] 7.8%,
[[Wells Fargo]] 9.4%) threw off **$4.7B** of attributable earnings (unrealized gains **$42B**).
Buffett gave **[[Todd Combs]] and [[Ted Weschler]]** each a small subsidiary to chair. He swapped
**Phillips 66 and Graham Holdings** stock for operating businesses (ending the [[Washington Post]]
round-trip).

**Buffett's anniversary essay — "Berkshire: Past, Present and Future."** A candid history: the
**original textile mistake** (taking control out of pique at Seabury Stanton's $11⅜ tender, then
marrying NICO to a dying business — "a decision that eventually diverted $100 billion… to a collection
of strangers"); Waumbec; the **cigar-butt** method that "was scalable only to a point"; and
**Munger's blueprint** — "**buy wonderful businesses at fair prices**" — crystallized by **[[See's Candies]]** ($25M for a brand gushing cash on tiny capital; $1.9B of cumulative pre-tax earnings on
$40M of added investment; "envision rabbits breeding"). The worst error: **[[Dexter Shoe]]**, paid in
stock now worth **$5.7 billion** ("a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records"). **Why the
conglomerate works:** it allocates capital **rationally, tax-free, and frictionlessly** across
industries (unlike a single-industry CEO or a taxed individual investor — "if horses had controlled
investment decisions, there would have been no auto industry"), can buy whole businesses *or* pieces
of wonderful ones, and is the **"home of choice"** for families versus synergy-hunting strategics or
debt-piling "private equity" ("equity is a dirty word for many private-equity buyers"). A withering
takedown of **conglomerate-era accounting** (the 1960s pooling/EPS "hocus-pocus"; ITT, Litton, LTV's
Jimmy Ling and "Meatball, Golf Ball and Goof Ball"). Spin-offs rejected ("2+2 will always equal 4").

**The next 50 years.** The chance of permanent loss "is as low as can be found among single-company
investments" — *unless* bought at an elevated price (near 2× book) or with **borrowed money** (the
stock has fallen ~50% three times). Berkshire is "the **Gibraltar** of American business," kept so by
three rules: a large diversified earnings stream, **≥$20 billion of cash** (in Treasury bills — "don't
leave home without it"), and **no large near-term cash demands** (no big short-term debt, **no more
collateral-posting derivatives**, no cash-out insurance). "It is **madness to risk losing what you
need in pursuing what you simply desire**." Eventually (10–20 years out) Berkshire will distribute
excess cash via dividends or — if below intrinsic value — buybacks. **Succession**: [[Howard Buffett]]
as a non-executive chairman "safety valve"; the right CEO is identified internally, must guard against
the **"ABCs of business decay — arrogance, bureaucracy and complacency"** (GM, IBM, Sears, U.S. Steel);
Combs and Weschler handle investments.

**Munger's anniversary essay — "The Berkshire System."** Munger codifies the model (see [[The Berkshire System]]): extreme **decentralization**; cash acquisitions; no dividends while $1 retained
makes >$1; near-perfect creditworthiness; never selling subsidiaries; a chairman who reserves only a
few activities (investments, choosing/paying CEOs, capital deployment, the annual letter, and "much
time for quiet reading and thinking"). He credits the "**Wooden effect**" (concentrating skill in one
long-serving person), names insurance as the engine, and — most strikingly — predicts Berkshire will
**stay "better-than-normal" even after Buffett**, because **[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]]** are
"**world-leading**… in some important ways, each is a better business executive than Buffett." Biggest
historical errors were of **omission** (e.g. not buying Walmart) — "at least $50 billion."

**Insurance.** Float grew to **$83,921M** ([[Ajit Jain|BH Reinsurance]] $42,454M, [[GEICO]] $13,569M,
[[General Re]] $19,280M, Other Primary $8,618M). **[[National Indemnity Company]]** now has GAAP net
worth of **$111 billion** — the largest of any insurer in the world ("per page, this has to be
Berkshire's best deal" — the 1½-page 1967 contract). **[[GEICO]]** reached **10.8% share** (from 2.5%
in 1995; Tony Nicely, 53 years). [[Ajit Jain]] wrote a **$3B single-premium** policy (2nd only to the
2007 Lloyd's $7.1B; "all eight" P/C policies in history with a >$1B premium "were written by
Berkshire"); **BHSI** expanded internationally. [[General Re]] "is now a gem." The smaller insurers
earned $2.95B of underwriting profit over the decade (float $1.7B → $8.6B).

**Regulated, capital-intensive ([[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]] + [[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]).**
BNSF interest coverage >8:1; ~15% of U.S. intercity ton-miles. BHE serves 11 states; **6% of U.S.
wind, 7% of U.S. solar**; owns two pipelines (8% of U.S. gas), the new **[[AltaLink]]** (Alberta
electric transmission, **C$3B**, 85% of the province), and U.K./Philippines utilities — "we will
continue to buy and build utility operations throughout the world for decades."

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $146,186, **+8.3%** (net worth +$18.3B); 50 years, **19.4%**. Stock-price gain
  1964–2014 **1,826,163%** (≈ intrinsic). Per-share investments $140,123 (+8.4%); non-insurance
  pre-tax EPS $10,847 (+19%).
- **Powerhouse five** record **$12.4B** pre-tax. **31 bolt-ons** for **$7.8B** (incl. **Duracell**).
  **Van Tuyl** (78 dealerships) → Berkshire Hathaway Automotive. Capex record **$15B**.
- **Insurance:** **12th straight underwriting profit** ($2,668M: BH Reins $606M, Gen Re $277M, GEICO
  $1,159M, Other Primary $626M); **float $83,921M** (12-yr UW profit $24B). NICO net worth **$111B**;
  GEICO share **10.8%**; Ajit wrote a **$3B single-premium** policy.
- **Big Four** ownership up (Amex 14.8%, Coke 9.2%, IBM 7.8%, WFC 9.4%); attributable earnings $4.7B;
  unrealized gains **$42B**.
- **BNSF:** $6B of 2015 capex planned (service recovery). **BHE:** AltaLink (C$3B); 6% U.S. wind / 7%
  U.S. solar.
