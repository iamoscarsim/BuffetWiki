# 1993 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Book value per share rose **14.3%** to **$8,854** (23.3% compounded over 29 years), and
net worth grew $1.5 billion — a figure Buffett decomposes into four non-operating items:
two negatives from new GAAP/tax rules (the deferred-tax change, −$70M; the corporate rate
rising to 35%, −$75M), one positive from carrying *all* common stocks at market (+$172M),
and +$478M from the **29,147 shares** issued (3,944 on the January debenture conversion and
**25,203 to acquire Dexter Shoe**). He restates that intrinsic value, not book, is what
counts — both grew ~14%, "satisfactory but unexciting" — while Berkshire's **market price
rose 39%**.

**Coke, Gillette, and the weighing machine.** Reprising his 1991 caution, Buffett notes
that from 1991–93 [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Gillette]] grew operating EPS 38% and 37% while their
*stocks* rose only 11% and 6% — "the companies overperformed their stocks" (Wall Street's
"new apprehension about brand names"). The history lesson: Coke went public at $40 in 1919,
fell below $20 by 1920, and one share with dividends reinvested was worth **$2.1 million** by
1993 — Graham's "in the short run a voting machine… in the long run a weighing machine"
([[Mr. Market]]).

**Dexter Shoe.** Building on [[H. H. Brown]] (1991) and [[Lowell Shoe]] (1992), Berkshire
acquired **[[Dexter Shoe]]** of Dexter, Maine (closed November 7, 1993), maker of
popular-priced men's and women's shoes — "one of the best-managed companies Charlie and I
have seen in our business lifetimes." Founder **[[Harold Alfond]]** (started at 25¢/hour,
founded Dexter in 1956 with $10,000) and nephew **[[Peter Lunder]]** built it to 7.5M
pairs/year (Maine + Puerto Rico), 77 retail outlets, ~15% of U.S. golf-shoe output, and
Nordstrom/J.C. Penney supplier awards. The deal was paid **in Berkshire stock** — Alfond and
Lunder, uninterested in cash, traded "a 100% interest in a single terrific business for a
smaller interest in a large group of terrific businesses," tax-free; 1994 shoe-operation
sales were expected above $550M with >$85M pre-tax. "Five years ago we had no thought of
getting into shoes… So much for strategic plans." *(In hindsight this stock-funded purchase
became Buffett's most-regretted deal — see [[Mistakes Ledger]] — but in 1993 it is
celebrated.)*

**Look-through earnings** reached **$856M**; with the new shares, the 2000 target rose to
**$1.85 billion** ("we will… risk embarrassment by painting [the target] first and shooting
later"). The **[[Capital Cities]] partial sale** — one-third of the 1986 stake (1M shares)
sold at **$630** (cost $172.50), a $297M after-tax gain — illustrates that look-through is
*conservative*: the realized gain "far exceeded the undistributed earnings allocable to
these shares" ($152M).

**Taxes & compounding.** Berkshire will pay ~$390M of 1993 federal tax (plus >$400M as its
share of investees' taxes) — about **0.5% of all U.S. corporate income tax**. Buffett has
"absolutely no complaint." The **Li'l Abner parable** (Appassionatta, Old Man Mose) makes
the deferred-tax point: a dollar doubling 27½ times in a *single* investment yields ~$130M
after one final tax, versus far less from a *succession* of taxed doubles — "tax-paying
investors will realize a far, far greater sum from a single investment that compounds
internally… than from a succession of investments compounding at the same rate" (see
[[Permanent Ownership]]).

**Insurance.** The industry combined-ratio table is **retired**: with interest rates down,
"the value of float has substantially declined," so a given combined ratio means less than
it did in the 1980s — true economics require weighing underwriting against the **risk-free
return on float**. Berkshire's 1993 was a banner year — an **underwriting profit of $31M**
on **$2,624.7M** of average float, i.e. **float at less than zero cost** (Berkshire was *paid*
to hold it). Buffett tempers the cheer: the **super-cat** book had no 1993 losses only by
luck (the Midwest floods didn't trigger it — few private flood policies), and a single year
proves nothing — "suppose an event occurs 25 times a century… give 5-for-1 odds against it…
you will eventually go broke." Berkshire believes it gets ~3.5-for-1, but "it will take
decades… to find out whether our underwriting judgment has been sound." Andrew cost $125M in
1992; an identical storm now (book expanded) could cost **$600M**. [[Ajit Jain]] is "by far
the best manager in this business," and Berkshire's net worth is **10–20× its main
competitors'** — each of the world's four largest reinsurers buys cover from Berkshire. A
warning: ~$5B of new reinsurance capacity has been raised, prices are softening, and
Berkshire will cut volume rather than underprice. Other units (homestate — Rod Eldred;
workers' comp — **Brad Kinstler**; credit-card — the Kizers; NICO auto/GL — Don Wurster) all
did well.

**The risk/beta essay.** A landmark passage rejecting the academic equation of risk with
**beta** (price volatility). Real risk is "the possibility of loss or injury" — the chance
that an investment's after-tax purchasing-power return falls short. Beta produces
"absurdities": it labels [[Washington Post]] *riskier* after its price fell in 1973, when it
was actually cheaper and safer. The true investor *welcomes* volatility ([[Mr. Market]]).
Buffett lists **five factors** for judging risk (certainty of the business's long-term
economics; of management's ability and of its honesty toward owners; the purchase price; and
prospective taxes/inflation) — Justice Stewart's "I know it when I see it." [[Coca-Cola]]
(44% of world soft drinks) and [[Gillette]] (60% of blades) have obvious moats; their beta
resembles that of "run-of-the-mill companies," which only proves beta meaningless. The
"pet rock vs. Barbie" distinction is one ordinary investors can make but beta cannot. (See
[[Risk and Volatility]], [[Economic Moat]].)

**Concentration & the know-nothing investor.** "We'll now settle for one good idea a year."
Concentration "may well *decrease* risk" by deepening the investor's understanding. The
**know-nothing** investor should buy an **index fund** and space out purchases — "when 'dumb'
money acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb"; the **know-something** investor
with 5–10 understandable, advantaged companies should ignore "standard diversification
dogma." Mae West: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful." (See [[Concentration]],
[[Circle of Competence]].)

**Corporate governance.** A major essay distinguishing three owner/manager situations and the
director's leverage in each: (1) **no controlling shareholder** (the common case) — directors
act for an "absentee owner," boards should be small (≤10) and mostly outside, evaluating the
CEO without him present; (2) **controlling owner who manages** — Berkshire's current mode,
where outside directors can only persuade or object, and resign if a serious matter is
ignored; (3) **controlling owner not in management** (Hershey, Dow Jones) — the most
effective for governance. Berkshire will enter **mode 3** after Buffett's death (his stock
passes to wife **[[Susan Buffett|Susie]]** or a foundation); **[[Howard Buffett|Howard]]**
joined the board in 1993 to represent the controlling interest. "All in all, we're prepared
for 'the truck.'" (See [[Corporate Governance]].)

**Miscellaneous.** SDC reached **$9.4M** (97%, 3,110 charities), with the per-share amount
**raised from $8 to $10**; the category breakdown (and abortion gifts both for and against)
shows owners giving freely. [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] turned **100** on December 3, 1993 (NFM
sales hit **$200M**). **[[Katharine Graham]]** retired as Washington Post chairman (elected to
*Fortune's* Business Hall of Fame); **[[Don Keough]]** retired from Coca-Cola (Buffett recounts
Keough as his across-the-street Omaha neighbor, and credits Keough + [[Roberto Goizueta]] with
taking Coke from $4.4B to $58B of market value in under 13 years). [[Frank Rooney]] (72) led
H. H. Brown to record profits (+35%) and brokered the Dexter deal. **[[Susan Jacques]]**, who
started at $4/hour, was named President and CEO of [[Borsheim's]] at 34.

## Key figures

- **Book value/share:** $8,854, **+14.3%**; 23.3% compounded over 29 years. Net worth +$1.5B.
  Market price **+39%**.
- **Look-through earnings:** **$856M** (1993); 2000 target raised to **$1.85B**.
- **Operating earnings:** $643.3M pre-tax / $477.8M to Berkshire. Total earnings (incl.
  $546.4M security-sale gains, less $146.3M new-accounting tax accruals) $1,189.7M / $688.1M.
- **Insurance:** **underwriting profit $31M**; average float **$2,624.7M**; cost of float
  **negative**; long-bond yield 6.35%.
- **Cap Cities partial sale:** 1M of 3M shares at $630 (cost $172.50) → $297M after-tax gain.
- **Federal taxes:** ~$390M direct (+ >$400M investee share) ≈ 0.5% of all U.S. corporate tax.
- **Dexter Shoe:** acquired 11/7/93 for 25,203 Berkshire shares; 7.5M pairs/yr. 1994 shoe-ops
  sales expected >$550M, pre-tax >$85M.
- **Major stocks (cost → market, $000s):** Coca-Cola 1,023,920 → **4,167,975**; GEICO 45,713
  → 1,759,594; Cap Cities/ABC (now 2M sh) 345,000 → 1,239,000; Gillette 600,000 → 1,431,000;
  Wells Fargo (6.79M) 423,680 → 878,614; Freddie Mac (13.65M) 307,505 → 681,023; Washington
  Post 9,731 → 440,148; General Dynamics 94,938 → 401,287; Guinness 333,019 → 270,822.
- **SDC:** $9.4M; 97%; 3,110 charities; per-share amount raised $8 → $10.
- **NFM sales:** $200M. **Coca-Cola:** 207M cases (1938) → 10.7B cases (1993).
