# 1979 Shareholder Letter

## Summary

Written March 3, 1980. A landmark letter for both its long-term scorecard and its
introduction of inflation as Buffett's central worry. A new accounting rule now
carries insurers' equities at market value, lifting reported net worth. Buffett
reaffirms that single-year operating performance should be measured as **operating
earnings over beginning equity at cost** (18.6% in 1979, down from 1978), again
deriding earnings per share — "even a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock
if the dividend payout ratio is low." The **primary test of managerial economic
performance is a high return on equity without undue leverage or accounting
gimmickry**, not EPS gains.

For long-term results he unveils the figure that anchors the whole Berkshire story:
book value per share grew from **$19.46 on September 30, 1964** (just before he took
control) to **$335.85 at year-end 1979** — **20.5% compounded annually** — achieved
with little leverage and essentially no share issuance, by acquiring thirteen
businesses outright and starting six others from the textile base. Then the
"crucial observation": **[[Inflation]]** plus taxes can render even a 20% return a
*negative* real result. He coins the **"investor's misery index"** (inflation rate
plus the tax cost of moving earnings into the owner's pocket); when it exceeds
return on equity, the owner's purchasing power shrinks "even though he consumes
nothing at all." A friend's barb: Berkshire's 1964 book value bought ~half an ounce
of gold, and fifteen years of "blood, sweat and tears" later it still buys about
half an ounce.

**Textiles**: Buffett confesses the **Waumbec Mills** purchase (a statistical
bargain, bought below working capital) was a mistake, yielding the durable lesson
that **"turnarounds seldom turn,"** and that "the same energies and talent are much
better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor
business purchased at a bargain price" — the pivot toward [[Good Business at a Fair Price]]
(see [[Charlie Munger]]). A network TV station, by contrast, "almost
unavoidably" earns extraordinary returns; the "easy" business, even at a fancy
price, is the better route. **Insurance**: [[Phil Liesche]] earned $8.4M
underwriting profit on ~$82M premiums while *deliberately shrinking volume* —
Buffett's clearest praise yet of [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]] ("if
business makes sense, he writes it; if it doesn't, he rejects it"), with a policy
of not laying off staff during voluntary volume swings. He is bearish on
reinsurance's "long tail" risks and forecasts a five-year industry combined ratio
around 105. A long **bond** section argues long-dated fixed-dollar bonds may be
"obsolete instruments" in an inflationary world ("Neither a short-term borrower nor
a long-term lender be"), and admits buying 15-year bonds was itself a mistake.

The **[[Illinois National Bank]]** is reported for the last time as a subsidiary
(record 2.3% on assets); the Fed's stance pushes Berkshire toward a *sale* rather
than spin-off. The letter closes with two influential statements of culture:
**[[Owner Orientation and Candor]]** — owners deserve to hear from the CEO directly,
report-as-if-roles-reversed, and Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy on attracting a
matched shareholder "clientele" — and **[[Decentralization]]**: centralized capital
allocation at the top, extreme delegation of operations, an HQ of ~1,500 square
feet that "could just field a basketball team."

## Key figures
- **Book value/share: $19.46 (9/30/1964) → $335.85 (YE 1979); 20.5% compounded.**
- Operating earnings: **18.6% of beginning equity** (down from 19.4%); EPS +~20%.
- Equity portfolio: cost **$185.4M**, market **$336.7M**.
- GEICO common: 5,730,114 shares, cost $28.3M, **market $68.0M** (largest holding).
- Liesche underwriting profit: $8.4M on ~$82M premiums.
- Illinois National Bank: ~2.3% on average assets (>3x average bank).
- New equity holdings: Affiliated Publications, Amerada Hess, General Foods,
  Handy & Harman, Media General, Ogilvy & Mather, F.W. Woolworth; Precision Steel
  acquired (via Wesco).
