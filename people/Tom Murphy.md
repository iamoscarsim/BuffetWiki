# Tom Murphy

Thomas S. Murphy built and ran **[[Capital Cities]]** Communications (later Capital
Cities/ABC) with his partner Dan Burke, and is the manager Buffett holds up, again and
again, as the gold standard of combined **operating skill and capital allocation** —
the human reason Berkshire was content to own a huge, non-controlled stake.

Murphy is present from the start. The [[1977]] letter uses Cap Cities — "extraordinary
properties and extraordinary management" — to argue that "we can obtain a better
management result through non-control than control." In [[1981]] (and on the
[[Premium Takeovers]] page) Buffett ranks him with Henry Singleton of Teledyne as a
**managerial superstar**. When Berkshire backed Cap Cities' acquisition of ABC ([[1984]]–
[[1985]]) with a $517.5M investment, Buffett called Murphy and Burke "tops in both
ability and integrity… the sort of fellows you'd want your daughter to marry," and
accepted an unusual long-term voting agreement letting Murphy vote Berkshire's block —
trusting the managers against "revolving-door capitalists." Through the late-1980s
([[1986]]–[[1988]]) Cap Cities/ABC is one of Berkshire's three **permanent** holdings,
its managers a standing example that Buffett "couldn't be… in better hands" with.

His reputation became a measuring stick: in [[1990]], introducing **[[Carl Reichardt]]**
and Paul Hazen of [[Wells Fargo]], Buffett writes that "the combination of Carl and Paul
reminds me of another — Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities/ABC," praising both
pairs for being "stronger than the sum of [their] parts," paying able people well while
abhorring excess head count, attacking costs in good times and bad, and "stick[ing] with
what they understand." (Murphy later sold Cap Cities/ABC to Disney in 1995 and joined
Berkshire's board — chapters for later letters.)

## Evolution
- [1977] The face of the non-control argument: Cap Cities' superb management means
  "a better management result through non-control than control."
- [1981] Named a **managerial superstar** alongside Henry Singleton (Teledyne); see
  [[Premium Takeovers]].
- [1984–85] Backed in the **ABC acquisition** ($517.5M); "tops in both ability and
  integrity"; the long-term voting agreement entrusts him with Berkshire's block.
- [1986–88] Manager of one of Berkshire's three **permanent** holdings (Cap Cities/ABC).
- [1990] The benchmark for great bank management — the yardstick for [[Carl Reichardt]]
  and Paul Hazen at [[Wells Fargo]].
- [1995] **The capstone tribute.** Murphy sells [[Capital Cities]]/ABC to **[[Disney]]** — and
  Buffett reveals he helped **broker the merger** at Sun Valley (July 14, 1995), a chance meeting
  with Murphy and Disney's Michael Eisner that unstuck the cash-vs-stock impasse. His encomium:
  "**he is as fine an executive as I have ever seen**… human qualities every bit the equal of his
  managerial qualities… the exemplar of [a manager] I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or
  trustee of my will. **If Murph should elect to run another business, don't bother to study its
  value — just buy the stock.**" Pinned to his own regret: "don't later be as dumb as I was two
  years ago when I sold one-third of our holdings in Cap Cities for $635 million (versus the $1.27
  billion those shares would bring in the Disney merger)." (Murphy later joins Berkshire's board.)
- [[2019]] **"Just buy good businesses."** Now "a valued director of Berkshire and an all-time great among
  business managers," Murphy is quoted for the advice he "long ago" gave Buffett on acquisitions: "**To
  achieve a reputation as a good manager, just be sure you buy good businesses.**" Buffett uses it to frame
  his candid review of his own "uneven record" (acquisitions "are similar to marriage").
- [[2024]] **"Praise by name, criticize by category."** Buffett opens his final letter as CEO by invoking
  the advice Murphy gave him "60 years ago" for discussing subsidiary problems — a rule he says still
  governs how candidly (and how kindly) Berkshire reports on its businesses.
