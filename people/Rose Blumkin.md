# Rose Blumkin

Rose Blumkin — "Mrs. B" — founded the [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]] and became one of
the most beloved figures in the Berkshire story. Introduced in the [[1983]] letter:
she talked her way past a border guard to leave Russia for America at 23, had no
formal schooling and no English, sold used clothing for years, and in 1937 opened
her furniture store with $500 in savings. When resources ran out she sold the
furniture from her own home to pay creditors "precisely as promised"; when
competitors pressured suppliers to boycott her, she won Fair Trade lawsuits and even
sold the judge $1,400 of carpet.

Buffett bought 90% of NFM from her in 1983 when she was **90 years old**; she stayed
on as Chairman, "on the sales floor seven days a week," refusing to retire and "risk
losing her marbles." Carpet was her specialty. She embodies the owner-operator
Buffett prizes above all — exceptional value to customers translating into
exceptional economics — and stands alongside [[Ben Rosner]] and [[Gene Abegg]] in his
gallery of merchant-geniuses. (Later letters record a famous family rift and her
brief departure and return.)

## Evolution
- [1983] Sold 90% of NFM to Berkshire at age 90; remained Chairman, working the
  sales floor daily. The grizzly-bear competitor Buffett would never want to face.
- [1984] At 91, received an **honorary doctorate in commercial science from NYU**
  (never having spent a day in school). Still at the store seven days a week. "I
  can't wait until it gets daylight so I can get back to the business." Her business
  in a phrase: "sell cheap and tell the truth."
- [1986] Chairman at **93**, at the store seven days a week — "continues to outsell and
  out-hustle any manager I've ever seen"; "competing with her represents a triumph of
  courage over judgment."
- [1987] At **94**, "gathering speed"; Buffett "persuaded the Board to scrap our
  mandatory retirement-at-100 policy."
- [1988] **95** and "her cart roll[s] on and on" (51 years a boss). Her extended family
  (sister Rebecca Friedman's branch) brings Berkshire **[[Borsheim's]]** in early 1989.
- [1989] At **96**, **quit NFM** in May after a dispute with the family over the carpet
  department, and promptly opened a *competing* carpet-and-furniture store across the
  street ("she works seven days a week" still). Buffett sides with the family on the
  specific issue (carpet sales had lagged) but reveres her undiminished drive — she
  "continues to make Horatio Alger's heroes look like victims of tired blood." (She and
  Berkshire later reconcile.)
- [1992] **Reconciled, at 99.** Still working seven days a week, Mrs. B sold her building and
  land back to [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]] and rejoined the Berkshire fold (continuing to run
  her carpet business in the same location — "no sense slowing down just when you're hitting
  full stride"). She graciously signed a **non-compete agreement** — which Buffett, "having
  been incautious on this point when she was 89," "snapped at." "Mrs. B belongs in the
  Guinness Book of World Records on many counts. Signing a non-compete at 99 merely adds one
  more."
- [1993] **100th birthday** on December 3 ("the candles cost more than the cake"). She
  postponed her own party to a night the store was closed rather than miss working — still
  there seven days a week, for as many hours as the store is open. Buffett retold her arc (to
  the U.S. 77 years earlier, no English, no schooling; NFM founded 1937 with $500; now $200M in
  sales) and, "delighted to attend Mrs. B's birthday party," noted "she's promised to attend
  *my* 100th." A commemorative See's assortment featuring her picture (in place of Mrs. See's)
  was planned for the annual meeting.
- [1994] At **101**, working daily in **"Mrs. B's Warehouse"** at the new NFM complex — "She
  never misses a day at the store — or, for that matter, an hour."
- [1995] At **102**, still at work in Mrs. B's Warehouse. Honored in November at the reopening of
  **"The Rose,"** a restored 1920s downtown Omaha theater that "would have been demolished had she
  not saved it."
- [1996] At **103**, "sometimes operates with an oxygen mask … attached to a tank on her cart.
  But if you try to keep pace with her, it will be you who needs oxygen." NFM did a record **$265
  million** of business — "a record for a single-location home furnishings operation."
