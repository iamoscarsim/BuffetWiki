# Lou Simpson

Lou Simpson ran investments at [[GEICO]] and is the only outside investor Buffett
consistently entrusted with major capital. Introduced in the [[1982]] letter as
"the best investment manager in the property-casualty business," Simpson compiled a
long market-beating record managing GEICO's equity portfolio in a style closely
aligned with Buffett's own — concentrated, value-oriented, low-turnover. In later
letters Buffett publishes Simpson's standalone returns approvingly and, in
succession discussions, names him as a capital-allocation backstop should anything
happen to Buffett and Munger. He retired around 2010.

## Evolution
- [1982] Introduced as GEICO's investment manager — "the best... in the
  property-casualty business."
- [1986] Buffett prints Simpson's **1980–86 equity record** at GEICO against the S&P
  500 — e.g. 1982 +45.8% (vs. 21.4%), 1984 +21.8% (vs. 6.2%), 1986 +38.7% (vs. 18.6%) —
  "it's a little embarrassing for me, the fellow responsible for investments at
  Berkshire, to chronicle Lou's performance." Praises the *method* as much as the
  numbers: undervalued common stocks that individually risked little permanent loss and
  collectively were "close to risk-free." Calls him the "second stage of the GEICO rocket."
- [1995] With Berkshire buying [[GEICO]] outright, Simpson is one of the two managers who "come
  with it" (alongside [[Tony Nicely]]). Buffett prints his record: **1980–95 equities returned
  22.8%/yr vs. 15.7% for the S&P**. "Lou takes the same conservative, concentrated approach to
  investments that we do." Crucially, Buffett names him a **succession backstop**: "His presence
  on the scene assures us that Berkshire would have an extraordinary professional immediately
  available to handle its investments if something were to happen to Charlie and me."
- [1996] Continued "to manage GEICO's money in an outstanding manner" — his equities beat the S&P
  500 by **6.2 points**. His investment bonus is tied to **four-year investment performance**, kept
  deliberately separate from GEICO's underwriting (so great work on one side can't be "neutralized"
  by the other — "If you bat .350 at Berkshire, you can be sure you will get paid commensurately").
  Buffett: in Lou and [[Tony Nicely]] "we are lucky to have Hall-of-Famers in both key positions."
- [1997] Buffett notes that Berkshire's reported portfolio "sometimes reflect[s] the investment
  decisions of GEICO's Lou Simpson," who "independently runs an equity portfolio of nearly **$2
  billion** that may at times overlap the portfolio that I manage, and occasionally … differ[s]
  from mine."
- [2006] **The succession backstop, and its limit.** In laying out the investment-side succession
  plan, Buffett calls Lou "a **top-notch investor** with an outstanding long-term record" who "would
  fill in magnificently for a short period" — but flags the problem: he "is only **six years younger
  than I**," so for the long term Berkshire needs a different answer. He recounts how he and Jack
  Byrne found Simpson in 1979 and structured pay for "sustained overperformance," and that Lou — who
  "could have left… to manage far greater sums on more advantageous terms" — never did. Berkshire
  will now seek "a younger person or two **made of the same stuff**" as its future CIO.
- [2010] **Retirement.** Simpson told Buffett he wished to retire — "since Lou was a mere 74 — an age
  Charlie and I regard as appropriate only for trainees at Berkshire — his call was a surprise." Buffett
  pays the rare public tribute he'd withheld for years (Simpson's GEICO equity record "made mine look
  bad… Who needs that?"): "**Lou is one of the investment greats. We will miss him.**" His departure (with
  Munger and Buffett aging) drove the search that hired **[[Todd Combs]]** — Buffett noting that Lou
  himself had been "little-known" when he joined GEICO in 1979.
