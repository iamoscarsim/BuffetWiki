# Tony Nicely

Tony Nicely runs the insurance side of [[GEICO]], the role he held when Berkshire bought the
whole company in 1995–96 ([[1995]]). Then **52, with 34 years at GEICO**, Buffett called him
the manager he'd most want there: "He has brains, energy, integrity and focus. If we're
lucky, he'll stay another 34 years." Under Nicely and his team, GEICO pushed underwriting and
loss-adjustment expenses down to **23.6% of premiums** in 1995 (nearly a point below 1994) —
the rock-bottom cost advantage "virtually no competitor can match," widening GEICO's
[[Economic Moat]]. He becomes one of the central operating managers of the post-1996
Berkshire, paired with **[[Lou Simpson]]** (who runs GEICO's investments). This page will
track his long tenure.

## Evolution
- [1995] Introduced as GEICO's insurance CEO (52, 34 years at GEICO) at the full acquisition;
  drove the expense ratio to 23.6% and "widened the moat."
- [1996] First full year running GEICO under Berkshire — "a superb business manager and a delight
  to work with." Voluntary auto policies grew **10%** (best-ever was 8%) while underwriting targets
  were beaten; Nicely's bonus (and the whole company's profit-sharing, a record 16.9% / $40M) is
  tied to just two variables — voluntary-policy growth and seasoned-business underwriting profit
  (see [[Executive Compensation]]). Buffett calls him and [[Lou Simpson]] "**Hall-of-Famers in both
  key positions**."
- [1997] "If I had known then what he had in store for us in 1997, I would have searched for still
  greater superlatives." Now **54, with 36 years at GEICO**, Nicely had "his best" year — voluntary
  policies in-force +16%, an 8.1% underwriting margin (so high that GEICO *cut* rates), and a record
  26.9% / $71M profit-sharing payout. "As CEO, he has transmitted vision, energy and enthusiasm to
  all members of the GEICO family — raising their sights from what *has* been achieved to what *can*
  be achieved."
- [1998] "**There is no one in the business world who could run GEICO better than Tony does. His
  instincts are unerring, his energy is boundless, and his execution is flawless.**" GEICO "shot
  the lights out" — in-force 3,562,644, profit-sharing a record $103M / 32.3% of salary. Buffett
  uses Nicely as the exemplar of Berkshire's hands-off environment: "Charlie and I haven't taught
  Tony a thing — and never will — but we *have* created an environment that allows him to apply all
  of his talents to what's important" (see [[Decentralization]]).
- [1999] "When Berkshire purchased GEICO at the beginning of 1996, we handed the keys to Tony and
  asked him to run the operation exactly as if he owned 100% of it. He has done the rest." Market
  share climbed 2.7% → 4.1%, in-force to 4,328,900, with a $113.3M / 28.4% profit-sharing payout —
  "Tony and I love writing those checks." Buffett vows his "foot will be on" Nicely's as the
  advertising pedal stays floored.
- [2000] In a hard year (policy growth halted, profit-sharing down to $40.7M), Nicely is "**an
  owner's dream. Everything he does makes sense. He never engages in wishful thinking or otherwise
  distorts reality**." He cut cost-ineffective advertising, "aggressively filed for price increases
  where we need them," and "looks at the loss reports every day and is never behind the curve." "To
  steal a line from a competitor, we are in good hands with Tony."
- [[2018]] **Retirement — "The company and the man are inseparable."** Nicely **retired as CEO on June
  30, 2018**, handing the job to long-time partner **[[Bill Roberts]]** (Nicely remained Chairman, "helpful
  to GEICO for the rest of his life. He's incapable of doing less"). Buffett's career verdict, with the
  full arc retold (joined 1961 at 18; promoted to CEO 1993 with just 1.9M policies, ranked 7th): under
  Nicely, GEICO became **America's #2 auto insurer**, sales grew **1,200%**, pre-tax underwriting profit
  since the 1996 buyout totaled **$15.5 billion**, and float grew **$2.5B → $22.1B** — Buffett estimates
  Nicely's management **"increased Berkshire's intrinsic value by more than $50 billion."** "He is a model
  for everything a manager should be… All Berkshire shareholders owe Tony their thanks. I head the list."
