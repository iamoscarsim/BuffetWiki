# Seth Schofield

Seth Schofield became CEO of [[USAir]] in 1991, inheriting an airline — and an
industry — in crisis just as Berkshire's $358M convertible preferred was being marked
down ([[1991]]). Buffett describes him as "making major adjustments in the airline's
operations in order to improve its chances of being one of the few industry
survivors," and credits him with the three things the job demands: "brains, guts, and
experience." The endorsement is pointed precisely because the surrounding economics
are so bad — Buffett's standing view that the airline industry, in aggregate, "has
posted a net loss since its birth after Kitty Hawk," with bankrupt carriers waging a
"burn-the-furniture-to-provide-firewood" fare war. Schofield is the capable manager
trapped in a business that "can be killed" regardless (see [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]).

## Evolution
- [1991] Introduced as USAir's new CEO, fighting for the airline's survival amid an
  industry-wide collapse.
- [1992] Buffett calls his repositioning of USAir "truly extraordinary" and singles out his
  courage in **accepting (and quickly settling) a fall-1992 strike** rather than capitulating
  to wage costs and work rules "considerably more onerous" than competitors' — recognizing
  "any high-cost producer faced extinction." When British Airways invested in early 1993,
  Buffett and Munger joined the USAir board.
- [1994] Schofield "has worked diligently to correct the company's historical cost problems
  but, to date, has not managed to do so" — fighting a "moving target" as rivals won labor
  concessions and shed costs in bankruptcy. With the preferred dividend suspended and the stake
  written down, Buffett and Munger stepped off the USAir board but offered to keep advising
  Schofield.
