# Charlie Munger

Charles T. Munger is Buffett's longtime partner, Berkshire's Vice Chairman, and
the intellectual counterweight repeatedly credited in the letters. Though not yet
named in the [[1977]] letter itself, he is inseparable from this era: he chaired
[[Wesco Financial]] and was central to the [[Blue Chip Stamps]] complex through
which [[See's Candies]] was bought. Munger is generally credited with pushing
Buffett away from "cigar-butt" bargain investing toward paying fair prices for
**wonderful businesses** with durable economics — the philosophy See's came to
embody.

## Evolution
- [1977] Active in the Blue Chip / Wesco structure of this period (not yet named in
  the letter text). Pages here will track his growing presence as the letters name
  him directly.
- [1979] His influence shows even unnamed: the letter's pivot to
  [[Good Business at a Fair Price]] ("turnarounds seldom turn") reflects the
  Munger view that pushed Buffett beyond Graham-style bargain-hunting.
- [1980] Credited (with Louie Vincenti) for "the superb job... repositioning"
  [[Mutual Savings and Loan]] ahead of the S&L industry's troubles.
- [1981] Named explicitly: **Vice Chairman of Berkshire and Chairman of Blue Chip**,
  and originator of the [[Shareholder-Designated Contributions]] program. "Charlie
  and I work as partners in managing all controlled companies. To almost a sinful
  degree, we enjoy our work as managing partners."
- [1983] Became **Chairman of [[Wesco Financial]]** (succeeding Louie Vincenti).
  Operates from Los Angeles; he and Buffett are "interchangeable in business
  decisions" ("a telephone call... more productive than a half-day committee
  meeting").
- [1987] His problem-solving role dramatized in the *déjà vu* close: when Buffett
  "went to Charlie with problems that were too tough for me to solve" (Dempster Mill in
  1962), Munger produced the fixer [[Harry Bottle]] — and did so again, on his own, for
  K&W Products in 1986–87 ("Charlie… knew there was no need to consult me"). Oversees
  K&W and Wesco; supplies the David Ogilvy "develop your eccentricities while you are
  young" credo behind the permanent-holdings stance.
- [1988] As overseer of [[Mutual Savings and Loan]], Munger authors a Wesco-letter
  analysis Buffett calls "the best description I have seen of the events that produced
  the present savings-and-loan crisis," and details Berkshire's new [[Freddie Mac]]
  position. His "Okay, at least it will keep you out of bars" is the running sign-off
  after [[Arbitrage|arbitrage]] discussions.
- [1989] His May 30, 1989 letter resigning Berkshire (Wesco) from the **U.S. League of
  Savings Institutions** — "convey[ing] our disgust with its policies" amid the S&L crisis
  — is reprinted in the annual report. Buffett credits Munger for understanding the
  good-business lesson early ("Charlie understood this… I was a slow learner"); his idea
  of traveling in style is "an air-conditioned bus… only when bargain fares are in effect,"
  and he named the upgraded jet "**The Indefensible**."
- [[2022]] **"Nothing Beats Having a Great Partner."** Buffett devotes a closing section to Munger,
  printing a page of his aphorisms ("what it takes me a page to explain, he sums up in a sentence"): "**All
  I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there**"; "A great company keeps working after
  you are not; a mediocre company won't"; "**A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal
  zero**" (on the danger of leverage); "Warren and I **hated railroad stocks for decades, but the world
  changed**… better late than never"; and his decision-clincher, "**Warren, think more about it. You're
  smart and I'm right.**" Buffett's own appended rule: "**Find a very smart high-grade partner — preferably
  slightly older than you — and then listen very carefully to what he says.**" *(This would be the last
  letter Buffett wrote with Munger alive.)*
- [[2023]] **Died November 28, 2023 — "The Architect of Berkshire Hathaway."** Munger died **33 days short
  of his 100th birthday**, and Buffett opens the letter with a one-page eulogy. The defining story: in
  **1965**, with no stake of his own in the partnership, Munger told Buffett that buying control of
  Berkshire had been "a dumb decision" — then gave the instruction that remade the company: "**Forget about
  ever buying another company like Berkshire… add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and
  give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices. In other words, abandon everything you learned from
  your hero, Ben Graham**" (the doctrine behind [[See's Candies]], [[Good Business at a Fair Price]]).
  Buffett's verdict: "**In reality, Charlie was the 'architect' of the present Berkshire, and I acted as
  the 'general contractor'**… part older brother, part loving father. Even when he knew he was right, he
  gave me the reins, and when I blundered he never — never — reminded me of my mistake." (The new 4th
  edition of *Poor Charlie's Almanack* is offered at the 2024 meeting; Munger had "lived under 15 of
  America's 45 presidents.")
