# Evolving Views — Dividends

How Buffett thinks about whether a company should pay dividends or retain earnings.
The principle has been remarkably stable — retain only if it creates value — but
the letters develop it with increasing rigor, and Berkshire's own (non-)dividend
practice is the live illustration.

## Timeline

- **[[1978]]–[[1983]]** — Early statements within [[Capital Allocation]]: retain only
  if reinvestment beats payout/buyback; the **$1 retention test** (each retained
  dollar should create ≥$1 of market value), formalized on a five-year rolling basis.
- **[[1984]]** — The full **dividend-policy essay**. Buffett splits earnings into:
  - **Restricted earnings** — "ersatz" earnings an asset-heavy business must
    reinvest just to maintain its position (Con Ed's "Dig We Must," reinvesting at
    25¢-on-the-dollar market value); these cannot prudently be paid out.
  - **Unrestricted earnings** — freely distributable, and the real subject. Retain
    these *only* when "for every dollar retained... at least one dollar of market
    value will be created." The **perpetual-bond-coupon analogy** shows why: take the
    coupon in cash or in more bonds depending on whether the reinvestment rate beats
    the market rate.
  He attacks the "schizoid" CEO who demands payout justification from subsidiaries
  but not from himself, and the **"Pro-Am golf"** effect — a great core business's
  high returns camouflage "disastrous" capital allocation elsewhere. Verdict: "if
  earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been
  unwisely retained." Dividends should still be **consistent and predictable**.
  Berkshire retains everything because it has cleared the test — paying out early
  would have been "disastrous." (Berkshire pays no dividend to this day, save a
  single 1967 payment Buffett jokes he must have been "in the bathroom" for.)
- **[[2012]]** — **The definitive dividend essay, with the "sell-off" math.** Responding to
  shareholders who'd like a cash dividend, Buffett lays out the **priority order for a profitable
  company's earnings**: (1) reinvest in the existing business (moat-widening — Berkshire's breadth
  lets it "**water the flowers and skip over the weeds**"); (2) acquisitions that raise *per-share*
  value; (3) **buybacks** when the stock is below intrinsic value; and only then (4) a dividend. He
  then proves with arithmetic that for a company compounding **above book value**, a **"sell-off"
  policy** (retain everything; an owner who wants cash periodically sells a sliver of shares) leaves
  that owner **wealthier** than a fixed dividend would — because the retained dollars keep compounding
  and the owner liquidates a *shrinking* fraction of a *growing* pie, whereas a dividend forcibly
  distributes the *same* proportion to everyone. Crucial caveat: this holds only while the stock
  trades **at or above book** (Berkshire's does). He repeats the textile mea culpa as the cautionary
  case of *bad* retention ("wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in
  business"). Berkshire pays nothing because it has cleared the reinvestment test for decades.
