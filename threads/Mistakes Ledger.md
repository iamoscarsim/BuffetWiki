# Mistakes Ledger

Buffett is unusually candid about his errors, and the letters return to them
deliberately. This thread collects admitted mistakes — of commission and omission
— as they appear, year by year.

## Ledger

- **[[1977]]** — *Textile forecasting.* "We have mistakenly predicted better
  results in each of the last two years" for [[Berkshire Hathaway Textiles]];
  Buffett questions his own forecasting and the wisdom of staying in a structurally
  poor business. *Insurance missteps of the prior decade:* a 1969 surety operation,
  the 1973 expansion of Home & Auto into Miami, an unresolved aviation "fronting"
  arrangement, and California Worker's Compensation problems. Frames the lesson:
  prefer businesses with **tailwinds** over **headwinds** — a lesson "learned and,
  unfortunately, sometimes re-learned."
- **[[1978]]** — *Insurance expansion.* Buffett warns shareholders against
  "unrestrained joy" at his intent to expand insurance: "Some of our expansion
  efforts — largely initiated by your Chairman — have been lackluster, others have
  been expensive failures." Concedes "it is easier to buy [a good insurance
  business] than create one." Homestate underwriting disappointing; California
  Worker's Comp described as having been "a disaster" before Frank DeNardo.
- **[[1979]]** — *Waumbec Mills.* Buying the New Hampshire textile mill (a
  statistical bargain, below working capital) was "a mistake" — the origin of
  **"turnarounds seldom turn"** and [[Good Business at a Fair Price]]. *Bonds.*
  Confesses buying 15-year bonds and, worse, not selling them as his inflation
  views crystallized; says these mistakes "should be viewed less charitably" than
  others' because Berkshire saw the danger and acted only half-heartedly.
- **[[1980]]** — *Waumbec, again.* On terminating Waumbec: "your Chairman made a
  costly mistake in not facing the realities of this situation sooner." *Bonds,
  again.* Admits Berkshire "lost important sums in bonds because your Chairman was
  talking when he should have been acting" — and that its asset/liability maturities
  remain "far more mismatched than we would wish."
- **[[1981]]** — *Aluminum about-face.* Having touted "the rosy future of the
  aluminum business" in 1980, Buffett notes "several minor adjustments... now
  aggregating approximately 180 degrees" — and trimmed the Alcoa/Kaiser positions.
  *Self-assessment on acquisitions:* "Our preaching was better than our performance.
  We neglected the Noah principle: predicting rain doesn't count, building arks
  does." Quotes Sam Goldwyn on the danger of forecasts, ribbing his own past textile
  predictions.
- **[[1982]]** — *A near-miss counted as a win.* Buffett says his "major
  accomplishment of the year was that a very large purchase to which we had firmly
  committed was unable to be completed" — he'd "left the room once too often" (Pascal)
  and nearly joined the "Acquisition Follies of 1982." A reminder that avoided
  mistakes count too. (See [[Premium Takeovers]].)
- **[[1983]]** — *Insurance errors come home to roost.* Berkshire's own combined ratio
  hit 121: "I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost,"
  rooted in decisions made when Buffett had direct managerial responsibility for
  insurance. *Mistakes of omission.* Confesses that a long-held bias toward tangible
  assets (taught by Graham) "caused me to make many important business mistakes of
  omission" — passing on franchise businesses; cites Keynes on "escaping from the old
  [ideas]." (See [[Economic Goodwill]].)
- **[[1984]]** — *Loss-reserving errors* (see [[Loss Reserving]]). Buffett publishes a
  table showing Berkshire's underwriting losses were repeatedly understated, and
  apologizes: "my errors in reporting to you have been substantial and recently have
  always presented a better underwriting picture than was truly the case" — costing
  shareholders accurate information and causing tax overpayments.
- **[[1985]]** — *Staying in textiles too long.* On closing the mill: "I should be
  faulted for not quitting sooner." He'd believed what he "preferred to believe,"
  ignoring Comte's warning that the intellect should be the servant of the heart "but
  not its slave." Also the famous *Cap Cities round-trip*: having sold the stock at
  $43 in 1978–80, he repurchased at $172.50 ("A little more time, please"). Reserving
  still ran short ("the real mistake is not the act, but the actor").
- **[[1986]]** — *Reserving, a third year running.* The 1985 reserve development showed
  "what I told you last year about our loss liabilities was far from true — and that
  makes three years in a row of error... If the physiological rules that applied to
  Pinocchio were to apply to me, my nose would now draw crowds." (See [[Loss Reserving]].)
  *Self-criticism on capital allocation:* "my performance did not match that of our
  managers" — while operators built business value, Buffett "was unable to skillfully
  deploy much of the capital they generated," drawing blanks in marketable equities.
- **[[1987]]** — *Twenty years to learn the obvious.* Buffett confesses "your Chairman,
  always a quick study, required only 20 years to recognize how important it was to buy
  good businesses. In the interim, I searched for 'bargains' — and had the misfortune to
  find some," earning "an education in the economics of short-line farm implement
  manufacturers, third-place department stores, and New England textile manufacturers."
  The pivot to [[Good Business at a Fair Price]] is framed as the lesson of those
  mistakes. (No fresh operating blunder this year; reserve development "looks better,"
  though long-tail uncertainty persists.)
- **[[1988]]** — *A (gentle, self-deprecating) omission.* Buffett says he "blundered" in
  1983 by "neglecting to ask Mrs. B a question any schoolboy would have thought of: 'Are
  there any more at home like you?'" — the relatives running [[Borsheim's]], whom
  Berkshire only bought into in 1989. **The major 25-year mistakes essay falls in the
  [[1989]] entry below.** More an admission of a slow study than a costly
  error, paired with his confession that newly-minted MBAs "too often… are short on
  personal commitment… and general business savvy."
- **[[1989]]** — *The landmark "Mistakes of the First Twenty-Five Years."* A consolidated
  reckoning: (1) **Buying control of Berkshire itself** — a "cigar-butt" textile bargain;
  "never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen"; "**Time is the friend of the
  wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre**." (2) **Hochschild Kohn** — the Baltimore
  department store bought via [[Diversified Retailing Company]] at a discount with
  "first-class people"; sold three years later "for about what I had paid" ("good jockeys"
  can't fix "broken-down nags"). (3) The **[[Institutional Imperative]]** — "my most
  surprising discovery," the force that makes rational managers irrational. (4) **Errors
  of omission** ("thumb-sucking") — Buffett "passed on a couple of really big purchases"
  he fully understood; "the cost of this thumb-sucking has been huge." (5) Only
  associating with people he likes/trusts/admires ("we've never succeeded in making a good
  deal with a bad person"). He explicitly judges Berkshire's **conservative finance NOT a
  mistake**: even at 99:1 odds he'd refuse leverage, since "a small chance of distress…
  cannot be offset by a large chance of extra returns." ("I was Snow White, but I drifted.")
- **[[1990]]** — *USAir, an "unforced error."* Buffett confesses on the [[USAir]] preferred:
  "your Chairman displayed exquisite timing: I plunged into the business at almost the exact
  moment that it ran into severe problems… No one pushed me; in tennis parlance, I committed
  an 'unforced error.'" He'd underrated both the Piedmont-merger turmoil and airline
  economics ("impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor"); the position is
  marked down "substantially." *Insurance, again:* on pre-Goldberg errors, "in the insurance
  business, there is no statute of limitations on stupidity."
- **[[1991]]** — *"Mistake Du Jour": the [[Fannie Mae]] thumb-suck.* Buffett abandons his
  plan to wait until 2015 for the next mistakes essay and confesses a fresh **error of
  omission**. In early 1988 he set out to buy 30M shares of Federal National Mortgage
  Association (a $350–400M position) — a business he understood and a CEO (David Maxwell) he
  admired. After buying ~7M shares the price climbed; "in frustration, I stopped buying," and
  then, "in an even sillier move, I surrendered to my distaste for holding small positions and
  sold the 7 million shares we owned." The estimated forgone gain by year-end 1991: **~$1.4
  billion**. "I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation… But there isn't one."
  He notes he avoided repeating the stop-buying error with [[Coca-Cola]]. Reaffirms that the
  worst mistakes are usually of **omission** — invisible, but no less costly.
- **[[1992]]** — *The 1982 blown deal, finally explained.* Buffett reveals what the
  near-miss he'd treated as a lucky escape in [[1982]] actually was: "In 1982 I made a huge
  mistake in committing to buy a company **burdened by extraordinary post-retirement health
  obligations**." The transaction "fell through for reasons beyond our control" — and then,
  worse than he'd realized, "another buyer appeared, the business soon went bankrupt and was
  shut down, and thousands of workers found those bountiful health-care promises to be
  largely worthless." The episode underwrites his rule of avoiding companies with big
  post-retirement liabilities. *[[USAir]], reaffirmed:* "even my mother acknowledges" the
  1989 confession of ignorance about airline economics — "it was I who happily jumped into
  the pool; no one pushed me"; he hadn't expected the industry's "prolonged kamikaze
  behavior."
- **[[1993]]** — *No new error confessed — but the seed of the biggest one is planted.* The
  **[[Dexter Shoe]]** acquisition (closed Nov 7, paid in **25,203 Berkshire shares**) is
  presented as a triumph: "one of the best-managed companies Charlie and I have seen." In time
  it becomes Buffett's **most-regretted deal** — domestic shoe manufacturing was hollowed out
  by imports, and, far worse, he paid in *appreciating Berkshire stock* rather than cash,
  compounding the loss. (This entry flags the deal; the explicit mea culpa arrives in later
  letters and will be recorded then.) The 1993 letter is otherwise a victory lap; its candor
  shows instead in the **taxes** discussion (no complaint about a ~$390M bill) and the
  reaffirmed discipline "there's no use running if you're on the wrong road" — resisting
  marginal deals just because Berkshire is "long on cash."
- **[[1994]]** — *"Mistake Du Jour… a vintage year with keen competition for the gold medal."*
  **Gold medal: [[USAir]].** The $358M preferred, an "unforced error" he'd named in 1990, "fully
  ripened" when the dividend was suspended in September 1994. Buffett owns it as "**sloppy
  analysis**… caused by the fact that we were buying a senior security or by hubris": he failed
  to see that in "an unregulated commodity business, a company must lower its costs to
  competitive levels or face extinction." Written down to **$89.5M (25¢ on the dollar)**, a
  $269M charge; he and Munger leave the board. Herb Kelleher: "Bankruptcy court for airlines has
  become a health spa." The redemptive maxim: "**You don't have to make it back the way that you
  lost it.**" **Silver medal: selling [[Capital Cities]] too early** — 10M shares dumped at $63
  in late 1993, worth $85.25 a year later (−$222.5M); having also sold at $4.30 in 1978–80,
  "now I've become a **repeat offender**… Maybe it's time to get a guardian appointed."
- **[[1995]]** — *The [[Gillette]] preferred — "my biggest mistake… never recognized on
  financial statements."* In a remarkable confession, Buffett judges his **best** convertible-
  preferred holding to also be his costliest error: by taking $600M of Gillette *preferred* in
  1989 (convertible into 48M split-adjusted shares) instead of negotiating for **common** (~60M
  shares at ~$10.50), he gave up roughly **$625M** of gain (net of ~$70M of "excess" preferred
  dividends) — "I was far too clever." He also revisits the **[[Capital Cities]]** sale with the
  Disney merger now pricing it: the third sold in 1993 for **$635M** would fetch **$1.27 billion**
  in the Disney deal — "don't… be as dumb as I was." On **[[Salomon]]**, the conversion option he
  prized "must be graded as very poor"; and on his blanket 1989 claim that the preferreds would
  "under almost any conditions… return us our money plus dividends" — "**that's one I would like
  to have back**" (Churchill: "eating my words has never given me indigestion"). The **early-1970s
  reinsurance** mistakes (his own, when he ran the operation) still generate bills 20+ years on —
  "a bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit."
- **[[1996]]** — *The [[USAir]] error gets a reprieve — but the verdict stands.* The 1994 "gold
  medal" mistake partly unwound: USAir turned profitable in H2 1996, paid **$47.9M** of arrears
  (compounded at the contract's prime+5% "penalty dividend"), and the preferred recovered to
  roughly its **$358M par**. Yet Buffett refuses to recast the original call as anything but
  wrong — his analysis was "**superficial and wrong**," "beguiled by the company's long history
  of profitable operations" while ignoring that deregulated revenues would meet a regulation-era
  cost structure ("If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of
  librarians"). His two failed attempts to *sell* at a loss (1995 at 50% of par, early 1996 at
  ~$335M) are the joke on himself: "I again failed in my attempt to **snatch defeat from the jaws
  of victory**." A recovery, not a vindication — "If you're so rich, why aren't you smart?"
- **[[1997]]** — *The "confession" on issuing stock, and the [[USAir]] miracle.* In a candid
  section titled **"A Confession,"** Buffett tallies every stock-only merger (ex-Diversified
  Retailing and Blue Chip) and concludes "**our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would
  have been had I not done the transactions … when I've issued stock, I've cost you money.**" The
  cause isn't bad sellers but a great roster: trading away ".380 hitters" for something new "almost
  never makes sense" (see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]). Separately, **[[USAir]]** — the 1994
  gold-medal error — is now "**unblemished by success**" on the *downside* too: he was "wrong in
  originally purchasing the stock, and … wrong later, in repeatedly trying to unload our holdings at
  50 cents on the dollar," before Stephen Wolf's turnaround handed Berkshire a likely "decent" (even
  "indecent") profit. The lesson is humility about even confessed errors: a salvaged mistake is luck
  meeting a great manager, not foresight.
- **[[1998]]** — *Selling [[McDonald's]] — "a very big mistake."* In a candid "confession (ugh),"
  Buffett admits that his 1998 portfolio actions actually **decreased** the year's gain, singling
  out the McDonald's sale: "you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off
  to the movies during market hours." A rare error of *commission* in the equity book — selling a
  fine consumer franchise — distinct from his usual confessions of omission. (The same letter notes
  that several *retained* investees — Coca-Cola, Disney, others — had "operating shortfalls,"
  pulling the portfolio below the S&P, though Buffett deemed those problems "almost certainly
  temporary.")
- **[[1999]]** — *"My grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D."* Buffett opens his **worst year** (book
  value +0.5%, the worst absolute and relative performance of his tenure) by naming the "guilty
  party": himself. "My 'one subject' is **capital allocation**," and the **equity portfolio** he
  runs (ex-[[Lou Simpson]]) badly trailed the market as [[Coca-Cola]], [[Gillette]] and others
  stumbled — "their stumbles damaged our performance … and it's no sure thing that they will quickly
  regain their stride." Two longer-running threads ripen here: **[[Dexter Shoe]]** is now visibly
  failing (93% of U.S. shoe purchases are imported), the import wave validating that the 1993
  stock-funded purchase was a serious error (the explicit mea culpa comes in later letters); and in
  the buyback essay Buffett admits he has at times **erred by *not* repurchasing** Berkshire shares
  when they were cheap (see [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]).
- **[[2000]]** — *The [[Dexter Shoe]] mea culpa — at last, explicit.* "**I clearly made a mistake in
  paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by
  using Berkshire shares in payment.**" Berkshire wrote off **all remaining accounting goodwill**
  from the deal — "we clearly have [no economic goodwill] at present." It becomes the textbook case
  for [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]: the true cost was not Dexter's lost earnings but the
  *forgone compounding of the Berkshire stock surrendered* (a stake later worth multiples of
  Dexter). Buffett also owns a fresh operating error: the **[[GEICO]] advertising** ramp-up he'd
  championed — "I told you last year that we would get our money's worth … but I was wrong" — the
  extra dollars failed to lift inquiries commensurately. And, in the Aesop essay, he lists his own
  circle-of-competence failures: "the fellow … who thought he understood the future economics of
  **trading stamps, textiles, shoes and second-tier department stores**."
- **[[2001]]** — *The September-11 underwriting failure — "I violated the Noah rule."* Buffett's
  central confession of the year is institutional and his own: he "allowed [[General Re]] to take
  on business without a safeguard I knew was important, and on September 11th, this error caught up
  with us." General Re broke all three [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|underwriting rules]],
  pricing terrorism off **experience, not exposure** — "I consequently let Berkshire operate with a
  dangerous level of risk." The self-rebuke: "**Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does**"
  (the Noah rule, echoing 1981). Compounding it, General Re's **$800M of belated prior-year
  [[Loss Reserving|loss recognition]]** showed Buffett had wrongly "assured you that General Re was
  underwriting with discipline — and I have been proven wrong." *[[Dexter Shoe]], restated:* the
  three errors are listed flatly again — "(1) buying it…; (2) paying for it with stock and (3)
  **procrastinating** when the need for changes… was obvious." A new wrinkle is the candor on
  **his equity-portfolio performance**: "my performance was anything but [satisfactory]… my results
  were poor, just as they have been for several years."
- **[[2002]]** — *"I was dead wrong" about General Re.* The 2001 confession deepens into a full
  diagnosis. When Buffett merged with [[General Re]] in 1998 he believed — after "stud[ying] the
  operation for decades" — that it stuck to the three [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|rules]].
  "**I was dead wrong.** Gen Re's culture and practices had substantially changed and unbeknownst to
  management — and to me — the company was **grossly mispricing its current business**" and had
  amassed a nuclear/chemical/biological aggregation that the WTC attack "**alone would have
  threatened the company's existence**." A further **$1.31B** of prior-year reserve error surfaced
  in 2002 ("I wish I didn't know now what I didn't know then"). *Derivatives:* he also owns that
  **[[General Re Securities]]** was a business "Charlie and I didn't want," that they "failed in
  [the] attempts to sell," and that they are now unwinding at a loss — the [[Derivatives]] essay
  partly a mea culpa for having inherited it. *California workers' comp:* "our reserving severely
  missed the mark… until we figure out how to get this business right, we will keep it small."
- **[[2003]]** — *Dithering on [[General Re Securities]], and not selling into the bubble.* Two
  candid confessions. First, "**I dithered**": "Charlie would have moved swiftly to close down Gen
  Re Securities… I, however, dithered," costing shareholders "**~$100 million**, pre-tax" beyond
  what was necessary — both he and Munger had judged the derivatives business unattractive at the
  1998 merger but he failed to act. Second, on the portfolio: "**I made a big mistake in not selling
  several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble**… If these stocks are fully priced now,
  you may wonder what I was thinking four years ago when their… prices [were] far higher. So do I."
  He also owns the earlier **Gen Re under-reserving** he failed to spot ("Aaarrrggghh"), which
  caused inaccurate figures and premature tax payments.
- **[[2005]]** — *"Fault me for dithering," and "I was dead wrong" on NetJets.* Buffett's now-annual
  **[[General Re Securities]]** confession sharpens: he had cost shareholders dearly by "wast[ing]
  several years" trying to sell rather than immediately closing the derivatives unit (cumulative exit
  losses now **−$404M**) — "When a problem exists… the time to act is **now**." On **[[Executive Jet|NetJets]]**: "I said last year that this business would earn money in 2005 — and **I was dead
  wrong**" (the U.S. operation "dipped far into the red"). A milder admission threads the
  [[Gillette]] discussion — the franchise's pre-Kilts **Duracell** purchase had "**destroyed
  billions**," a capital-allocation blunder "never made visible by conventional accounting."
- **[[2007]]** — *Three "unforced errors," and the final Dexter accounting.* In the great/good/
  gruesome confession Buffett lists three. (1) He **almost blew the See's purchase** over $5M ("that
  $1.35 billion would have gone to somebody else"). (2) He **turned down Tom Murphy's $35M
  Dallas-Fort Worth NBC station** (a See's-like, capital-light business now worth ~$800M+): "**my
  brain had gone on vacation and forgot to notify me**" ("if his I.Q. was any lower, you would have
  to water him twice a day"). (3) Worst, **[[Dexter Shoe]]**, finally quantified: paying in **25,203
  Berkshire A shares** made the true cost "**not $400 million, but rather $3.5 billion**" — "I gave
  away 1.6% of a wonderful business — one now valued at $220 billion — to buy a worthless business…
  the **worst deal that I've made**." The canonical [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]] lesson.
- **[[2008]]** — *"During 2008 I did some dumb things in investments."* Buffett owns "**at least one
  major mistake of commission**… several lesser ones… [and] some errors of omission." **The major
  one: [[ConocoPhillips]].** "**Without urging from Charlie or anyone else, I bought a large amount
  of ConocoPhillips stock when oil and gas prices were near their peak.**" The subsequent energy
  collapse made the timing "**dead wrong**," costing Berkshire "several billion dollars" (he still
  believed oil would eventually sell higher — the error was timing/size, not thesis). **The lesser
  ones: two Irish banks**, bought for **$244M** because they "appeared cheap," written down to
  **$27M** (an **89% loss**) by year-end and falling further — "the tennis crowd would call my
  mistakes 'unforced errors.'" He also confesses **errors of omission**, "sucking my thumb when new
  facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking." A meta-mistake is owned with
  humility on derivatives: the 1998 Gen Re wind-down (23,218 contracts) proved he and Munger
  couldn't "get our minds around" a huge derivatives book — "include Charlie and me in this hapless
  group."
- **[[2009]]** — *"A painful confession": the GEICO credit card, and the NetJets near-disaster.*
  Two self-inflicted wounds. (1) **The [[GEICO]] credit card** — "**a very expensive business fiasco
  entirely of my own making**." Buffett had long sought side-products for GEICO's customers and
  "finally succeeded, coming up with a brilliant insight that we should market our own credit card."
  GEICO's managers warned that instead of the "cream" of customers he'd get "the… non-cream"; "I
  subtly indicated that I was older and wiser. **I was just older.**" Cost: ~$6.3M of operating
  losses plus a $44M loss dumping the $98M receivables book at 55¢. (2) **[[Executive Jet|NetJets]]**
  — "**It's clear that I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this condition**": an 11-year
  aggregate pre-tax loss of $157M and debt ballooning from $102M to $1.9B (a $711M loss in 2009
  alone) before [[David Sokol]] rescued it. (3) A milder regret of *omission*: he "**should have done
  far more**" buying ridiculously cheap corporate and municipal bonds in 2008 — "**when it's raining
  gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble.**"
- **[[2010]]** — *Capital-allocation errors, owned in the aggregate.* No single new "Mistake Du Jour,"
  but a candid line on the Mfg/Svc/Retail roster: while most units earn 12–100% on unleveraged
  tangible assets, "a few have **very poor returns, a result of some serious mistakes I have made in my
  job of capital allocation**" — buying businesses whose "competitive strength" or "future economics"
  he "misjudged" because "sometimes my eyesight has been poor." He also re-mocks his foundational
  blunder — pouring money into textiles in 1965 and then "**in a final burst of brilliance, I went out
  and bought another textile company. Aaaaaaargh!**" The year's positive flip-side is **[[Executive Jet|NetJets]]**, the prior confession now resolved by [[David Sokol]]'s turnaround.
- **[[2011]]** — *"A big mistake" — the [[Energy Future Holdings]] bonds.* Buffett confesses spending
  ~**$2 billion** on bonds of the Texas utility EFH, "a mistake — **a big mistake**." The bet rode on
  natural-gas prices, which "tanked shortly after our purchase and remain[ed] depressed"; Berkshire
  wrote the position down $1B (2010) + $390M (2011) to $878M (collecting ~$102M/yr of interest
  meanwhile). "However things turn out, **I totally miscalculated the gain/loss probabilities…** In
  tennis parlance, this was a **major unforced error** by your chairman." He also owns being "**dead
  wrong**" in last year's forecast that "a housing recovery will probably begin within a year or so."
  And he repeats the standing admission on the Mfg/Svc/Retail laggards — "a result of some serious
  mistakes I made in my job of capital allocation… sometimes my eyesight has been poor. Charlie's has
  been better; he voted no more than 'present' on several of my errant purchases."
- **[[2014]]** — *The 50-year reckoning: buying Berkshire itself was the original blunder.* In his
  Golden Anniversary essay Buffett delivers his most candid self-audit. **(1) Berkshire control** —
  out of pique at Seabury Stanton's reneged $11⅜ tender, he bought control of a dying New England
  textile maker "**a decision that eventually diverted $100 billion or so**" from his partners "to a
  collection of strangers"; compounding it, **Waumbec Mills** (1975) "was a disaster." **(2) The
  [[Dexter Shoe]] error, re-quantified** — paying in stock now worth **$5.7 billion** for a business
  whose value "went to zero": "As a financial disaster, this one deserves a spot in the **Guinness Book
  of World Records**." The lesson hardens: "**Trading shares of a wonderful business… for ownership of
  a so-so business irreparably destroys value**" — "**you can't get rich trading a hundred-dollar bill
  for eight tens**" (see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]). **(3)** Munger's companion essay adds
  that the costliest errors were of **omission** — e.g. not buying **Walmart** — "Berkshire's net worth
  would now be at least $50 billion higher" had it seized "several opportunities it was not quite smart
  enough to recognize as virtually sure things."
- **[[2020]]** — *The [[Precision Castparts]] write-down — "I paid too much."* An **$11 billion** GAAP
  write-down (the bulk of it PCC) is "almost entirely the quantification of a mistake I made in 2016."
  Buffett is precise about the *type* of error: "**No one misled me… I was simply too optimistic about
  PCC's normalized profit potential**," a misjudgment "laid bare by adverse developments throughout the
  aerospace industry" in the COVID year. He maintains PCC is "a fine company — the best in its business"
  with an excellent CEO (**[[Mark Donegan]]**); his error was the *price* — "I was wrong… in judging the
  average amount of future earnings and, consequently, wrong in my calculation of the proper price to pay."
  "**PCC is far from my first error of that sort. But it's a big one.**" (A classic overpayment error,
  distinct from the stock-funded [[Dexter Shoe]] / [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]] family.)
- **[[2023]]** — *BHE regulatory returns — "a costly mistake."* In a year of "severe earnings
  disappointment" at **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]**, Buffett owns a misjudgment of *kind*, not just degree:
  the long-stable utility "social compact" (a fixed, satisfactory regulated return) "has been broken in a
  few states," exposing BHE to open-ended **wildfire liabilities** (PacifiCorp) and the bankruptcy fates of
  PG&E and Hawaiian Electric. "**I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in
  regulatory returns and, along with Berkshire's two partners at BHE, I made a costly mistake in not doing
  so.**" A rare admission about the *regulated-utility* thesis Buffett had championed since 1999. (He also
  notes BNSF's margins slipping versus peers — an expectations error, if not a capital-allocation one.)
- **[[2024]]** — *"Mistakes — Yes, We Make Them at Berkshire" — candor as policy.* Buffett opens the letter
  with a meta-reflection on error: he used "**mistake" or "error" 16 times in his 2019–23 letters**, where
  "many other huge companies have never used either word… it has generally been happy talk and pictures."
  Two kinds recur — misjudging "the future economics of a business" (capital allocation) and misjudging "the
  abilities or **fidelity** of the managers" (a "fidelity disappointment" that "can approach… a failed
  marriage"). The **cardinal sin** is Munger's "**thumb-sucking**" — "delaying the correction of mistakes":
  "Problems… cannot be wished away. They require action, however uncomfortable." The redemptive flip side:
  "**Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom**" (GEICO, Ajit Jain, finding Munger). A summing-up of
  six decades of candor rather than a single new confession.
