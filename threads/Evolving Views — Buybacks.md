# Evolving Views — Buybacks

Buffett's thinking on share repurchases — when they create value and when they
destroy it — tracked across the letters. The throughline is consistent in
principle but applied with shifting emphasis as Berkshire itself moves from
endorsing *other* companies' buybacks to eventually repurchasing its own stock.

## Timeline

- **[[1978]]** — First mention: in a low-return business, "earnings should be paid
  out or used to repurchase shares — often by far the most attractive option for
  capital utilization." (See [[Capital Allocation]].)
- **[[1980]]** — A fuller endorsement, delivered as a "short commercial." Buybacks
  by investees are greeted "with special enthusiasm": "if a fine business is selling
  in the market place for far less than intrinsic value, what more certain or more
  profitable utilization of capital can there be than significant enlargement of the
  interests of all owners at that bargain price?" Cites [[GEICO]] shrinking its
  shares from 34.2M to 21.6M as "dramatically enhancing the interests of
  shareholders." The governing rule, already implicit: **only repurchase below
  intrinsic value.**
- **[[1984]]** — Two benefits named. (1) The arithmetic: repurchases below value get
  "$2 of present value for $1." (2) The **signal** — by buying back, management
  "demonstrates" (the key word) pro-owner motivation, so the market rationally
  grants its assets a higher price/value ratio; a manager who spurns obvious
  buybacks "reveals more than he knows," and "his heart is not listening to his
  mouth." Praises buybacks by GEICO, Washington Post, General Foods, Exxon. Sharp
  contrast drawn with **greenmail** — "odious and repugnant" — where a corporate
  raider and frightened insiders settle "at any price — as long as the price is paid
  by someone else."
- **[[1999]]** — **The definitive essay, now turned on Berkshire's own stock.** The lone
  justification for a buyback is two facts together: (1) the company has "**funds … beyond the
  near-term needs of the business**" and (2) its stock sells "**below its intrinsic value,
  conservatively-calculated**" — plus the caveat that shareholders must have been "supplied all the
  information they need" to estimate that value (else insiders exploit "uninformed partners").
  Buffett indicts the 1990s buyback fad as "all too often made for an unstated and … **ignoble
  reason: to pump or support the stock price**," or to offset option exercises ("buy high, sell
  low"): "**Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around.**" Option
  and repurchase decisions "should stand on [their] own feet." He admits the symmetric error — "at
  certain times in the past, I have **erred in not making repurchases**" when Berkshire was cheap
  (though thin volume limited the gain). With the A shares recently below **$45,000**, Berkshire
  *considered* buying, will wait until owners read the report, and would transact only **at or below
  the NYSE bid** (favoring B if >2% below A) — "**We will never make purchases with the intention of
  stemming a decline.**" *(This is the conceptual groundwork for Berkshire's later buyback eras.)*
- **[[2011]]** — **Berkshire authorizes its own buyback — at a stated price.** In September 2011 Buffett
  announced Berkshire would repurchase shares **at up to 110% of book value** (it bought just $67M
  before the price ran away). The two conditions are restated cleanly: (1) ample funds beyond the
  business's operational/liquidity needs and (2) a price "**at a material discount to… intrinsic
  business value, conservatively calculated**" — with the **"first law of capital allocation: what is
  smart at one price is dumb at another"** (Jamie Dimon of J.P. Morgan praised as a model). Two new
  guardrails: Berkshire will **never buy if cash falls below $20 billion** ("financial strength that is
  unquestionable takes precedence over all else"), and "we have no interest in supporting the stock"
  (bids "fade in particularly weak markets"). The companion **net-buyer lesson** (illustrated with
  **[[IBM]]**'s planned ~$50B of buybacks): a long-term holder of a repurchasing company should *wish
  the stock to languish*, since cheaper buybacks retire more shares and lift its ownership — "low
  prices became my friend" after Buffett read Chapter 8 of Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*. (See
  [[Capital Allocation]], [[Intrinsic Business Value]].)
- **[[2012]]** — **The limit raised to 120% of book — and the first big repurchase.** In December 2012
  Berkshire **lifted its buyback ceiling from 110% to 120% of book value** and bought **$1.3 billion**
  of stock, including a ~$1.2B block from the estate of a long-time shareholder (at ~116% of book). The
  doctrine is unchanged — repurchase only below conservatively-estimated intrinsic value, never below
  the cash floor — but the higher limit reflects Buffett's view that the gap between Berkshire's
  intrinsic value and book value had widened (float and insurance goodwill being a "huge reason," see
  [[Insurance Float]]). Buybacks are formally listed as one of the **five building blocks** of
  per-share intrinsic value (with reinvestment, bolt-ons, investee growth, and the occasional big
  acquisition).
- **[[2016]]** — **The principle, distilled — and a candor twist.** Buffett devotes a full essay to
  repurchases, reducing the logic to the math of three partners buying out one (a fair price benefits the
  stayers; an excessive price harms them) and a slogan a CEO and board "should stand, join hands and in
  unison declare": "**What is smart at one price is stupid at another.**" He calls it "puzzling" that
  buyback announcements almost never name a ceiling price — something no one would omit when buying an
  *outside* business — and names the **two exceptions** when even an underpriced company should not buy
  back: when it needs the cash to protect/expand operations, or when a far more attractive acquisition
  beckons. Berkshire's own ≤**120%-of-book** authorization is reaffirmed, with the candor twist that
  repurchasing "has proved hard to do" *because* his transparency signals intrinsic value well above 120%
  of book — and he wants the stock to trade near intrinsic value, neither too high ("no fun having owners
  who are disappointed") nor too low. He rebuts the charge that buybacks are "un-American": capital is not
  scarce — "I'm not aware of any enticing project that in recent years has died for lack of capital."
- **[[2018]]** — **The 120%-of-book cap scrapped — buy back below intrinsic value, period.** In mid-2018
  Berkshire's board **removed the 120%-of-book ceiling** entirely, freeing Buffett and Munger to
  repurchase shares whenever they trade **below a conservative estimate of intrinsic value** (cash staying
  above the **$20B floor**). The change is tied to the same year's **[[Intrinsic Business Value|abandonment of book value]]** as a metric — buybacks above book but below intrinsic value *raise* per-share intrinsic
  value while *lowering* per-share book value, so a book-pegged cap had become illogical. The doctrine is
  otherwise unchanged: repurchases benefit continuing holders only when "price-sensitive" ("**blindly
  buying an overpriced stock is value-destructive**"), and shareholders must be fully informed so none
  "sell shares back to the company because he or she has been misled." Buffett also notes investees' own
  buybacks compound Berkshire's ownership (the **[[American Express]]** 12.6% → 17.9% example).
- **[[2019]]** — **The first meaningful year of Berkshire repurchases: $5 billion.** With the price/value
  equation "modestly favorable at times," Berkshire bought back **~$5B (about 1% of the company)** — its
  largest repurchase to date. The discipline is restated tersely: buy back **only if** (a) the stock sells
  "for less than it is worth" and (b) ample cash remains afterward — and "**neither of us feels any urgency
  to buy an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents.**" Buffett wants the share count to fall over
  time and "will likely become more aggressive" if the discount widens — "**We will not, however, prop the
  stock at any level.**" (Investee buybacks remain a parallel engine via [[The Power of Retained Earnings]].)
- **[[2020]]** — **A record $24.7 billion — repurchasing 5.2% of the company.** With the 120%-of-book cap
  long gone and the price attractive, Berkshire bought back **80,998 A-share-equivalents for $24.7B** —
  raising every holder's ownership of all Berkshire businesses **by 5.2% "without requiring you to so much
  as touch your wallet."** Buffett underscores the contrarian discipline: "American CEOs have an
  embarrassing record of devoting more company funds to repurchases when prices have **risen** than when
  they have tanked. **Our approach is exactly the reverse.**" The **[[Apple]] illustration** crystallizes
  the doubled effect — Apple's *own* buybacks lifted Berkshire's stake from 5.2% to 5.4% even after a sale,
  and Berkshire's repurchases mean holders "indirectly own a full **10% more of Apple**" than in mid-2018:
  "**The math of repurchases grinds away slowly, but can be powerful over time.**"
- **[[2021]]** — **$51.7B over two years — repurchases as the primary value lever.** With acquisitions and
  stocks both pricey ("low long-term interest rates push the prices of all productive investments upward"),
  repurchases became Berkshire's main path: **9% of all shares bought over 2020–21 for $51.7 billion**
  (+$1.2B more by late February 2022), lifting continuing owners' stake ~10%. Buffett restates the
  price-discipline ("it would be value-destroying… to overpay when we are buying Berkshire") and notes a
  quirk: because his shareholder base is so long-term, buyback *opportunities* are limited (low volatility/
  volume) — yet repurchases also **raise float per share** (up 25% to $99,497/A-share over the two years).
- **[[2022]]** — **"An economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue."** Amid political attacks on
  buybacks (and a new 1% federal excise tax), Buffett mounts a full defense. Berkshire repurchased **1.2%**
  of itself in 2022, and investee buybacks at **[[Apple]]** and **[[American Express]]** lifted Berkshire's
  stakes "without any cost to us." Using a three-owner auto-dealership analogy, he shows a **value-accretive
  repurchase harms no one** — and lands the line: "**When you are told that all repurchases are harmful to
  shareholders or to the country, or particularly beneficial to CEOs, you are listening to either an
  economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue (characters that are not mutually exclusive).**" The
  discipline still binds: "when a company overpays for repurchases, the continuing shareholders lose."
