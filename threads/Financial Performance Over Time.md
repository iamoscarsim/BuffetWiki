# Financial Performance Over Time

The standing quantitative record of Berkshire across the letters — book-value
growth, the per-share performance table, return on equity, insurance float, and
other key figures. Maintained as a standard: every ingested letter adds its
numbers here.

## Book value per share (the long-term scorecard)
- **9/30/1964: $19.46** (just before Buffett took control).
- **YE 1979: $335.85** (equities at market) → **20.5% compounded annually** over the
  ~15 years. ([[1979]])
- **YE 1980: $400.80** (16 years, still **20.5% compounded**). ([[1980]])
- **YE 1981: $526.02** (17 years, **21.1% compounded**, GAAP basis). ([[1981]])
- **YE 1982: $737.43** (18 years, **22.0% compounded**); net worth +$208M (+40%). ([[1982]])
- **YE 1983: $975.83** (19 years, **22.6% compounded**); +32% in the year. ([[1983]])
- **YE 1984: $1,108.77** (20 years, **22.1% compounded**); +13.6% in the year ("mediocre"). ([[1984]])
- **YE 1985: $1,643.71** (21 years, **23.2% compounded**); +48.2% ($613.6M, "Halley's Comet"). ([[1985]])
- **YE 1986: $2,073.06** (22 years, **23.3% compounded**); +26.1% ($492.5M). ([[1986]])
- **YE 1987: $2,477.47** (23 years, **23.1% compounded**); +19.5% ($464M). ([[1987]])
- **YE 1988: $2,974.52** (24 years, **23.0% compounded**); +20.0% ($569M). ([[1988]])
- **YE 1989: $4,296.01** (25 years, **23.8% compounded**); +44.4% ($1.515B). ([[1989]])
- **YE 1990: $4,612.06** (26 years, **23.2% compounded**); +7.3% ($362M). ([[1990]])
- **YE 1991: $6,437** (27 years, **23.7% compounded**); **+39.6%** ($2.1B) — but ~$1.6B
  of it from Coke + Gillette P/E expansion, not operations. ([[1991]])
- **YE 1992: $7,745** (28 years, **23.6% compounded**); +20.3% ($1.52B). ([[1992]])
- **YE 1993: $8,854** (29 years, **23.3% compounded**); +14.3% ($1.5B); market price +39%. ([[1993]])
- **YE 1994: $10,083** (30 years, **23% compounded**); +13.9% ($1.45B); net worth $11.9B. ([[1994]])
- **YE 1995: $14,426** (31 years, **23.6% compounded**); per-share +43.1% (net worth **+45.0%, +$5.3B**); market cap $43B. ([[1995]])
- **YE 1996: $19,011** (32 years, **23.8% compounded**); per-share +31.8% (net worth **+36.1%, +$6.2B**); shares up on FlightSafety + Class B issuance. ([[1996]])
- **YE 1997: $25,488** (33 years, **24.1% compounded**); per-share **+34.1%** (net worth **+$8.0B**) — but only "Quack" vs. the booming S&P. ([[1997]])
- **YE 1998: $37,801** (34 years, **24.7% compounded**); per-share **+48.3%** (net worth **+$25.9B**; total **$57.4B**) — but "not as good as it looks": most of the gain came from **share issuance** for General Re + Executive Jet, not intrinsic-value growth. ([[1998]])
- **YE 1999: $37,987** (35 years, **24.0% compounded**); per-share **+0.5%** (net worth +$358M; total **$57.8B**) — **the worst year of Buffett's tenure**, absolute and relative; equity portfolio slumped + Gen Re underwriting loss. ([[1999]])
- **YE 2000: $40,442** (36 years, **23.6% compounded**); per-share **+6.5%** (net worth +$3.96B) — "a decent year," beating the S&P (−9.1%); eight acquisitions (~$8B, 97% cash). ([[2000]])
- **YE 2001: $37,920** (37 years, **22.6% compounded**); per-share **−6.2%** (net worth **−$3.77B**) — the **first decline of the modern era**, but still beat the S&P (−11.9%) by 5.7 pts. September 11 + General Re drove the loss. ([[2001]])
- **YE 2002: $41,727** (38 years, **22.2% compounded**); per-share **+10.0%** (net worth +$6.1B) — "a banner year," beating the S&P (−22.1%) by an "aberrational" **32.1 pts**. Float to $41.2B at 1% cost. ([[2002]])
- **YE 2003: $50,498** (39 years, **22.2% compounded**); per-share **+21%** (net worth **+$13.6B**); record float **$44.2B at no cost** + **$1.7B underwriting profit**. Equity now ~50% of net worth (vs ~114% in the 1980s). ([[2003]])
- **YE 2004: $55,824** (40 years, **21.9% compounded**); per-share **+10.5%** (net worth +$8.3B) — **lagged the S&P (10.9%)** by 0.4 pts; ~**$43B cash** ("struck out" on acquisitions). Float $46.1B, **+$1.55B underwriting profit**. ([[2004]])
- **YE 2005: $59,377** (41 years, **21.5% compounded**); per-share **+6.4%** (net worth +$5.6B); beat S&P (+4.9%). **$3.4B hurricane losses** (Katrina) yet float still costless (+$53M UW profit). Five acquisitions (no stock); per-share investments $74,129. ([[2005]])
- **YE 2006: $70,281** (42 years, **21.4% compounded**); per-share **+18.4%** (net worth **+$16.9B**, a U.S. record one-year gain). No hurricanes → **$3,838M underwriting profit**; **ISCAR** (first foreign deal, $4B); **Equitas** ($7.12B/$13.9B). ([[2006]])
- **YE 2007: $78,008** (43 years, **21.1% compounded**); per-share **+11%** (net worth +$12.3B). Investments $141B; float $59B. **Marmon** (60% for $4.5B, largest cash deal); **BNSF** stake begun; **PetroChina sold** (~$3.5B gain); subprime crisis dawning. ([[2007]])

## Narrative
- **[[1977]]** — First data point in the collection. Buffett anchors performance
  on [[Return on Equity]] (19% on beginning equity), not EPS. Insurance investments
  (ex-Blue Chip) grew from $134.6M to $252.8M over two years; insurance equity
  holdings cost $106.9M / market $181.1M. Berkshire net worth history noted: ~$53M
  at the 1955 merger, shrank to $22M by 1964 under textiles.
- **[[1978]]** — Operating return on beginning equity 19.4%; per-share net worth
  ~doubled over the prior three years (~25% annual compounding from operating
  earnings + capital gains). Insurance equities grew to $129.1M cost / $216.5M
  market (from $39.3M cost in 1975); ~$112M of realized+unrealized 3-year equity
  gains while the Dow fell 852→805. Controlled businesses: ~7,000 employees,
  >$500M revenue. Sources of Earnings table introduced.
- **[[1979]]** — 18.6% on beginning equity; long-term book value milestone above.
  Equity portfolio cost $185.4M / market $336.7M, led by GEICO ($68M market). NASDAQ
  trading of Berkshire stock began. New equity base for 1980 ~$236M (at cost).
- **[[1980]]** — 17.8% on beginning equity. Common stocks cost $325.2M / market
  $529.7M; undistributed [[Look-Through Earnings]] of <20% holdings now *exceed*
  total reported operating earnings. GEICO market $105.3M. $60M of 12¾% notes due
  2005 issued for "financial firepower." Illinois National Bank divested.
- **[[1981]]** — 15.2% on beginning equity; GAAP book value $526.02 (21.1%
  compounded). Net worth grew $124M (~31%), over half from **GEICO** (market
  $199.8M). Common stocks cost $351.7M / market $639.2M. See's pre-tax $21.9M.
  Industry combined ratio 105.7 and worsening.
- **[[1982]]** — Net worth +$208M (+40%); book value $737.43 (22.0% compounded).
  Operating ROE 9.8% — yardstick **retired** for Berkshire in favor of net-worth
  growth and "economic" earnings. Common stocks cost $424.0M / market $945.6M; GEICO
  market $309.6M. Insurance underwriting loss of $(11.3M) after tax.
- **[[1983]]** — Book value $975.83 (+32%; 22.6% compounded over 19 yrs). Common stocks
  cost $566M / market $1,306M; GEICO market $398M. Berkshire's combined ratio a poor
  121 (GEICO 96). Acquired 90% of [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]]; completed full Blue
  Chip merger (1,146,909 shares out vs 1,137,778 in 1965). Intrinsic value now far
  exceeds book.
- **[[1984]]** — Book value $1,108.77 (+13.6%, "mediocre"; 22.1% over 20 yrs). Common
  stocks cost $585M / market $1,269M (GEICO $397M, Washington Post $150M, new Exxon
  position). Berkshire combined ratio a "humbling" 134. ~$139M WPPSS bonds added.
  Buffett warns ~22% history is "just that — history."
- **[[1985]]** — Net worth +$613.6M (**+48.2%**); book value $1,643.71 (23.2% over 21
  yrs). Boosted by the General Foods sale (+$338M) and a surging portfolio (GEICO
  $596M, WaPo $205M). Combined ratio improved to 111; premium volume tripled. Discount
  of Berkshire's stock to intrinsic value has vanished (slight premium now). Three big
  deals: Cap Cities/ABC, Scott Fetzer, Fireman's Fund.
- **[[1986]]** — Net worth +$492.5M (**+26.1%**); book value $2,073.06 (23.3% over 22
  yrs). Operating earnings to Berkshire jumped to $131.5M (from $92.9M), led by a much
  better insurance result (combined ratio 111 → 103) and a full year of Scott Fetzer
  and Fechheimer. Marketable equities cost $655M / market $1,874M, dominated by the
  three permanent holdings (Cap Cities/ABC $802M, GEICO $675M, WaPo $270M). With no new
  equities meeting his tests, Buffett bought ~$700M of "mediocre" tax-exempt bonds and
  paid down debt. The year's landmark is conceptual, not numeric: the **[[Owner Earnings]]**
  appendix.
- **[[1987]]** — Net worth +$464M (**+19.5%**); book value $2,477.47 (23.1% over 23 yrs).
  Operating earnings to Berkshire jumped to $214.7M as insurance investment income surged
  on rising float (combined ratio 105). The **[[Sainted Seven]]** earned ~$180M pre-tax on
  ~$175M equity (~57% after-tax ROE). The three permanent holdings crossed **$2B** of
  market value (Cap Cities/ABC $1,035M, GEICO $757M, WaPo $323M) but delivered only $11M
  of *reported* earnings vs. >$100M of look-through. Big new commitment: the **$700M
  [[Salomon]]** convertible preferred. Berkshire issued $250M of 2018 debentures
  ("loaded gun"). Stock-market activity was minimal through the 1987 crash.
- **[[1988]]** — Net worth +$569M (**+20.0%**); book value $2,974.52 (23.0% over 24 yrs).
  Operating earnings to Berkshire $313.4M (insurance investment income surged to $231M
  pre-tax on rising float; combined ratio 104). The Sainted Seven plus small units earned
  ~**67% on average equity**. Two big new stock commitments — **[[Coca-Cola]]** (cost
  $592.5M) and **[[Freddie Mac]]** — bought to "hold forever." Arbitrage added **$78M**.
  Berkshire **listed on the NYSE** and was raised to **AAA**. Buffett reframes the
  **size** drag: a 15% decade return now needs **$10.3B** of profit (vs. $3.9B in 1984).
- **[[1989]]** — Net worth **+$1.515B (+44.4%)**; book value $4,296.01 (23.8% over 25 yrs)
  — the biggest dollar gain yet, driven by a buoyant market lifting the portfolio
  (Coca-Cola, doubled to a $1.8B position; GEICO past $1B; Cap Cities/ABC $1.69B). Buffett
  flags this as a **"double-dip"** that won't repeat (a "single-dip" future). Look-through
  earnings ~$212M of retained investee earnings sit atop ~$300M reported. Four convertible
  preferreds now total **$1.558B** ([[Gillette]], [[USAir]], [[Champion International]],
  [[Salomon]]); Berkshire issued **$902.6M** of zero-coupon debentures. Insurance net
  worth ~**$6B** underwrites a $250M super-cat debut.
- **[[1990]]** — Net worth **+$362M (+7.3%)** — the weakest year of the batch, as the
  "permanent four" stalled (media down, Coca-Cola up). Book value $4,612.06 (23.2% over 26
  yrs); look-through earnings ~**$590M**. The non-insurance group earned **51% on average
  equity**. Two enduring frameworks debut: the **cost of float** (~1.6% in 1990; below the
  government's cost in 19 of 24 years) and **[[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]**. Lone new
  stock: **[[Wells Fargo]]** (~10%, $290M, bought in a bank panic); added **RJR Nabisco**
  bonds ($440M). [[Gillette]]'s preferred was called (→12M shares).
- **[[1991]]** — Net worth **+$2.1B (+39.6%)**; book value **$6,437** (23.7% over 27 yrs)
  — the biggest dollar gain yet, but Buffett immediately discounts it: **~$1.6B** came from
  a one-time **P/E re-rating** of [[Coca-Cola]] (mkt now $3.75B, larger than all of
  Berkshire's net worth three years earlier) and [[Gillette]] — a **"double-dip"** that
  yields to a "single-dip" future. *Operating* results were softer: look-through earnings
  **fell 14%** to ~$516M (media decline + the Gillette preferred conversion + a Wells Fargo
  break-even), and insurance swung to a **$119.6M underwriting loss** (cost of float 6.31%,
  still under the 7.40% long-bond yield; float up to ~$1.895B). The 15% intrinsic-value goal
  is stated explicitly (needs ~$22B over the decade). One acquisition — **[[H. H. Brown]]**
  (work shoes, ~$25M pre-tax, closed 7/1/91) — and one new stock, **[[Guinness]]**
  (Berkshire's first significant foreign holding). [[See's Candies]] hit a record 21.6%
  pre-tax margin. The year's confessed error: the **[[Fannie Mae]]** thumb-suck (~$1.4B
  forgone).
- **[[1992]]** — Net worth **+$1.52B (+20.3%)**; book value $7,745 (23.6% over 28 yrs).
  **Look-through earnings $604M**, and Buffett sets a public marker — they must reach
  **>$1.8B by 2000** to hold the 15% pace. **Hurricane Andrew** (largest insured loss in
  history) cost the super-cat book ~$125M (≈ a full year's premium), holding net super-cat to
  −$2M and denting GEICO's look-through. Cost of float improved to **4.76%** (float up to
  **$2,290M**; beat the government in 21 of 26 years). Buffett issues **two warnings**: the
  S&P's next decade will return far less than the last, and Berkshire's growing capital base
  will erode its relative edge ("slugging percentage, not batting average"). Portfolio
  doublings/additions: Freddie Mac (>doubled, 8.2%), Wells Fargo, Guinness, and new
  **[[General Dynamics]]** (an arbitrage-turned-investment). Lone operating acquisition:
  **[[Central States Indemnity]]** (82%). [[Scott Fetzer]] set a record ($110M pre-tax on
  $116M equity). Conceptually, the year's landmarks are the **value/growth synthesis** and
  the **stock-option-expensing** plea.
- **[[1993]]** — Net worth **+$1.5B (+14.3%)**; book value $8,854 (23.3% over 29 yrs) —
  "satisfactory but unexciting," though the **market price rose 39%** (Berkshire now richer
  relative to intrinsic value). Insurance had a banner year: an **underwriting profit of
  $31M** on **$2,624.7M** of average float — Berkshire was *paid* to hold its float — and the
  old industry combined-ratio table was **retired** (low rates having gutted the value of
  float). Look-through earnings jumped to **$856M** (2000 target now $1.85B after new shares).
  The year's deal was **[[Dexter Shoe]]** (paid in 25,203 Berkshire shares — later the famous
  mistake). Berkshire **sold one-third of [[Capital Cities]]** at $630 (cost $172.50; +$297M
  after tax), proving look-through earnings conservative. Conceptual landmarks: the **risk/beta
  essay**, the **three-mode corporate-governance** framework (Howard joins the board), and the
  **Li'l Abner** deferred-tax-compounding parable.
- **[[1994]]** — Net worth **+$1.45B (+13.9%)**; book value $10,083 (23% over 30 yrs) — and
  Buffett's blunt warning that "**a fat wallet is the enemy of superior investment results**"
  (net worth now $11.9B; minimum deployment $100M). A **second straight insurance underwriting
  profit** lifted float to **$3,056.6M at negative cost**. Look-through earnings hit
  **$1,030M** (ahead of the $1.85B-by-2000 pace, but flattered by super-cat). The year's two
  conceptual set-pieces are the **[[Scott Fetzer]] worked example** (book value crumbles to
  $148.2M while intrinsic value doubles — ROE that would top the Fortune 500) and the linked
  **[[Capital Allocation]]** and **[[Executive Compensation]]** essays. **[[American Express]]**
  built to ~10% ($1.36B cost), and **[[GEICO]] crossed 50%**. The confessed errors: the
  **[[USAir]]** gold medal (preferred written down to $89.5M; dividend suspended) and selling
  Cap Cities too early (−$222.5M). Decade frame: 1984→1994, securities $1.7B→$18B, non-security
  pre-tax earnings $6M→$384M.
- **[[1995]]** — Net worth **+$5.3B (+45.0%)**; per-share book value +43.1% to **$14,426**
  (23.6% over 31 yrs) — a "rising tide lifts all yachts" year Buffett refuses to celebrate. The
  substance: **three acquisitions roughly doubling revenues** — the **100% purchase of [[GEICO]]**
  ($2.3B for the remaining half, adding ~$3B of float), plus **[[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]]** and
  **[[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]** (the "ABWA" deals). A **third straight underwriting
  profit** pushed float to **$3,607.2M** at negative cost; the **float-as-equity** insight is
  formalized. The equity book swelled to **$22.0B market** (cost $5.75B) on a soaring market;
  Cap Cities was exchanged for **[[Disney]]** stock (early 1996). The capstone move is the
  **[[Class B Shares]]** recap (to thwart "clone" unit trusts), announced with the warning that
  at $36,000 the stock is one "Charlie and I would not consider buying." A ten-year table puts
  the long arc in view: per-share securities compounding 33.4%/yr and per-share operating
  earnings 14.7%/yr since 1965.
- **[[1996]]** — Net worth **+$6.2B (+36.1%)**; per-share book value **+31.8%** to **$19,011**
  (23.8% over 32 yrs) — the share count rising for the first time in years as Berkshire issued
  stock for **[[FlightSafety International]]** and sold **[[Class B Shares|Class B]]** stock. The
  defining numeric event is **insurance float vaulting to $6,702.0M** (from $3,607M) as the
  wholly-owned **[[GEICO]]** is consolidated — a fourth straight underwriting profit keeping it
  free. The **two-column intrinsic-value table** (Buffett's standing valuation aid) shows
  investments per share **$28,500 (+29.0%)** and pre-tax operating earnings per share **$421.39
  (+63.2%)**; **look-through earnings hit $1,522M**. Two acquisitions — the large, unusually
  **capital-intensive** FlightSafety (~$1.5B) and the small **[[Kansas Bankers Surety]]** ($75M,
  → [[Wesco Financial|Wesco]]). The portfolio (cost $7.9B / market **$27.75B**) is led by a
  post-split **[[Coca-Cola]] at $10.5B**, with new positions in **[[Disney]]** ($1.72B) and
  **[[McDonald's]]** ($1.37B). Conceptually the year belongs to **"[[The Inevitables]]"** (Coke
  and Gillette) and the published **Owner's Manual**. Berkshire paid **$860M** of federal income
  tax. Buffett's twin cautions endure: a larger capital base must "dampen returns," and results
  "will not be smooth."
- **[[1997]]** — Net worth **+$8.0B**; per-share book value **+34.1%** to **$25,488** (24.1% over 33
  yrs) — yet rated only "**Quack**," since in a roaring bull market the passive, tax-free S&P rose
  almost as fast (Berkshire paid/accrued **$4.2B** of tax, ~18% of beginning net worth). The
  yardstick Buffett sets is "a **modest advantage over the Index** over time." The two-column table
  reaches investments **$38,043/share** (+33.5%) and operating earnings **$717.82/share** (+70.3%) —
  the latter flattered by super-cat luck and an unsustainable GEICO margin. **Look-through earnings
  jumped to $1,930M**; insurance posted a **fifth straight underwriting profit** with float at
  **$7,093.1M** (the "accounting irony" — float as a liability worth more than equal net worth).
  Two acquisitions agreed (**[[Star Furniture]]**, **[[International Dairy Queen]]**), three
  **[[Unconventional Commitments]]** taken (silver, oil derivatives, $4.6B zero-coupon Treasuries),
  and the long [[Salomon]] saga closed via the [[Travelers Group]] merger. With prices high, Buffett
  preaches **Ted Williams** discipline ("wait for the fat pitch") and warns the **[[Margin of Safety]]** has "materially eroded" amid a "cheery consensus." [[Roberto Goizueta]] died in October.
- **[[1998]]** — Net worth **+$25.9B**; per-share book value **+48.3%** to **$37,801** (24.7% over
  34 yrs) — "**not as good as it looks**" (Wagner: "better than it sounds"), since **most of the gain
  came from issuing shares** in the **[[General Re]]** ($22B) and **[[Executive Jet]]** ($725M)
  acquisitions, which lift per-share *book* value (Berkshire trades above book) without a matching
  rise in *intrinsic* value. Total net worth reached **$57.4B**, the largest of any U.S. corporation.
  The defining numeric event is **insurance float roughly tripling to $22,762M** (Gen Re ⅔). The
  two-column table: investments **$47,647/share** (+25.2%), operating earnings per share **−33.9%**
  to $474.45 (Gen Re's underwriting loss). **GEICO "shot the lights out"** (in-force 3.56M; $103M
  profit-sharing), but several big public investees ([[Coca-Cola]], [[Disney]]) had "operating
  shortfalls" so the **equity portfolio trailed the S&P**. Buffett confessed **selling [[McDonald's]]
  "a very big mistake"** and devoted two essays to **accounting abuse** (option non-expensing;
  restructuring "big baths"; an endorsement of SEC chairman Arthur Levitt). The 15% intrinsic-value
  goal is now "the very peak of possible outcomes" — the hunt is for **elephants** ("popcorn stands
  just won't do").
- **[[1999]]** — **The worst year of Buffett's tenure.** Net worth grew just **$358M**; per-share
  book value **+0.5%** to **$37,987** (24.0% over 35 yrs) — worst absolute *and* relative
  performance ever, for which Buffett grades his **capital allocation** a "**D**." The cause was the
  **equity portfolio** (his domain): big investees ([[Coca-Cola]] $11.65B, [[Gillette]] $3.95B) slid
  on "disappointing operating results," even as **[[American Express]]** jumped to $8.4B. The
  insurance side took a **$1.4B underwriting loss** (cost of float **5.8%**) on an "aberrational"
  **[[General Re]]** result, lifting total float to **$25,298M**; the two-column table's operating
  column went *negative* (−$458.55/share). Two **cash** acquisitions advanced the base —
  **[[Jordan's Furniture]]** and the ~$2B **[[MidAmerican Energy]]** stake (Berkshire's **first
  electric utility**). Buffett restates **why Berkshire owns no tech** ([[Circle of Competence]]),
  warns equities are "wildly optimistic" (future returns far below expectations), lays out the
  definitive **[[Evolving Views — Buybacks|share-repurchase]]** doctrine (and admits erring by *not*
  buying when cheap), and weighs in on **purchase-vs-pooling** accounting. [[See's Candies]] set a
  record 24% margin; [[Lorimer Davidson]] died at 97; [[Dexter Shoe]] buckled under imports.
- **[[2000]]** — **A decent rebound.** Net worth grew **$3.96B**; per-share book value **+6.5%** to
  **$40,442** (23.6% over 36 yrs) — beating an S&P that fell **9.1%**. The headline is an
  **eight-acquisition spree** (~$8B, **97% cash**, shares +⅓ of 1%): Berkshire "embraced the 21st
  century by entering … brick, carpet, insulation and paint" — **[[CORT Business Services]],
  [[U.S. Liability]], [[Ben Bridge Jeweler]], [[Justin Industries]], [[Shaw Industries]], [[Benjamin Moore]], [[Johns Manville]]**, plus **[[MidAmerican Energy]]** closing. A drying-up junk-bond
  market made Berkshire (an all-equity buyer) far more competitive, and increasingly the **buyer of
  choice**. Float reached **$27,871M** but cost **6%** (a $1.6B underwriting loss, partly from
  [[Ajit Jain]]'s deliberately front-loaded **retroactive-reinsurance** deals — including a record
  **$2.4B** premium). **GEICO stumbled** (new policies fell amid a State Farm price war; the
  ad-ramp "I was wrong"). The conceptual centerpiece is the **Aesop "[[Bird in the Bush]]"** essay
  and its **dot-com-bubble** warning ("a pin lies in wait for every bubble"). Buffett delivered the
  explicit **[[Dexter Shoe]] mea culpa** (writing off its goodwill), sold **nearly all [[Freddie Mac]] and [[Fannie Mae]]**, and saw **[[Ralph Schey]] retire** into "Berkshire's Hall of Fame."
- **[[2001]]** — **The first down year of the modern era.** Net worth **fell $3.77B**; per-share book
  value **−6.2%** to **$37,920** (22.6% over 37 yrs) — yet still 5.7 points ahead of the S&P (−11.9%),
  vindicating Buffett's focus on *relative* results. The cause was **September 11**: the insurance
  industry's largest-ever loss, which exposed [[General Re]]'s undisciplined terrorism underwriting,
  drove a **−$4,318M reinsurance underwriting loss** and pushed the **cost of float to 12.8%** (worst
  since 1984) even as total float surged to **$35,508M**. Operating earnings to Berkshire went
  *negative* (**−$47M**); total earnings were **$795M**. Buffett's signature confessions: the **Noah
  rule** ("predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does"), the candid admission that his own
  **equity-portfolio results were "poor… for several years,"** and the restated three-part [[Dexter Shoe]] error. The acquisition machine ran on regardless (all cash): **[[MiTek]]**, **[[XTRA]]**,
  **[[Larson-Juhl]]**, and **[[Fruit of the Loom]]** (via its distressed debt), plus the
  **Berkadia/[[FINOVA Group]]** workout. Berkshire now needs **"elephants"** to move the needle.
- **[[2002]]** — **A banner rebound.** Net worth grew **$6.1B**; per-share book value **+10.0%** to
  **$41,727** (22.2% over 38 yrs), beating a −22.1% S&P by an "aberrational" **32.1 points** (helped
  by Berkshire's now-smaller equity weighting). Insurance was the engine: **float +$5.7B to $41.2B**
  at a **1% cost**, with [[Ajit Jain]] ($534M UW profit), [[GEICO]] ($416M UW profit, $6.9B
  premiums) and a repaired [[General Re]] all contributing — though Gen Re took a **further $1.31B**
  reserve charge as Buffett confessed "**I was dead wrong**" about its discipline. The year's
  conceptual landmarks are the **[[Derivatives]]** ("financial weapons of mass destruction") essay
  and a sweeping **[[Corporate Governance]]**/audit/[[Executive Compensation|options-expensing]]
  critique. Acquisitions: **[[CTB]]**, **[[Garan]]**, **[[The Pampered Chef]]**, plus MEHC's
  **Kern River** and **[[Northern Natural Gas]]** pipelines. Stocks stayed dull (cost $9.2B / mkt
  $28.4B), but **junk bonds/loans sextupled to $8.3B**.
- **[[2003]]** — **A 21% year.** Net worth grew **$13.6B**; per-share book value **+21%** to
  **$50,498** (22.2% over 39 yrs), intrinsic value somewhat more. Insurance was a "standout": **record
  float $44.2B at no cost** with a **$1.7B underwriting profit** across all segments (a now-fixed
  [[General Re]], [[Ajit Jain]]'s +$1,434M, [[GEICO]]'s +$452M). Berkshire paid **$3.3B of tax** (~2½%
  of all U.S. corporate income tax). Two big acquisitions — **[[Clayton Homes]]** and **[[McLane]]**
  ($23B of sales) — and a notable macro bet: **~$12B of foreign-currency contracts** against the
  dollar (see [[Trade Deficit and the Dollar]]; FX added $825M of gains). Stocks held flat (cost
  $8.5B / mkt $35.3B; new **[[PetroChina]]** and HCA), Buffett confessing he erred by **not selling
  into the Great Bubble**. The 22-year **[[Shareholder-Designated Contributions]]** program was
  terminated. Equity is now ~50% of net worth (vs ~114% in the 1980s), so market swings move
  Berkshire less.
- **[[2004]]** — **A lackluster, cash-heavy year.** Net worth grew **$8.3B**; per-share book value
  **+10.5%** to **$55,824** (21.9% over 40 yrs) — but **lagged the S&P (10.9%)**, Buffett's first
  underperformance in years, blamed on himself ("I struck out" on big deals; few cheap securities)
  rather than the operating CEOs. Year-end cash swelled to ~**$43B**. Insurance again starred — float
  **$46.1B**, a **$1.55B underwriting profit** despite **$1.25B** of Florida-hurricane losses — and
  Buffett used the year to teach *how* Berkshire beats commodity insurance: NICO's deliberate
  volume-shrinking [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|discipline]] ("Portrait of a Disciplined
  Underwriter") and GEICO's low-cost model. The **"Big Four"** (Amex, Coca-Cola, Gillette, Wells
  Fargo) threw off ~$1.2B of look-through earnings. The **dollar/FX bet grew to ~$21.4B**. A
  cautionary failure: MidAmerican's **−$579M zinc write-off** → the "mono-linked chains" lesson.
- **[[2005]]** — **A decent +6.4% in a disaster year.** Net worth grew **$5.6B**; per-share book value
  **+6.4%** to **$59,377** (21.5% over 41 yrs), beating the S&P (+4.9%). **Hurricane Katrina** and her
  "ugly sisters" cost Berkshire **$3.4B**, yet insurance float (**$49.3B**) stayed **costless** — a
  +$53M net underwriting profit — because **[[GEICO]]** (+$1,221M; productivity +32% in two years)
  carried the reinsurance losses. Buffett initiated **five acquisitions, none for stock**
  ([[Medical Protective]], [[Forest River]], [[Business Wire]], [[Applied Underwriters]],
  [[PacifiCorp]] via MidAmerican) and the **repeal of PUHCA** let Berkshire consolidate MidAmerican.
  Two new per-share scorecards (investments $74,129; non-insurance pre-tax earnings $2,441) capture
  the long shift from stocks to operating businesses. The **"widening the moat"** essay; the
  Gillette→[[Procter & Gamble]] merger; the [[General Re Securities]] exit losses mount to −$404M.
- **[[2006]]** — **A record $16.9B net-worth gain** (most ever by a U.S. company, ex-mergers);
  per-share book value **+18.4%** to **$70,281** (21.4% over 42 yrs). Insurance had its best year ever
  — "Mother Nature went on vacation," so a **$3,838M underwriting profit** with float at **$50.9B**
  (plus ~$7B from the **[[Equitas]]** retroactive deal). The year's landmark is **[[ISCAR]]** —
  Berkshire's **first non-U.S. acquisition** ($4B for 80% of the Israeli cutting-tools maker) — plus
  **[[TTI]]** and Fruit's **[[Russell Athletic|Russell]]**/VF tuck-ins. NetJets turned profitable;
  the [[General Re Securities]] saga ended ("All's well that ends"). Buffett laid out the
  **investment-side succession** search (Lou Simpson too old) and the deepening trade-deficit
  ("reverse compounding") warning, while quietly noting he writes profitable derivatives himself.
- **[[2007]]** — **+11% to $78,008** (net worth +$12.3B); the year the **subprime crisis** dawns ("you
  only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out"). Insurance had a second
  catastrophe-light year (**+$3,374M underwriting profit**, float $58.7B), and investments reached
  **$141B**. The marquee deal was the Christmas-Day **[[Marmon Group]]** (60% for **$4.5B**, the
  largest cash purchase yet), and Buffett quietly began the **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe]]**
  position (17.5%). He **sold [[PetroChina]]** for $4B (~$3.5B gain), laid out the **[[The Great the Good and the Gruesome|great/good/gruesome]]** framework, delivered the **definitive [[Dexter Shoe]]** mea culpa ($3.5B), and detailed the **equity-index put** and **CDS** derivative books that
  would roil reported earnings in 2008.
- **[[2008]]** — **The worst year in the table.** Net worth **fell $11.5B**; per-share book value
  **−9.6%** to **$70,530** (44 years, **20.3% compounded**) — yet still **27.4 points ahead** of an
  S&P that fell 37.0%, the year's saving grace. Written amid the financial crisis ("a freefall in
  business activity… at a pace that I have never before witnessed"), the letter pairs gloom with
  Buffett's enduring optimism ("**America's best days lie ahead**"). The insurance engine held: a
  **sixth straight underwriting profit** (+$2,792M) meant Berkshire was **paid $2.8B to hold its
  $58.5B of float**. With "**pessimism… your friend, euphoria the enemy**," Buffett deployed
  **$14.5B** into crisis-priced fixed-income-plus-equity deals (**[[Goldman Sachs]]**,
  **[[General Electric]]**, **[[Wrigley]]**/Mars), funding them by paring [[Johnson & Johnson]],
  [[Procter & Gamble]] and [[ConocoPhillips]] rather than touch the cash cushion. The confessed
  **mistake of commission** — enlarging [[ConocoPhillips]] near peak energy prices — cost "several
  billion." Reported results swung on a **−$5.1B mark** on the equity-index puts (Black-Scholes
  overstating very-long-dated options) and the [[Constellation Energy]] windfall (+$1,092M).
  Per-share investments fell to **$77,793** purely on prices, not sales.
- **[[2009]]** — **A 19.8% snap-back.** Net worth grew **$21.8B**; per-share book value **+19.8%** to
  **$84,487** (45 years, **20.3% compounded** on book — or **22%** on market price, an 801,516% total
  gain). The defining event is **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe]]** (~$26B for the 77.5% not already
  owned), funded ~70% cash / 30% Berkshire stock and inaugurating a deliberately **capital-intensive**
  "regulated utility & railroad" sector that will absorb "tens of billions" of equity. Insurance
  posted a **seventh straight underwriting profit** (+$1,559M) on **$62B of float**; the equity
  portfolio rebounded hard (the **[[BYD]]** stake alone went $232M → ~$2B) and the five **non-traded
  crisis securities** (Dow, GE, Goldman, Swiss Re, Wrigley) were now carried at $26.0B (cost $21.1B),
  yielding $2.1B/yr. Buffett's regret was buying *too few* cheap bonds in 2008 ("when it's raining
  gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble") and his confessions were the **GEICO credit-card fiasco**
  and letting **NetJets**' debt balloon (turned around by [[David Sokol|Sokol]]; a $711M loss → profit).
- **[[2010]]** — **The BNSF year (+13%).** Per-share book value rose 13% to **$95,453** (46 years,
  **20.2%**), with the **February close of [[Burlington Northern Santa Fe]]** lifting Berkshire's
  "**normal" earning power ~40% pre-tax** to an estimated ~$17B pre-tax / $12B after-tax. Buffett
  re-anchors valuation on **three pillars** — investments ($158B; per-share growth slowed to 6.6%/yr
  in the 2000s as cash went to whole businesses), non-insurance earnings ($5,926/share pre-tax, 21%/yr
  over 40 years), and the subjective quality of future **retained-earnings deployment**. Insurance
  notched an **eighth straight underwriting profit** (float $65,832M); **NetJets** swung +$918M to
  profit under Sokol; **Marmon** headed to 80%. The **[[GEICO]] case study** quantified the book-vs-
  intrinsic gap ($1.4B carried goodwill vs ~$14B economic value). **[[Lou Simpson]]** retired and
  **[[Todd Combs]]** was hired; the crisis preferreds (Swiss Re, Goldman, GE) began rolling off.
- **[[2011]]** — **A modest +4.6%** to **$99,860** (47 years, **19.8%**). The story is the rise of the
  **"fabulous five"** non-insurance earners ([[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]], [[ISCAR]],
  **[[Lubrizol]]** (bought for ~$9B in September), [[Marmon Group]], [[MidAmerican Energy]]) — each a
  record, >$9B pre-tax combined, versus $393M from the only one owned seven years earlier. Two big
  marketable bets broke old habits: **[[IBM]]** ($10.9B — Buffett's first major tech stock) and a
  **[[Bank of America]]** preferred-plus-warrants. Insurance notched a **ninth straight underwriting
  profit** (a slim $248M in a catastrophe-heavy year; record float $70.6B). The crisis preferreds
  (Swiss Re/Goldman/GE) were called for $12.8B; the **[[Energy Future Holdings]]** bonds were owned as
  "a major unforced error." Buffett laid out the mature **[[Evolving Views — Buybacks|buyback]]**
  doctrine (≤110% of book; $20B cash floor) and the net-buyer logic, and **[[Ted Weschler]]** joined
  [[Todd Combs]] in the investment-management succession.
- **[[2012]]** — **A "subpar" +14.4%** to **$114,214** (48 years, **19.7%**) — subpar only because a
  **$24.1B** gain still trailed a +16% S&P (the ninth such miss in 48 years; "we do better when the
  wind is in our face"). Berkshire bought back **$1.3B** of its own stock and announced the **$12B
  [[H.J. Heinz]]** partnership with **[[Jorge Paulo Lemann|3G]]** (closing 2013). The "**powerhouse
  five**" cleared $10.1B pre-tax; insurance hit a **tenth straight underwriting profit** on $73B of
  float (GEICO share 9.7%, despite Hurricane Sandy). **[[Todd Combs]] and [[Ted Weschler]]** each beat
  the S&P by double digits (funds raised to ~$5B each). The year's set-piece essays were on
  **[[Evolving Views — Dividends|dividends]]** (why Berkshire pays none — the "sell-off" math) and
  **[[Evolving Views — Newspapers|newspapers]]** (28 dailies bought despite secular decline; local
  news + paywalls). Capex and employment both set records.
- **[[2013]]** — **+18.2%** to **$134,973** (49 years, **19.7%**) — a big year that still trailed a
  roaring +32.4% S&P (the 10th shortfall in 49 years). Net worth grew **$34.2B** on ~**$18B of
  acquisitions** — all of **[[NV Energy]]** and a major stake in **[[H.J. Heinz]]** (the 3G
  "partnership template") — plus **$3.5B** to take **[[Marmon Group]]** and **[[ISCAR]]** to 100%. The
  "Powerhouse Five" hit a record $10.8B pre-tax; insurance logged an **eleventh straight underwriting
  profit** (float $77.2B; **[[GEICO]] now #2**) and launched **[[Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance|BHSI]]**. The reflective set-piece was the **"two small investments"** essay (a farm and an
  NYU retail parcel) distilling Buffett's investing fundamentals: stay in your circle, judge an asset
  by its productivity not its quote, "a climate of fear is your friend." **[[Todd Combs]] and [[Ted Weschler]]** each surpassed $7B and beat both the S&P and Buffett.
- **[[2014]]** — **The Golden Anniversary (+8.3%)** to **$146,186** (50 years, **19.4%** book / a
  **1,826,163% market-price gain** Buffett equates with intrinsic value). The "Powerhouse Five" hit a
  record **$12.4B** pre-tax; insurance logged a **12th straight underwriting profit** (float **$84B**;
  [[National Indemnity Company|NICO]]'s net worth reached **$111B**, the world's largest insurer).
  Berkshire became "car guys" (**[[Van Tuyl Automotive]]**) and contracted 31 bolt-ons for $7.8B
  (incl. **[[Duracell]]**); the one blemish was **BNSF's service failures** (answered with $6B of 2015
  capex). The year's substance, though, is the pair of **anniversary essays**: Buffett's candid
  50-year history and **conglomerate defense**, and Munger's **"[[The Berkshire System]]"** — both
  arguing Berkshire is "ideally positioned for life after Charlie and I leave the scene," with
  **[[Greg Abel]]** and **[[Ajit Jain]]** "world-leading."
- **[[2015]]** — **+6.4%** to **$155,501** (51 years, **19.2%**), beating a flat S&P. The headline deal
  is **[[Precision Castparts]]** (~$32B cash, Berkshire's largest, sourced by [[Todd Combs]]) — soon
  the "Powerhouse Six." **BNSF** rebounded to a record **$6.8B** pre-tax after a $5.8B capex blitz; the
  Powerhouse Five cleared $13.1B. Insurance logged a **13th straight underwriting profit** (float
  **$88B**; [[National Indemnity Company|NICO]] the world's largest P/C insurer). The Heinz partnership
  **merged with Kraft → [[Kraft Heinz]]** (Berkshire ~27%). Buffett **changed the intrinsic-value
  methodology** to include insurance underwriting income (now-stable), and devoted a long essay to
  American optimism ("the babies being born… are the luckiest crop in history"). Combs and Weschler now
  manage ~$9B each.
- **[[2016]]** — **+10.7%** to **$172,108** (net worth **+$27.5B**; 52 years, **19% compounded**); the
  52-year **market-price gain (1,972,595%)** has now outpaced the book gain (884,319%) as price tracks
  the widening intrinsic-vs-book gap. After-tax earnings split **$17.57B operations / $6.50B capital
  gains**. Insurance logged a **14th straight underwriting profit** ($2,131M; 14-yr total **$28B**),
  float **$91,577M** (soon >$100B via the **[[AIG retroactive reinsurance|AIG]]** deal); **[[Tad Montross]]** retired at [[General Re]] ([[Kara Raiguel]] succeeding). **BNSF** had an off year while
  **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]** (90% owned) grew; together the regulated sector = **33% of after-tax
  operating earnings**. The portfolio (mkt **$122B**) gained two landmarks — **[[Apple]]** (Berkshire's
  first big tech-hardware stake) and a basket of **[[Evolving Views — Airlines|four airlines]]** — a
  double reversal of old aversions. The letter's set pieces: the **share-repurchase essay** ("what is
  smart at one price is stupid at another"), three **accounting** essays (amortization, depreciation,
  "adjusted earnings"), and **"[[Index Funds and Fees|The Bet]]"** — at nine years the index fund was up
  **85.4%** vs. the hedge funds-of-funds' **2.2%** ("Fees never sleep"; the **[[Jack Bogle]]** tribute).
- **[[2017]]** — **+23.0%** to **$211,750** (53 years, **19.1%**) — but Buffett deflates it at once: of the
  **$65.3B** gain, only **$36B** came from operations; **$29B** was "delivered… when Congress rewrote the
  U.S. Tax Code" (the **Tax Cuts and Jobs Act** deferred-tax revaluation). He warns that a **new GAAP
  rule** (2018) running unrealized stock gains through net income will make the GAAP bottom line "useless"
  (see [[Operating Earnings vs GAAP Net Income]]). Insurance broke its streak: a **−$3.2B underwriting
  loss** (Harvey/Irma/Maria), the **first in 15 years**, though float reached **$114.5B** on the record
  **[[AIG retroactive reinsurance|AIG]]** policy. With prices "at an all-time high" ("Don't ask the barber
  whether you need a haircut"), the only stand-alone deal was a 38.6% stake in **[[Pilot Flying J]]**;
  cash piled to **$116B**. **[[Apple]]** was built ~4× into a top-three holding and the **[[Bank of America]]** warrant was exercised. **"[[Index Funds and Fees|The Bet]]"** finished — index **+125.8%**
  vs. the funds-of-funds — with a coda that bonds can be riskier than stocks ([[Risk and Volatility]]).
  And, "saved… for last," **[[Ajit Jain]] and [[Greg Abel]]** were named **Vice Chairmen** — the
  succession made operational ([[The Berkshire System]]).
- **[[2018]]** — **The book-value era ends.** After ~30 years, Buffett **drops per-share book value** as
  the headline metric (Berkshire is now operating-business-dominated; buybacks above book and mark-to-
  market noise have made it misleading) and turns to **market price** and the **[[The Five Groves|five groves]]** appraisal. GAAP net income was a near-meaningless **$4.0B** (whipsawed by the new
  mark-to-market rule — Q4 alone showed a $25.4B GAAP loss), while **operating earnings hit a record
  $24.8B**. The corporate **tax cut to 21%** ("40% of the Treasury's AA shares returned… free of charge")
  lifted after-tax results and intrinsic value. **[[Apple]]** became the **largest holding** ($40.3B);
  **[[Bank of America]]** was built up; **[[JPMorgan Chase]]** added. **[[Tony Nicely]]** retired from
  GEICO. The capstone was the **[[The American Tailwind|"American Tailwind"]]** essay — $114.75 in 1942
  → $606,811 in an index fund. Buffett bought **~$43B** of stocks (cheaper than whole companies) while
  still hoping for an "elephant."
- **[[2019]]** — **Market +11.0%** (lagging the S&P's +31.5%); GAAP net income a headline **$81.4B**
  (mostly the $53.7B mark-to-market swing — "a crazy 1,900% increase" over 2018's $4B), operating earnings
  little changed at **$24B**. The cornerstone essay, **"[[The Power of Retained Earnings]]"** (Edgar
  Lawrence Smith / Keynes), shows the 10 largest holdings' **$8.3B of retained earnings** dwarfing their
  **$3.8B of dividends**. **[[Apple]]** ballooned to **$73.7B** (~30% of the $248B portfolio); a new
  **$10B [[Occidental Petroleum]]** financing and a **[[Visa]]** stake were added. **[[Kraft Heinz]]**
  slid *below cost*. Insurance earned +$400M (float **$129B**); **[[Guard Insurance|GUARD]]** and **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]'s 20th anniversary** were highlighted. Buffett bought back **$5B** of Berkshire and, in
  "The Road Ahead," declared the company "**100% prepared for our departure**," disclosing his **will**
  (Berkshire shares held, then distributed to foundations over 12–15 years). The **directors essay**
  skewered "independence" theater ([[Corporate Governance]]).
- **[[2020]]** — **The COVID year: +2.4%** (S&P +18.4%); operating earnings fell **9% to $21.9B**, and an
  **$11B write-down of [[Precision Castparts]]** ("I paid too much") dragged a GAAP figure of $42.5B. With
  no acquisitions, the headline capital action was a **record $24.7B of buybacks (5.2% of shares)**.
  Buffett crystallized Berkshire as a **"Big Four"** — insurance, **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]]**,
  **[[Apple]]**, **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]** — and noted Berkshire owns the **most U.S. property, plant
  and equipment of any company ($154B)**. The portfolio reshuffled hard: **all four airlines sold** at a
  loss in the travel collapse ([[Evolving Views — Airlines]]); most bank stakes (WFC, JPMorgan, Goldman)
  exited while **[[Bank of America]]** was *added*; new energy ([[Chevron]], plus the marked-down
  [[Occidental Petroleum]]), telecom ([[Verizon Communications|Verizon]]), pharma, and the **[[Japanese Trading Houses]]** (~5% each, yen-funded). The "[[The American Tailwind|Never bet against America]]"
  essay celebrated the founders of See's, GEICO, NICO, NFM, Clayton and Pilot. **[[Carl Ice]]** handed
  BNSF to **[[Katie Farmer]]**; the 2021 meeting would reunite Buffett, Munger, [[Greg Abel|Abel]] and
  [[Ajit Jain|Jain]] in Los Angeles.
- **[[2021]]** — **Market +29.6%** (vs S&P +28.7%); the letter foregoes an earnings recap and is built
  around the **"Four Giants"** — insurance (float **$147B**), **[[Apple]]** ($161B; $5.6B share of
  earnings), **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]]** (record **$6B**), and **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]**
  (record **$4B**). With acquisitions and stocks both dear, **repurchases were the main event**: 9% of
  shares retired over 2020–21 for **$51.7B**. Cash sat at **$144B** ($120B in T-bills). Berkshire owns the
  most U.S. PP&E of any company (**$158B**) and pays ~$9M/day in federal tax. Tributes to **[[Paul Andrews Jr|Paul Andrews]]** (TTI) and the partnership ethos ("business-pickers, not stock-pickers").
- **[[2022]]** — **Market +4.0%** (vs S&P −18.1% — a rare outperformance in a down market); **record
  operating earnings of $30.8B**, even as GAAP swung to a net *loss* on mark-to-market. Berkshire bought
  **[[Alleghany Corporation]]** (~$11.6B; [[Joe Brandon]]), lifting float to **$164B**, and grew enormous
  stakes in **[[Chevron]]** and **[[Occidental Petroleum]]** (now equity-method). The signature essay,
  **"The Secret Sauce,"** showed [[Coca-Cola]] and [[American Express]] — each ~$1.3B, mid-1990s — now worth
  $22–25B apiece ("the weeds wither away… as the flowers bloom"). Buffett defended buybacks ("an economic
  illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue") and tallied **$32B of federal tax** over the decade. A page
  of **[[Charlie Munger]]** aphorisms ("Nothing Beats Having a Great Partner") closed what proved his last
  letter with Charlie alive.
- **[[2023]]** — **Market +15.8%** (S&P +26.3%); **record operating earnings $37.35B**. The letter opens
  with the eulogy for **[[Charlie Munger]]** (died Nov 28, 2023 — "the architect of the present
  Berkshire"). Insurance "carried the day" (UW profit $5,428M; investment income $9,567M on the swelling
  T-bill pile), offsetting disappointments at **[[Burlington Northern Santa Fe|BNSF]]** (margins slipped;
  $70B book vs ~$500B replacement) and **[[MidAmerican Energy|BHE]]** (wildfire/regulatory — "a costly
  mistake"). **GAAP net worth $561B** is now ~6% of the entire S&P 500's, so "**no possibility of
  eye-popping performance**." **[[Occidental Petroleum]]** reached 27.8% and the **[[Japanese Trading Houses]]** ~9% each; Coke and Amex sat untouched. The letter is framed for sister **[[Bertie Buffett| Bertie]]**, and names **[[Greg Abel]]** "ready to be CEO of Berkshire tomorrow."
- **[[2024]]** — **Market +25.5%** (S&P +25.0%); **record operating earnings $47.4B** even as 53% of the
  189 businesses earned less — lifted by **insurance** (GEICO, reshaped by [[Todd Combs]]) and surging
  T-bill **investment income**. The headline record was a **$26.8B federal tax bill** — the largest ever
  paid by any U.S. company (cumulative >$101B since 1965). Berkshire **sold heavily** (marketable equities
  **$354B → $272B**, chiefly **[[Apple]]** and **[[Bank of America]]**), piling up record T-bills while
  insisting "the majority of your money remains in equities." **BHE** was taken to **100%**; the **[[Japanese Trading Houses]]** ceiling was relaxed. Buffett opened with a candor manifesto ("Mistakes — Yes, We Make
  Them") and named the succession: "**it won't be long before [[Greg Abel]] replaces me as CEO and will be
  writing the annual letters**." A tribute to **[[Pete Liegl]]** ([[Forest River]]) and a coda with sister
  **[[Bertie Buffett|Bertie]]** close Buffett's final letter as CEO-author. *(Abel became CEO in early
  2025.)*
