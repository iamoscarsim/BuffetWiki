# Evolving Views — Airlines

For most of his career Buffett treated airlines as the **archetypal "gruesome" business** — capital-
hungry, commodity-priced, labor-burdened, and chronically unprofitable in aggregate. The [[2016]] purchase
of stakes in **all four major U.S. carriers** therefore stands out as one of the sharpest apparent
reversals in the letters.

**The aversion (1989–2007).** The wound was self-inflicted: the [[1989]] **[[USAir]]** convertible
preferred became Buffett's "gold medal" mistake, written down to a fraction of cost ([[1994]]) with its
dividend suspended, "saved" only by a late-1990s industry fluke ([[1997]]). He generalized the lesson
mercilessly. Airlines became his standing example of a business with no [[Economic Moat|moat]], devouring
capital while delivering nothing to owners — the "gruesome" pole of the [[2007]] **"[[The Great the Good and the Gruesome|Great, Good and Gruesome]]"** taxonomy. He famously quipped that a far-sighted capitalist
at Kitty Hawk would have done investors a favor by shooting down the Wright brothers, and that the
industry's cumulative profits since inception were roughly zero.

**The reversal (2016).** Berkshire disclosed sizeable positions in **[[Delta Air Lines]]** (7.5%),
**[[Southwest Airlines]]** (7.0%), **[[United Continental Holdings|United Continental]]** (8.4%), and
**[[American Airlines]]** — "large investments in America's four major carriers." The reasoning (developed
more in shareholder-meeting commentary than in the letter itself): after waves of bankruptcy and
consolidation, the U.S. industry had collapsed to **four carriers controlling ~80% of domestic capacity**,
with high load factors, capacity discipline, and pricing power that resembled — for the first time — an
**oligopoly** rather than a commodity free-for-all. A basket bet across all four, rather than a single
pick, reflected uncertainty about which would win. The positions were partly the work of **[[Todd Combs]]**
and **[[Ted Weschler]]**, then embraced and enlarged by Buffett.

**The exit (2020).** The chapter closed abruptly. As COVID-19 grounded air travel in the spring of 2020,
Berkshire **sold its entire stakes in all four carriers** at a loss — a decision Buffett announced at the
May 2020 virtual annual meeting ("the world has changed for the airlines… I don't know that three, four
years from now people will fly as many passenger miles"). By the [[2020]] letter, the airlines had simply
**vanished from the portfolio table**. Buffett took the blame, noting he "made a mistake" in the airline
investment and that the four carriers — each now burdened with pandemic debt — were a different proposition
than the oligopoly he had bought into. The episode rhymes with [[USAir]]: a second time, airline economics
bit a Buffett position — vindicating, in a backhanded way, his decades-long aversion.

The trajectory is a study in [[Good Business at a Fair Price|the difference between an industry's history and its future]]: the same economics Buffett condemned could, after structural change, become *temporarily*
tolerable — but a single exogenous shock (a grounded-travel pandemic) exposed how little margin of safety
a capital-intensive, high-fixed-cost, debt-laden commodity business carries.

## Evolution
- [[1989]]–[[1997]] The **[[USAir]]** preferred: bought 1989, written down hard ([[1994]]), the "unforced
  error" Buffett flogs as proof of airline economics.
- [[2007]] Airlines named the "gruesome" archetype in the **[[The Great the Good and the Gruesome]]** essay.
- [[2016]] **The reversal:** stakes in all four major U.S. carriers (Delta, Southwest, United Continental,
  American). Bet on a consolidated, disciplined oligopoly rather than the old commodity industry.
- [[2020]] **The exit:** all four stakes **sold at a loss** in the COVID-19 travel collapse (announced May
  2020; gone from the year-end table). "The world has changed for the airlines." Airline economics bit a
  Buffett position a second time — a backhanded vindication of the old aversion.
