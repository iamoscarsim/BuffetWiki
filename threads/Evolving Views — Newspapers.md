# Evolving Views — Newspapers

Buffett's view of the newspaper business travels a long arc — from "among the very
best [economics] in the business world" in the 1980s to a candidly declining
industry in the 2000s–2010s. The [[Buffalo Evening News]] and [[Washington Post]]
are the running case studies; the [[Capital Cities]]/ABC stake is the broadcast
cousin.

## Timeline

- **[[1984]]** — Peak optimism. "The economics of a dominant newspaper are
  excellent, among the very best in the business world." A dominant paper has
  "bulletin board" value to readers and near-monopoly pull for advertisers — so much
  so that "while first-class newspapers make excellent profits, the profits of
  third-rate papers are as good or better — as long as either... is dominant within
  its community." Once dominant, "the newspaper itself, not the marketplace,
  determines just how good or how bad the paper will be." Buffett stresses the News's
  exceptional penetration and 50%+ "news hole" as a chosen quality standard, not a
  market requirement.

- **[[1990]]** — **The turn begins.** Buffett concedes he was "surprised" by the media
  industry's "far more vulnerability to the early stages of a recession than has been the
  case in the past," and — crucially — judges it **not merely cyclical**: media will
  "prove considerably less marvelous than I, the industry, or lenders thought… only a few
  years ago." The cause is structural, not physical: slower ad-dollar growth, retailers
  shifting to direct mail, and "the number of both print and electronic advertising
  channels has substantially increased," dispersing ad dollars and eroding the "**pricing
  power**" that made the franchise. He states plainly that this "**materially reduce[s]
  the intrinsic value**" of the media investees ([[Capital Cities]], [[Washington Post]])
  *and* the operating [[Buffalo Evening News]] — "though all remain fine businesses." The
  News held up (−5%; news hole 52.3%, the rise coming from *fewer ad pages*), but earnings
  "will fall substantially" in 1991. This is the first crack in the 1984 thesis.

- **[[1991]]** — **The thesis is formalized.** Buffett declares that newspaper, TV, and
  magazine properties "have begun to resemble *businesses* more than *franchises*" (see
  [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]). The cause is the secular one he flagged in 1990 —
  proliferating entertainment and advertising choices competing for a fixed "500 million
  American eyeballs and a 24-hour day" — and he supplies the **valuation math** that makes
  it bite. A property earning $1M, *if* expected to grow 6% forever without added capital
  and discounted at 10%, is worth **$25M** (≈16× pre-tax). Shift the expectation to a
  cyclical "**bob-around**" with no growth, and the same $1M is worth only **$10M** (≈6½×
  pre-tax). "Dollars are dollars whether they are derived from… media properties or… steel
  mills"; what once justified a media premium — guaranteed capital-light growth — is gone,
  so "valuations must change dramatically." Berkshire's media intrinsic value "has declined
  materially," though cushioned because [[Buffalo Evening News]], [[Capital Cities]], and
  [[Washington Post]] are exceptionally well-run, nearly **debt-free** (unlike rivals
  carrying 5×+ income in debt), and stayed out of the late-1980s bidding frenzy. Buffett
  holds anyway under the no-sell rule for permanent holdings. "Gone are the days of
  bullet-proof franchises and cornucopian economics."

- **[[1995]]** — **"Another notch" down.** Reviewing the [[Buffalo Evening News]] among units with
  declining earnings, Buffett extends the 1991 verdict: "Today, the industry retains its excellent
  economics, but has lost still **another notch**. Over time, we expect the competitive strength of
  newspapers to gradually erode, though the industry should nevertheless remain a fine business for
  many years to come." A measured, ongoing markdown — not yet the sharper internet-era pessimism of
  later letters.

- **[[2006]]** — **The full internet-era reckoning.** Buffett delivers his most extended newspaper
  post-mortem. The old economics were "**Survival of the Fattest**": in a one-paper town, "**no
  paper… however bad the product or however inept the management, could avoid gushing profits**"
  ("I owe my fortune to two great American institutions: monopoly and nepotism"). But "if cable and
  satellite broadcasting, as well as the **internet**, had come along first, newspapers as we know
  them probably would never have existed." He recalls his 1991 warning (then resented by publishers)
  and notes "**eroding fundamentals will [eventually] overwhelm managerial brilliance**" — "if you
  want a reputation as a good businessman, be sure to get into a good business." The **[[Buffalo Evening News|Buffalo News]]** (Stan Lipsey; editor Margaret Sullivan) has the highest market
  penetration of any large U.S. paper and a leading local website, yet "**the days of lush profits
  from our newspaper are over**." Berkshire will hold it anyway (economic principle 11 — "Charlie and
  I love newspapers… a free and energetic press is a key ingredient… of a great democracy"), but
  expects only profit-margin slides and foresees "**non-economic individual buyers**" (vanity/civic)
  emerging as the print franchise decays.

- **[[2012]]** — **"We Buy Some Newspapers… Newspapers?"** The surprising twist: even while reaffirming
  the secular decline, Berkshire **bought 28 dailies for $344M** over fifteen months (and would buy
  more "at very low multiples"). The reconciliation is a refined thesis: news is "**what people don't
  know that they want to know**," and papers have lost primacy in national news, sports scores, stock
  quotes and classifieds (help-wanted ad revenue −90% in 12 years) — but **still reign in *local*
  news** to "**tightly-bound communities**" ("a story about the reader himself or his neighbors will
  be read to the end"). Two survival conditions: a **paywall** (the self-inflicted error was giving the
  paper away free online while charging for print — the Arkansas *Democrat-Gazette* under Walter
  Hussman and the *WSJ* are the models), and a refusal to **cut news content or frequency** ("skimpy
  news coverage will… lead to skimpy readership"). Managed under Terry Kroeger's Omaha World-Herald
  group; one unprofitable Media-General daily was shut (Rule 11). The same letter marks **[[Stan Lipsey]]'s retirement** from the [[Buffalo Evening News|Buffalo News]] — "the News might now be
  extinct were it not for Stan." Cash earnings "will almost certainly trend downward," but at the
  prices paid the papers clear Berkshire's economic test.
