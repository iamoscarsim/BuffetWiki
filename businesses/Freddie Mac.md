# Freddie Mac

The Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation ("Freddie Mac") was a major 1988 Berkshire
investment — a preferred stock that Buffett notes is "financially equivalent to a
common stock." It is unusual among the holdings for being owned not by an insurance
subsidiary but by **[[Mutual Savings and Loan]]**, Berkshire's California thrift, which
is why it is carried at cost rather than market on the consolidated balance sheet.

In the [[1988]] letter Freddie Mac appears in the >$100M holdings table — **2,400,000
shares, cost $71.7M, market $121.2M** — one of the year's "two major purchases" (with
[[Coca-Cola]]), bought as a long-term holding at "the maximum allowed by law" (a
savings institution could then own only a limited stake). [[Charlie Munger]], who
oversaw the thrift, described the position at length in his Wesco letter, set against
his analysis of the unfolding **savings-and-loan crisis**. Berkshire held Freddie Mac
profitably for years before exiting around 2000.

## Evolution
- [1988] Bought as a "major purchase": 2,400,000 shares (cost $71.7M / market $121.2M),
  the legal maximum, held via [[Mutual Savings and Loan]] and carried at cost. A
  preferred that is "financially equivalent to a common stock."
- [1991] Holding edged up to **2,495,200 shares** (3.4%, net of Wesco minority; cost $77.2M /
  market **$343.1M**) — one of the few positions Berkshire added to in an otherwise stay-put
  year. Look-through share $15M.
- [1992] **More than doubled** (alongside a stock split) to **16,196,700 shares** — ownership
  rising from 3.4% to **8.2%** (cost $414.3M / market **$783.5M**) — one of only four holdings
  changed during the year. Look-through share jumped to $29M. "We like to buy."
- [1993] Holding shown at **13,654,600 shares** (~6.8%; cost $307.5M / market **$681.0M**); the
  look-through share rose to **$41M**. (Share/cost figures reflect Wesco-minority accounting;
  the position remains a core thrift-held holding.)
- [1994] 12,761,200 shares (~6.3%; cost $270.5M / market **$644.4M**); look-through share $47M.
  Held as a core holding (Buffett notes its value, with See's and Wells Fargo, could drop in a
  worst-case California earthquake scenario alongside super-cat losses).
- [1995] 12,502,500 shares; market **$1,044M** (cost $260.1M) — crossing $1B as financials rallied.
  Held among the untouched top positions.
- [1996] **64,246,000 shares** (8.4%; cost $333.4M / market **$1,772.8M**) — a stock split lifts
  the share count; look-through share **$77M**. (Berkshire would exit Freddie Mac around 2000.)
- [1997] 63,977,600 shares (8.6%; cost $329.4M / market **$2,683.1M**); look-through share
  **$86M**. Held essentially unchanged among the financial holdings.
- [1998] 60,298,000 shares; market **$3,885M** (cost $308M) — a big gain as financials rallied;
  held among the itemized over-$750M positions. (Berkshire would exit around 2000.)
- [1999] 59,559,300 shares (8.6%); market **$2,803M** (cost $294M); look-through share **$127M**.
  Held among the itemized over-$750M positions in Buffett's worst year.
- [2000] **Nearly all sold.** Berkshire "sold nearly all of our Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae shares"
  in 2000, cutting the Freddie stake to just **0.3%** (still a $106M look-through contribution on
  the year's average ownership). The exit — reportedly on growing unease about the two GSEs' risk
  and accounting — preceded their eventual 2008 collapse by eight years. (See [[Fannie Mae]].)
