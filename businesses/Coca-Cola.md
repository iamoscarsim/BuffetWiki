# Coca-Cola

The Coca-Cola Company is the global soft-drink franchise that became Berkshire's most
celebrated common-stock investment — the purest embodiment of the [[Economic Moat]],
brand-driven [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics|franchise economics]], and
[[Permanent Ownership]]. Berkshire began buying in 1988 and would soon make it the
largest holding in the portfolio.

Coca-Cola first appears in the [[1988]] letter's holdings table: **14,172,500 shares,
cost $592.5M, market $632.4M** — described, with Freddie Mac, as one of the year's "two
major purchases," to be held "for a long time." (Berkshire continued buying into 1989,
ultimately ~7% of the company for ~$1.0B; the famous full essay on *why* Coca-Cola is a
wonderful business comes in the [[1989]] letter, with Buffett's confession that he had
been drinking the company's product for decades before buying the stock — and that he
"missed" the obvious for years.) The investment fit Buffett's evolved doctrine exactly:
a globally dominant consumer brand with enormous, durable pricing power and a tiny need
for incremental capital — "our favorite holding period is forever," and a flagship
example of [[Concentration]] ("too much of a good thing can be wonderful").

## Evolution
- [1988] First disclosed: 14,172,500 shares (cost $592.5M / market $632.4M), one of two
  "major purchases" (with [[Freddie Mac]]) of the year, intended as a very long-term
  holding. Fuller rationale promised — and delivered — in [[1989]].
- [1989] **The famous essay**, and a near-doubling of the stake to **23,350,000 shares**
  (cost $1,023.9M, market $1,803.8M) — Berkshire's largest commitment to date. Buffett
  mocks "the incredible speed with which your Chairman responds": he sold Cokes as a boy
  in 1936 and observed the franchise for 52 years while buying "street railway companies,
  windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers" instead — "only in the summer of 1988 did
  my brain finally establish contact with my eyes." He credits CEO [[Roberto Goizueta]]
  (from 1981) and President [[Don Keough]] with a "perfect" mesh of marketing and finance
  driving exploding overseas sales — "a shareholder's dream." The archetypal wonderful
  business held "forever"; he warns only that the price is now "vulnerable to a general
  market decline."
- [1990] The position stands at **46,700,000 shares** (the doubling from 23.35M is Coke's
  2:1 stock split, not new buying — "we neither bought nor sold a share"), market **$2.17B**
  on the $1.024B cost — and it *rose* even as the media holdings fell. Buffett calls Coke
  "the most valuable franchise in the world" and highlights its **buybacks**: when Coke
  repurchases stock it lifts Berkshire's ownership of that franchise more tax-efficiently
  than paying a dividend would — the [[Look-Through Earnings]] logic in action.
- [1991] Position unchanged at **46,700,000 shares**, but market value surged to **$3.75B**
  (cost $1.024B) — "now our Coke stock alone is worth more than" Berkshire's *entire* net
  worth ($3.4B) when the stake was bought three years earlier. Together with [[Gillette]],
  Coke's **P/E re-rating** drove ~$1.6B of Berkshire's $2.1B net-worth gain. Buffett labels
  this the **"double-dip"** — earnings growth *plus* a market reappraisal he deems warranted
  but non-repeatable: "We'll have to settle for a single dip in the future." He also notes he
  avoided, with Coke, the stop-buying-on-a-rising-price error he made with [[Fannie Mae]].
  Coke is invoked as the U.S.-based global-franchise twin of new holding [[Guinness]].
- [1992] **93,400,000 shares** (a 2:1 split of the 46.7M; no shares bought or sold), market
  **$3,911M** on the $1.024B cost; Berkshire's look-through share of Coke's earnings rose to
  $82M (from $69M). The largest holding in the portfolio, and the standing exemplar in the
  year's value/growth essay of a business that compounds incremental capital at very high
  returns (see [[Value and Growth]]).
- [1993] 93,400,000 shares held; market **$4,168M**. From 1991–93 Coke grew operating EPS 38%
  while its **stock rose only 11%** — "the companies overperformed their stocks" (Wall Street's
  "new apprehension about brand names"). Buffett supplies two history lessons: Coke's 1919 IPO
  at $40 fell below $20 by 1920 yet one share with dividends reinvested was worth **$2.1M** by
  1993; and a 1938 *Fortune* story already fretted Coke was "too late" — yet 1938's 207M cases
  became **10.7 billion** by 1993 (a 50× volume gain). Coke's **44% global soft-drink share** is
  the lead example in the year's [[Risk and Volatility]] / [[Economic Moat]] essay. Buffett also
  recounts that his read of neighbor [[Don Keough]] helped prompt the investment, and credits
  Keough + [[Roberto Goizueta]] with lifting Coke's market value from $4.4B to $58B in under 13
  years.
- [1994] Berkshire **added**, lifting the stake to **100,000,000 shares** (7.8%; cost
  $1,298,888K / market **$5,150,000K**) — one of only two holdings enlarged in the year (with
  [[American Express]]). Buffett quantifies the franchise as "**soft-drink earnings**": ~280B
  8-oz servings sold in 1994 at ~a penny each, of which Berkshire's 7.8% is an interest in ~21B
  servings worth ~**$200M**. The boyhood vignette: his first venture was buying a six-pack for
  25¢ and selling bottles at 5¢ — "It took only fifty years before I finally got it: the real
  money was in the **syrup**."
- [1995] 100,000,000 shares held untouched ("Rip Van Winkle mode"); market soared to **$7,425M**
  (cost $1,298.9M) — Berkshire's largest holding by far, in a year when "any fool could make a
  bundle."
- [1996] **200,000,000 shares** (a 2:1 split of the 100M; no shares traded), market **$10,525M**
  (cost $1,298.9M) — Berkshire's first holding past $10B, and the anchor example of the new
  **"[[The Inevitables]]"** concept (with [[Gillette]]). Buffett quotes Coke's **1896** report
  — president Asa Candler ("Coca-Cola is the article, par excellence, for the health and good
  feeling of all people") and 116,492 gallons of syrup sold that year vs. ~**3.2 billion** in
  1996 — to argue the "blueprint for the next 100 years was already drawn." He credits
  [[Roberto Goizueta]] (with Don Keough and Doug Ivester) for an "absolutely incredible job,"
  while warning that Coke and Gillette's chief past danger was **loss of focus** (the shrimp/oil
  digressions) — "not going to happen again … given their current and prospective managements."
  Look-through share of Coke's earnings: **$180M** (8.1% owned).
- [1997] 200,000,000 shares held; market surges to **$13,337.5M** (cost $1,298.9M) — by far
  Berkshire's largest holding; look-through share **$216M** (8.1%). The year's news is the loss
  of **[[Roberto Goizueta]]**, Coke's CEO since 1981, who **died in October**. Buffett reread
  "every one of the more than 100 letters" Goizueta had sent him — "a guidebook for success in
  both business and life" — and quotes the note: "what she refers to as an obsession, you call
  **focus**." Goizueta had "prepared for a seamless succession," and "the Coca-Cola Company will
  be the same steamroller under **Doug Ivester** as it was under Roberto." Coke's repurchases at
  past low prices are cited as having "benefitted Berkshire far more than do today's
  repurchases" (see [[Mr. Market]]).
- [1998] 200,000,000 shares held; market **$13,400M** (cost $1,299M) — still Berkshire's largest
  holding. Coke is among the major investees Buffett flags as having had "**operating shortfalls**"
  that dragged Berkshire's equity portfolio below the S&P 500 — problems he judges "almost
  certainly temporary" with "excellent" long-term prospects. (No look-through table this year; the
  General Re acquisition closed too near year-end.)
- [1999] 200,000,000 shares held; market **fell to $11,650M** (from $13,400M) as Coke's stock
  slid on "disappointing operating results" — one of the large investees whose stumbles made 1999
  Buffett's worst year (capital-allocation grade "D"). Look-through share **$144M**. "We still
  like these businesses and are content to have major investments in them."
- [2000] 200,000,000 shares held; market **$12,188M** (cost $1,299M) — still the largest holding;
  look-through share **$160M**. Held untouched among a portfolio Buffett calls "only mildly
  attractive" ("no 'bargains' among our current holdings").
- [[2022]] **"The Secret Sauce."** Buffett uses Coke as the headline example of how a few great decisions
  compound: Berkshire finished buying its **400 million shares in August 1994 for $1.3 billion**; the
  annual dividend grew from **$75 million (1994) to $704 million (2022)** ("Growth occurred every year,
  just as certain as birthdays… All Charlie and I were required to do was cash Coke's quarterly dividend
  checks"). At year-end 2022 the stake was worth **$25 billion** — about **5% of Berkshire's net worth**,
  roughly its weighting "long ago." "**The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom.**" (See
  [[Concentration]], [[The Power of Retained Earnings]].)
- [[2023]] **Untouched for "well over two decades."** Berkshire neither bought nor sold a share of Coke (or
  Amex) in 2023, "extending our own Rip Van Winkle slumber"; both "again rewarded our inaction… by
  increasing their earnings and dividends." Each accounts for **4–5% of Berkshire's net worth**. The
  lesson restated: "**When you find a truly wonderful business, stick with it. Patience pays, and one
  wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable.**"
