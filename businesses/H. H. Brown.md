# H. H. Brown

H. H. Brown Shoe Company is the leading North American manufacturer of work shoes
and boots, acquired by Berkshire on July 1, 1991 — the year's one sizable
acquisition ([[1991]]). It came to Berkshire through the kind of serendipity Buffett
prizes: CEO [[Frank Rooney]] had handed the sale assignment to a major investment
bank that never thought of Berkshire, but while playing golf in Florida, Rooney was
told by John Loomis — a Buffett friend and Berkshire shareholder "always on the
alert for something that might fit us" — that the company "should be right up
Berkshire's alley." Rooney called, and the deal was done almost at once. (Brown has
no connection to Brown Shoe of St. Louis.)

The business itself is a hard one: of the billion pairs of shoes bought annually in
the U.S., about 85% are imported, and most domestic manufacturers do poorly. The
wide range of styles and sizes makes inventory and receivables capital-heavy. Brown
nonetheless "has a history of earning unusually fine margins on sales and assets"
(~$25M pre-tax annually), which Buffett attributes to outstanding management — the
group built by founder [[Ray Heffernan]] and led by Rooney.

What "warms my heart" most is Brown's **compensation system**, one of the most
unusual Buffett had encountered: key managers draw a token salary of **$7,800** plus
a designated percentage of company profits *after a charge for capital employed*. So
"these managers therefore truly stand in the shoes of owners" — bearing the cost of
the capital they use, unlike the typical plan that is "long on carrots but short on
sticks" and "treat[s] equity capital as if it were cost-free." The arrangement
became a touchstone in Buffett's writing on [[Executive Compensation]]. In its half
year under Berkshire, Brown contributed $13.6M pre-tax ($8.6M to Berkshire).

## Evolution
- [1991] Acquired 7/1/91 via Frank Rooney; the capital-charge compensation system
  highlighted as a model; ~$25M pre-tax earner in a tough imported-goods industry.
- [1992] First full year under Berkshire: pre-tax earnings **$27.9M** (up from a half-year
  $13.6M). At year-end Brown made a sizable **add-on acquisition — [[Lowell Shoe|Lowell Shoe Company]]** (~$90M sales; maker of *Nursemates*, a leading line of nurses' shoes) — the
  largest of Berkshire's five 1992 bolt-ons and the prototype of "enlarg[ing] the domain of
  managers we already know to be outstanding" (see [[Acquisition Criteria]]).
- [1993] **Record profits, +35% above the 1992 high**, under [[Frank Rooney]], Jim Issler, and
  team — "expectations… considerably exceeded." Brown's success (and Rooney's confidence in the
  shoe business) directly led to the **[[Dexter Shoe]]** acquisition: Rooney had known
  [[Harold Alfond]] and [[Peter Lunder]] for decades and brokered the introduction. The three
  shoe units (Brown, [[Lowell Shoe|Lowell]], Dexter) reported together; 1994 combined sales
  were projected above $550M.
- [2001] **Brown becomes the rescue vehicle for [[Dexter Shoe]].** With shoe operations losing
  $46.2M pre-tax (Brown and Justin profits "swamped by losses at Dexter"), Buffett put the failing
  Dexter operation under **[[Frank Rooney]] and Jim Issler at H.H. Brown** — "these men have
  performed outstandingly… skillfully contending with the extraordinary changes that have bedeviled
  the footwear industry." Separately, in a P&R-history echo, Brown bought **Acme Boot's inventory
  and trademarks ($700K)** — Acme having been the second business bought by Buffett's old
  Graham-Newman affiliate decades earlier (see [[Fruit of the Loom]]).
