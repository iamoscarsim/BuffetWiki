# Scott Fetzer

The Scott & Fetzer Company of Cleveland, acquired by Berkshire right after year-end
1985 for about **$320 million**, is a diversified manufacturer and direct seller —
"a prototype" of the businesses Berkshire wants: "understandable, large,
well-managed, a good earner" ([[1985]]). It comprised 17 businesses with ~$700M of
sales, including **World Book** encyclopedias (~40% of sales; the U.S. leader,
outselling its four biggest rivals combined), **Kirby** home-care systems, Campbell
Hausfeld air compressors, and Wayne burners and pumps.

Run by CEO [[Ralph Schey]], whose "operating and capital-allocation record is
superb," Scott Fetzer became one of Berkshire's most quietly successful purchases.
In later letters Buffett repeatedly cites it as the example proving that high returns
on capital — not size or accounting complexity — make a business valuable: Scott
Fetzer earned extraordinary returns on tangible equity while sending most of its
earnings to Omaha. The deal also illustrated Berkshire's opportunistic style —
Buffett wrote Schey a cold letter after a leveraged ESOP buyout collapsed, and a
contract was signed within weeks.

## Evolution
- [1985] Acquired (~$320M) right after year-end; 17 businesses (World Book, Kirby,
  etc.), ~$700M sales, run by Ralph Schey. A prototype Berkshire acquisition.
- [1986] First full year; ~$315M paid for net assets carried at $172.4M (a $142.6M
  premium). Becomes the worked example of **[[Owner Earnings]]** in the year's
  Appendix — the "Company O vs. Company N" demonstration that purchase-price write-ups
  (~$11.6M of extra non-cash, non-deductible charges) lower GAAP earnings without
  changing the cash a business produces. [[World Book]] (encyclopedia units +7%) and
  [[Kirby]] (units +4%) both posted a fourth straight year of unit gains; Scott
  Fetzer's diversified-manufacturing units earned $25.4M pre-tax. Buffett's enthusiasm
  for [[Ralph Schey]] grows: "a superb businessman and a straight shooter."
- [1987] Results "even better" than 1986: pre-tax earnings **+10% while average capital
  employed declined significantly**. [[Ralph Schey]] now manages **19 businesses**;
  Buffett wishes he could "find a few additional units that could be put under Ralph's
  wing." [[World Book]] introduced its most dramatically revised edition since 1962 (color
  photos 14,000 → 24,000) with U.S. units up a fifth straight year. The Scott Fetzer Mfg.
  Group, World Book and Kirby are three of the **[[Sainted Seven]]**.
- [1988] All of [[Ralph Schey]]'s operations "performed splendidly"; returns on capital
  "continue to be exceptional." Within the Manufacturing Group, **Campbell Hausfeld**
  (the country's leading small/medium air-compressor maker) "more than doubled earnings
  since 1986." Kirby and World Book unit sales rose significantly with strong exports —
  World Book reached the Soviet Union (the only general encyclopedia in Moscow's largest
  American bookstore). Schey runs 19 businesses while serving on multiple Ohio boards.
- [1989] Aggregate earnings have "increased every year since our purchase"; returns on
  capital "exceptional." World Book is decentralizing from its single Chicago Merchandise
  Mart location into four sites (transition costs absorbed). Kirby's exports surged
  (international up 5× in four years, now 20% of unit sales) ahead of a 1990 model change.
  Schey's operations are ~**40% of non-insurance earnings**; Buffett ranks his ROE "in
  the top decile" of the Fortune 500 — "our acquisition of Ralph as a manager was fully
  as important as our acquisition of the businesses."
- [1990] [[Ralph Schey]] "runs 19 businesses with a mastery few bring to running one";
  Scott Fetzer's ROE would rank "close to the top of the Fortune 500." Its **finance unit**
  earned a record $12.2M pre-tax. World Book earnings rose on slightly lower units (the
  decentralization costs easing); **Kirby's "Generation 3"** vacuum was "an unqualified
  success" (units up, earnings held back by start-up/learning-curve costs; exports +20%);
  **Campbell Hausfeld** set a record $109M of sales, 30%+ from products under five years old.
- [1992] **"Ralph Schey hit a grand slam"**: a record **$110M pre-tax on just $116M of
  equity capital** — "not the product of leverage" (only minor borrowing, plus the finance
  sub). Scott Fetzer now operates with *less* inventory and fixed assets than at the 1986
  purchase, and over seven years of ownership has **distributed more than 100% of its
  earnings** to Berkshire while still growing them sharply — Buffett's standing proof that a
  high-return business needs little incremental capital (see [[Value and Growth]]). "Ralph
  just keeps on outdoing himself."
- [1994] **The centerpiece worked example** of book vs. [[Intrinsic Business Value]]. Bought
  1986 for **$315.2M** against **$172.6M** book (a $142.6M premium = intrinsic value near double
  book). A nine-year table shows earnings climbing from **$40.3M to $79.3M** while book value
  *fell* (excess cash paid out, no leverage added) — so ROE became "**truly extraordinary**":
  had Scott Fetzer been on the 1993 Fortune 500, its return on equity would have ranked **first**
  (excluding bankruptcy-windfall firms), double the #10 company. The **accounting lesson**: GAAP
  forced Berkshire to amortize the purchase premium, marking Scott Fetzer's carrying value
  *down* to **$148.2M** ($94M book + $54.2M remaining premium) even as intrinsic value soared —
  charges Buffett deems "not… an economic cost." Credit to [[Ralph Schey]]: "Ben Graham taught
  me… it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results." (Still 22
  businesses, the same line-up as at purchase; main units World Book, Kirby, Campbell Hausfeld.)
- [1998] **A new record — "[[Ralph Schey]] was just warming up."** Scott Fetzer earned **$96.5M
  after-tax on its $112M net worth** "operating with no leverage" (beyond a conservative level of
  debt in its finance subsidiary) — the extraordinary-ROE story still compounding 13 years after
  purchase. Buffett cites it (revisiting his 1994 letter) as proof that Berkshire's operating
  managers "almost invariably deliver earnings that are at the very top of what conditions … allow."
  Pre-tax (ex-finance) $137M.
- [2000] **[[Ralph Schey]] retires** at year-end after **15 years** running Scott Fetzer. Buffett
  tallies the legacy: under Schey the company **distributed $1.03 billion to Berkshire against a
  $230 million net purchase price** — cash redeployed into other businesses, so "Ralph's
  contributions to Berkshire's present value extend well into the billions." "As a manager, Ralph
  belongs in **Berkshire's Hall of Fame**." (Pre-tax ex-finance $122M.)
