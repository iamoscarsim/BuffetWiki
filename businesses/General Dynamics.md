# General Dynamics

General Dynamics, the defense contractor, became a new Berkshire common-stock holding in
1992 — and a vivid example of an [[Arbitrage]] position converting into a long-term
investment ([[1992]]). At year-end Berkshire held 4,350,000 shares (14.1% of the company;
cost $312.4M / market $450.8M).

Buffett "had paid little attention to the company" until mid-1992, when it announced it
would repurchase ~30% of its shares via a **Dutch tender**. Seeing an arbitrage
opportunity, he began buying expecting to tender for a small profit (a move Berkshire had
made "perhaps a half-dozen times" in recent years). But studying the company changed his
mind: CEO **[[Bill Anders]]** "had a clearly articulated and rational strategy… focused
and imbued with a sense of urgency," with "truly remarkable" results — disciplined
shrinkage of a shrinking-demand defense business and large returns of capital to owners.
"In short order, I dumped my arbitrage thoughts and decided that Berkshire should become a
long-term investor with Bill." The tender's swollen trading volume let Berkshire buy 14%
of the post-tender shares in a single month. Look-through earnings contribution in 1992:
$11M.

## Evolution
- [1992] New holding (14.1%, cost $312.4M / market $450.8M). Began as a Dutch-tender
  arbitrage, converted to a long-term position on admiration for CEO [[Bill Anders]]'
  capital-return strategy.
- [1993] 4,350,000 shares (13.9%), market **$401.3M**; look-through share $25M. [[Bill Anders]]'
  capital-return program was so aggressive that special distributions **cut Berkshire's cost
  basis from $312.4M to $94.9M** in a single year — a vivid demonstration of the disciplined
  shrinkage Buffett admired.
- [1994] **Exits the disclosed holdings** (no longer among Berkshire's over-$300M positions),
  having been largely sold/distributed — a successful, relatively short-lived position that
  began as arbitrage. The original [[Bill Anders]] capital-return thesis had played out as
  intended.
