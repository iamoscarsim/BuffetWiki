# National Indemnity Company

National Indemnity Company (NICO) is Berkshire's foundational insurance
operation and the engine of its transformation from a failing textile maker into
an investment powerhouse. Buffett entered the insurance industry in **1967** by
buying National Indemnity and its sister company National Fire and Marine for
approximately **$8.6 million** ([[1977]]). At purchase their combined premium
volume was $22 million; by 1977 Berkshire's aggregate insurance premium volume
had reached $151 million — roughly a 600% increase — achieved without issuing any
new Berkshire shares.

NICO writes commercial auto and general liability, and operates a reinsurance
department (managed in 1977 by George Young). Its defining cultural trait is
**[[Insurance Underwriting Discipline]]**: the willingness to let competitors
take business "even at foolish prices" rather than write policies at inadequate
rates. In 1977, under [[Phil Liesche]] (with Roland Miller in underwriting and
Bill Lyons in claims), NICO posted large volume gains and excellent margins after
competitors retreated in the wake of the 1974–75 industry crisis — while
generating large sums for investment, the early articulation of **[[Insurance Float]]**.

Buffett observes that insurance products are commoditized promises — easily
copied, with no trademark or patent moats — so "the nature of the insurance
business magnifies the effect which individual managers have on company
performance." NICO's value rests heavily on the discipline and skill of its
people.

## Evolution
- [1977] First appearance. Bought 1967 for ~$8.6M; premium volume grown to $151M.
  Underwriting discipline and the cyclical nature of insurance pricing established
  as core themes.
- [1978] [[Phil Liesche]]'s segment earned ~$11M underwriting profit on ~$90M
  premiums — "a truly extraordinary achievement." Founder [[Jack Ringwalt]]'s
  philosophy credited as "etched upon the company." Frank DeNardo brought in to fix
  the troubled California Worker's Comp office (volume cut to ~25% of prior levels);
  George Young's reinsurance still generating heavy investable funds but
  underwriting "not what it should be," with a warning about industry self-delusion
  in casualty reserving.
- [1979] Liesche segment: $8.4M underwriting profit on ~$82M earned premiums
  (volume deliberately reduced). [[Jack Ringwalt]]'s founding discipline "never
  wavered." Entered **surety reinsurance** under Chet Noble; Frank DeNardo fixed the
  California Worker's Comp operation (seven-figure savings). George Young's
  reinsurance still unsatisfactory on underwriting; Buffett expects to shrink
  reinsurance amid a coming wave of irrational competition.
- [1980] Liesche's managers "outdid themselves" — flat volume but underwriting
  margins relative to the industry at an all-time high; corporate HQ will not
  complain about expected lower 1981 volume, and won't cut staff/salaries. Buffett:
  the Ringwalt-rooted discipline, "if suspended, probably could not be fully
  regained." Frank DeNardo, who had fixed the California Worker's Comp mess, **died
  at 37**; Dan Grossman stepped in (using Redwood Fire & Casualty as the vehicle).
  Insurance Company of Iowa (chronic loser since 1973) was abandoned and merged into
  Cornhusker Casualty.
- [1982] Underwriting deteriorated sharply (insurance group loss); traditional NICO
  lines "now priced at levels that guarantee underwriting losses." **[[Phil Liesche]]
  retired at 65** — Buffett calls him and predecessor [[Jack Ringwalt]] "the two prime
  movers in National Indemnity's success" and the company "the most important
  operation in Berkshire's growth." Parent-company insurance oversight passed from
  Buffett to [[Mike Goldberg]].
- [1992] *(Coverage gap 1983–1991 — to be backfilled at lint.)* NICO is invoked as the
  template for the year's [[Central States Indemnity]] purchase — Berkshire's "first"
  acquisition (1967, from founder-friend [[Jack Ringwalt]]) echoed by Central States (from
  35-year friend [[Bill Kizer]]): "Hollywood has had good luck with sequels; I believe we,
  too, will." Operationally, NICO's **commercial-auto and general-liability** book under
  **Don Wurster** recorded an underwriting profit, generating float at a *less-than-zero*
  cost and contributing materially to Berkshire's low cost of float (see [[Insurance Float]]).
- [1996] NICO's **traditional business posted a combined ratio of 74.2** (a three-year average of
  83.0 under **Don Wurster**), again developing "a large amount of float compared to premium
  volume." It anchors Berkshire's cluster of superbly-run small primary insurers — homestate
  under Rod Eldred (CR 87.1), workers' comp under Brad Kinstler, Central States under John Kizer,
  and the new [[Kansas Bankers Surety]] — whose aggregate underwriting record Buffett calls
  "virtually unmatched in the industry."
- [1997] An even better year: NICO's traditional business posted a **32.9% underwriting profit**
  (three-year average 24.3% under **Don Wurster**), again throwing off "a large amount of float
  compared to premium volume." Across the five smaller primary insurers (NICO, homestate 14.1% /
  Rod Eldred, workers' comp / Brad Kinstler, Central States / John Kizer, [[Kansas Bankers Surety]] / Don Towle) the aggregate underwriting profit was **15.0%** — "the two Dons, along
  with Rod, Brad and John, have created significant value for Berkshire."
- [2004] **"Portrait of a Disciplined Underwriter."** Buffett devotes the insurance section to NICO
  as the proof that a commodity insurer *can* build a durable edge — through a **managerial mindset
  most insurers can't replicate**. A 25-year table shows written premium deliberately sliding from
  **$366M (1986) to $55M (1999)** — "we never left customers — but they left us" — enabled by a
  **no-layoff promise** that removes employees' incentive to rationalize bad pricing (the
  [[Institutional Imperative]] and "fear factor" beaten). "Had we not made this acquisition,
  Berkshire would be lucky to be worth half of what it is today." CEO **[[Don Wurster]]** ("the
  graduate," running NICO since 1989) "will accept a walk rather than swing at a bad pitch," holding
  **~$950M** of near-certain negative-cost float. Of NICO's four CEOs since 1940, "none have bent."
- [2014] **"Per page, this has to be Berkshire's best deal."** In the 50th-anniversary letter Buffett
  retells the 15-minute 1967 purchase from Jack Ringwalt (no audit, no lawyers, a homemade 1½-page
  contract) and reveals NICO's GAAP net worth now stands at **$111 billion — exceeding that of any
  other insurer in the world.** It remains the foundation of Berkshire's insurance fortress (and houses
  Ajit Jain's reinsurance group). "**All of this began with our 1967 purchase of National Indemnity for
  $8.6 million.**"
- [[2020]] **The founding parable — "A Tale of Two Cities."** Buffett retells NICO's origin: **Jack
  Ringwalt**, an Omaha Central High graduate, started it in **1940** with **$125,000**, his "pipsqueak
  operation" competing against giant, well-capitalized insurers with no cost advantage — succeeding by
  focusing on **"odd-ball" risks** the "big boys" ignored. The 1967 sale to Berkshire took "**all of 15
  minutes to reach a handshake. I never asked for an audit**" (Ringwalt, who "disliked regulators," got
  the urge to sell whenever annoyed by their supervision). Today NICO is "**the only company in the world
  prepared to insure certain giant risks**" — still based in Omaha, "a few miles from Berkshire's home
  office," and home to [[Ajit Jain]]'s reinsurance group.
