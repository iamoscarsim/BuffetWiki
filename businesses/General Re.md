# General Re

General Re Corp. is the reinsurance giant Berkshire acquired on **December 21, 1998** for
about **$22 billion** in stock — by far its largest acquisition to that point, and the
deal that made Berkshire a global all-lines reinsurer. The company owns 100% of **General
Reinsurance Corporation** (the largest U.S. property-casualty reinsurer) and 82% of
**Cologne Re** (the world's oldest reinsurance company, with an arrangement to buy the
rest); together they reinsure all lines and operate in **124 countries**. Its long-time
CEO was **[[Ron Ferguson]]**, under whom "General Re's name has stood for quality,
integrity and professionalism in reinsurance."

**Why it fit Berkshire.** Buffett's thesis is structural. A reinsurer "gets paid for
absorbing the volatility that the client insurer wants to shed" — yet a *publicly-held*
reinsurer is itself "graded by both its owners and those who evaluate its credit on the
**smoothness of its own results**," so wide earnings swings hurt its P/E and credit
ratings. That market reality pushes public reinsurers into costly moves — laying off
business through **retrocessions**, or rejecting good business "simply because it threatens
to bring on too much volatility." Berkshire, "a **Fort Knox of capital**" that "happily
accepts volatility, just as long as it carries with it the expectation of increased profits
over time," is "the **perfect structure for writing — and *retaining* — reinsurance in
virtually any amount**" (the same edge behind its [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]] book).
General Re supplies the distribution force, technical skill, and international reach;
Berkshire supplies balance-sheet strength, tax and investment benefits, and freedom from
quarterly-earnings pressure — so the company can "do what it does best, unfettered by the
constraints that have limited its growth." "We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."

Berkshire assumed General Re's **investment portfolio** (selling its ~250 equity positions
right after the merger, incurring $935M of tax — "we don't back into decisions"), though
*not* Cologne Re's. It would **not** be involved in underwriting. General Re's enormous
float — about **two-thirds** of Berkshire's $22.76B year-end total — is unlikely to grow
fast in a soft reinsurance market near-term. Fittingly, General Re "played a key role in
the resuscitation of **[[GEICO]]** from its near-death status in 1976." *(In later years
General Re's loss reserves and underwriting would prove a serious disappointment — a
thread later letters will record.)*

## Evolution
- [1998] Acquired for ~$22B (closed 12/21/98); 100% of General Reinsurance + 82% of Cologne
  Re; CEO [[Ron Ferguson]]; 124 countries. Bought for Berkshire's structural edge — capital
  strength + tolerance for earnings volatility — that lets it write and retain reinsurance no
  public reinsurer can. Investment portfolio liquidated on the merger ($935M tax); float ⅔ of
  Berkshire's total. Replaced Gen Re's stock options with a cash plan (see [[Executive Compensation]]).
- [1999] **A brutal first full year — an "aberrational" $1.4B-plus underwriting loss** that drove
  Berkshire's whole insurance cost of float to **5.8%** (General Re alone held **$15,166M** of the
  $25,298M total float). "Our business was extremely **underpriced**, both domestically and
  internationally, a condition that is improving but not yet corrected." Buffett still expects the
  loss to "fall considerably" in 2000 absent a mega-cat, though full rate-increase benefit lags.
  Two fixes: **rates are being raised**, and incentive compensation at General Re and Cologne is
  now **tied directly to float growth and cost of float** ("the same variables that determine value
  for owners"). His verdict on the franchise stays optimistic: with Berkshire's backing General Re
  "has … the financial clout to become **the world's most profitable reinsurance company**," and
  "we have no doubt that **[[Ron Ferguson]]** and his crew can make it happen." *(In hindsight the
  reserve and pricing problems ran deeper than "aberrational" — later letters revisit this.)*
- [2000] **"The news has turned considerably better."** [[Ron Ferguson]], joined by **Joe Brandon**
  and **Tad Montross**, "took many actions during 2000 to bring that company's profitability back to
  past standards" — significantly repricing or dropping "business that was severely unprofitable."
  Pricing isn't "fully corrected," but absent a mega-cat, General Re's float cost "should fall
  materially" in 2001. General Re still held ~$15.5B of Berkshire's $27.9B float. "The last couple
  of years haven't been any fun for Ron and his crew … but they have stepped up to tough decisions,
  and Charlie and I applaud them." (Brandon would soon succeed Ferguson as CEO.)
- [2001] **The reckoning — and a confession.** "In the past I have assured you that General Re
  was underwriting with discipline — and I have been proven wrong." General Re **broke all three
  of Berkshire's [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|underwriting rules]]**: it underpriced
  (not least by ignoring terrorism *exposure*), chased market share, and — most damaging — **failed
  to reserve correctly**, belatedly recognizing **$800M of prior-year loss costs** in 2001 (see
  [[Loss Reserving]]). September 11th then hit General Re hardest of any Berkshire unit, driving a
  reinsurance underwriting loss of **−$4,318M pre-tax**. Buffett takes the terrorism-exposure
  failure as personally his ("I violated the **Noah rule**"). **Joe Brandon** (CEO from September)
  and **Tad Montross** (president) — likened to Jack Welch — are charged with restoring discipline
  "with appropriate urgency"; float nonetheless grew to **$19,310M**. "When it was an independent
  company, General Re often shone… With underwriting discipline restored, General Re should be a
  huge asset for Berkshire." See [[Joe Brandon]], [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [2002] **"I was dead wrong" — the fullest reckoning, and the turn.** Buffett admits that when he
  merged in 1998 he believed Gen Re stuck to the three [[Insurance Underwriting Discipline|rules]] —
  "I had studied the operation for decades… At merger time, I detected no slippage." In fact "Gen
  Re's culture and practices had substantially changed": it was "**grossly mispricing its current
  business**" and had amassed a **nuclear/chemical/biological (NCB) aggregation** so large that "had
  Gen Re remained independent, the World Trade Center attack **alone** would have threatened the
  company's existence." 2002 took a **further $1.31B** of prior-year reserve correction ("I wish I
  didn't know now what I didn't know then"). Under **Joe Brandon** and **Tad Montross**, NCB risk
  was cut to a tolerable level, underwriting was repriced ("size simply doesn't count"), and
  reserving tightened — restoring a near-no-cost float. Crucially, Gen Re emerged the **sole
  across-the-board AAA** reinsurer among the giants after all three of its largest rivals were
  downgraded — "in a class by itself in respect to financial strength," since "**cheap reinsurance
  is a fool's bargain**." Float $22,207M. It also brought the unwanted **[[General Re Securities]]**
  (see [[Derivatives]]).
- [2003] **"Gen Re is fixed."** The discipline and reserving restored under **Joe Brandon** and
  **Tad Montross** held up — Gen Re returned to a **+$145M underwriting profit** (vs. −$1,393M in
  2002) and "will be a powerful engine driving Berkshire's future profitability" (though reinsurance
  "is certain to deliver blows from time to time"). Its edge widened: after rivals' downgrades,
  **Gen Re and [[National Indemnity Company]] are the only two AAA-rated major reinsurers** —
  decisive because Berkshire "**retain[s] virtually all** of the risks we assume," so its ability to
  pay never depends on others.
- [2004] **Joe Brandon** "has restored a long-admired culture of underwriting discipline that, for a
  time, had lost its way." Current-year results were good (a slim **+$3M** underwriting profit) but
  **offset by adverse development** from pre-Brandon years. With NICO, Gen Re remains one of only
  two AAA reinsurers. Float $23,120M.
- [2005] A **−$334M** underwriting loss, driven by the year's record hurricanes (Katrina et al.) —
  but "performed well… considering the extraordinary hurricane losses that battered the industry"
  under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross. Buffett applies **Pascal's wager** to climate uncertainty
  (see [[Super-Catastrophe Reinsurance]]): write mega-cat only at much higher prices. Float
  $22,920M.
- [2006] **Fully recovered** — a **+$526M** underwriting profit (no hurricanes; disciplined
  pricing). Joe Brandon and Tad Montross had completed the turnaround Buffett charged them with in
  2001. Float $22,827M. *(Brandon would depart in 2008 amid finite-reinsurance litigation fallout;
  Montross succeeded him.)*
- [2007] **"The Tiffany of reinsurers."** Gen Re is now Berkshire's largest source of "home-grown"
  float (**$23B**) and "a huge asset." Buffett delivers the cleanest statement of his 1998
  misjudgment: Gen Re's reputation for underwriting skill "**outlived its factual underpinnings, a
  flaw that I completely missed**… The General Re of 1998 was not operated as the General Re of 1968
  or 1978." Brandon and Montross — six years in — have "restored the luster," doing "**first-class
  business in a first-class way**" despite "costly and time-consuming legacy problems." Underwriting
  profit **+$555M**.
- [2008] **"Regained its luster" — and a leadership handoff.** Another outstanding year (underwriting
  profit **+$342M**; float $21,074M). **[[Joe Brandon]] stepped down in spring 2008** and **[[Tad Montross]]** became CEO; Buffett thanks Brandon "for righting the ship" and is "certain that, with
  Tad, General Re's future is in the best of hands." The crisis underscored the franchise's edge:
  "reinsurance is a business of long-term promises… A promise is no better than the person or
  institution making it" — and General Re "is the **only reinsurer backed by an AAA corporation**."
  Ben Franklin: "It's difficult for an empty sack to stand upright."
- [2009] **"A gleaming jewel in our insurance crown."** Under Tad Montross, General Re had an
  outstanding underwriting year (**+$477M**; float $21,014M) and delivered "unusually large amounts of
  float per dollar of premium." It finally attained **100% ownership of Cologne Re** (a key
  partially-owned piece of its global presence since 1995). Its life-reinsurance arm "has grown
  increasingly valuable."
- [2010] **The "four disciplines" exemplar.** Using General Re (Tad Montross), Buffett codifies the
  **four disciplines of sound underwriting**: (1) understand all loss exposures; (2) conservatively
  assess the odds and cost of a loss; (3) set a premium that profits on average; and (4) **"the
  willingness to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained."** "Many insurers pass the
  first three tests and flunk the fourth" — bowing to Wall Street, brokers, or a "testosterone-driven
  CEO['s]" refusal to accept shrinking volume. "Tad has observed all four of the insurance commandments,
  and it shows": General Re's huge float "has been **better than cost-free**" (UW profit **$452M**;
  float $20,049M).
- [2011] **"In the first few years… a major headache. Now it's a treasure."** Buffett's blunt verdict
  on the 1998 acquisition's full arc. Under Tad Montross, General Re again honored the four underwriting
  disciplines (UW profit **$144M**; float $19,714M), its float "better than cost-free." (He restates
  the four disciplines with the wrong-way-driver joke: "Mabel, they don't know the half of it… there
  are hundreds of them.")
- [2012]–[2013] The four disciplines keep paying off (UW profit $355M in 2012, $283M in 2013; float
  ~$20B), with **international life reinsurance** the standout growth engine. Buffett's verdict on the
  1998 deal completes its arc: once "a major headache" that briefly made him fear "I had made a huge
  mistake," **"General Re is now a gem."**
- [[2016]] **A leadership handoff and a "powerhouse" year.** Underwriting profit **$190M** (float
  **$17,699M**). **[[Tad Montross]] retired after 39 years** at General Re — "a class act in every way" —
  succeeded as CEO by **[[Kara Raiguel]]**, who had worked with **[[Ajit Jain]]** for 16 years. Buffett
  calls Gen Re "another reinsurance powerhouse" and uses it to restate the **four disciplines** of sound
  underwriting (understand exposures; assess loss odds/cost conservatively; price for an average profit;
  and **"be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained"**): "Many insurers pass
  the first three tests and flunk the fourth… Tad never listened to that nonsensical excuse for sloppy
  underwriting, and neither will Kara."
