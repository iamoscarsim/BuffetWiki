# Dexter Shoe

Dexter Shoe Company of Dexter, Maine — a maker of popular-priced men's and women's shoes —
was acquired by Berkshire on **November 7, 1993**, the third leg of Berkshire's brief
plunge into footwear after [[H. H. Brown]] (1991) and [[Lowell Shoe]] (1992). Buffett called
it "one of the **best-managed companies** Charlie and I have seen in our business lifetimes"
and, pointedly, a business that "needs *no* fixing" ([[1993]]).

Founder **[[Harold Alfond]]** had started in a shoe factory at 25¢/hour and founded Dexter
in 1956 with $10,000; his nephew **[[Peter Lunder]]** joined in 1958. Together they built a
producer of **7.5 million pairs a year** (most made in Maine, the rest in Puerto Rico),
**77 retail outlets** in the Northeast, and ~**15% of U.S. golf-shoe output**, winning
supplier awards from Nordstrom and J.C. Penney. Its competitiveness against low-wage imports
— shared with H. H. Brown — was, Buffett argued, proof that "ingenious managements" and
"skilled labor forces" could keep U.S. shoe plants viable. With Dexter, Berkshire's 1994 shoe
operations were expected to exceed **$550M in sales** and **$85M pre-tax**.

**The fateful structure.** Alfond and Lunder, uninterested in cash, took **25,203 Berkshire
shares** — trading "a 100% interest in a single terrific business for a smaller interest in a
large group of terrific businesses," tax-free. Buffett celebrated the deal in 1993; it would
become, in later letters, his **most-regretted acquisition** — not because Dexter's business
collapsed under import competition (it did), but because he paid in *Berkshire stock*, handing
the sellers a permanently appreciating stake for a business that went to near-zero. That
reckoning belongs to later years; this page will record it as the corpus advances. (See
[[Mistakes Ledger]], [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]].)

## Evolution
- [1993] Acquired 11/7/93 for 25,203 Berkshire shares; 7.5M pairs/yr, 77 outlets, ~15% of
  U.S. golf shoes; founders Harold Alfond and Peter Lunder kept running it. Celebrated as a
  best-in-class operator and a debt-free, no-fixing-needed business.
- [1995] The combined **Shoe Group** (Dexter + [[H. H. Brown]] + [[Lowell Shoe]]) saw earnings
  fall to **$58.4M pre-tax** (from $85.5M) in an industry suffering "depressed earnings throughout
  last year." Buffett framed it as **cyclical, not secular** — Berkshire "at least maintained, and
  in some instances widened, our competitive superiority" against weaker rivals, and he expected a
  rebound. *(The secular import problem that would ultimately gut Dexter is not yet acknowledged.)*
- [1999] **The secular problem is finally named.** Dexter was "the exception" among Berkshire's
  otherwise-excellent manufacturing/retail businesses — but, Buffett stresses, "the shortfall did
  **not** occur because of managerial problems" (the Dexter executives "are every bit the equal of
  our other managers"). The cause is structural: Berkshire "manufacture[s] shoes primarily in the
  U.S.," and "**approximately 93% of the 1.3 billion pairs of shoes purchased in this country came
  from abroad**, where extremely low-cost labor is the rule." Berkshire (Dexter + [[H. H. Brown]])
  is now "the leading domestic manufacturer of shoes," but to "remain viable" is **sourcing more
  output internationally**, incurring "significant severance and relocation costs." Buffett still
  voices optimism that the shoe leaders ([[Harold Alfond]], [[Peter Lunder]], [[Frank Rooney]], Jim
  Issler) can engineer a Helzberg's-style rebound — but this is the letter where the 1993 deal's
  fatal flaw comes into view (see [[Mistakes Ledger]]; the explicit mea culpa lands in later years).
- [2000] **The explicit confession.** "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in
  1993. Furthermore, I **compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in
  payment**." To "recognize my error," Berkshire **charged off all the remaining accounting goodwill
  attributable to the Dexter transaction** — "We may regain some economic goodwill at Dexter in the
  future, but we **clearly have none at present**." The shoe businesses face "another very tough
  year" as domestic production proves uncompetitive; Buffett praises the (blameless) shoe managers,
  "working very hard at a tough — and often terribly painful — job." This is the canonical Dexter
  mea culpa — the deal becoming Buffett's textbook lesson that paying in *appreciating stock* for a
  business that went to zero compounded the loss many times over (see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]], [[Mistakes Ledger]]).
- [2001] Shoe operations **lost $46.2M pre-tax**, "with profits at [[H. H. Brown]] and Justin
  swamped by losses at Dexter." Buffett restates the three Dexter errors plainly — "(1) buying
  it in the first place; (2) paying for it with stock and (3) procrastinating when the need for
  changes in its operations was obvious" — and refuses to shift blame ("they were mine"). Dexter
  is **placed under [[Frank Rooney]] and Jim Issler at [[H. H. Brown]]**; 2002 will still be hurt
  by unprofitable sales commitments made in 2001, after which the shoe business should be
  "reasonably profitable." The operating footprint is being shifted offshore to survive.
- [2002] **The operation is turned around.** "Thanks to [[Frank Rooney]] and Jim Issler of H.H.
  Brown, the Dexter operation has been turned around" — Berkshire earned **$24M in shoes**, an
  upward swing of **$70M from 2001** (Randy Watson at Justin also raised margins while trimming
  capital). The *business* is salvaged; the *capital* error of the 1993 stock-funded purchase
  remains permanent (see [[Mistakes Ledger]], [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]).
- [2007] **The definitive quantification — "the worst deal that I've made."** In the great/good/
  gruesome confession Buffett puts a final number on it: Dexter cost "**not $400 million, but rather
  $3.5 billion**" because he paid in **25,203 Berkshire A shares** rather than cash — "**I gave away
  1.6% of a wonderful business — one now valued at $220 billion — to buy a worthless business.**" The
  canonical lesson of [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]. (Bobby Bare: "I've never gone to bed with
  an ugly woman, but I've sure woke up with a few.")
