# WPPSS Bonds

Berkshire's 1983–84 purchase of bonds of the Washington Public Power Supply System
("Whoops") — a contrarian fixed-income bet introduced in the [[1984]] letter that
illustrates Buffett's **"bond-as-a-business"** lens. WPPSS had defaulted in July
1983 on $2.2 billion of bonds for the abandoned Projects 4 and 5; the cloud over the
still-performing Projects 1, 2, and 3 (backed by Bonneville Power Administration)
let Berkshire buy ~$139M of those bonds (under 3% of the issue) at depressed prices.

Buffett analyzes the bonds exactly as he would a business: the $139M "buys" a
business contractually earning **$22.7M after tax (~16.3% on unleveraged capital)**,
available currently in cash — economics few operating businesses match, and which
would cost $250–300M to buy in an operating company. The minuses he names honestly:
a "very slight risk" of total loss, possible interest interruption, and capped
upside (~48% above cost, the $205M face value). The bet exemplifies his
[[Concentration]] under [[Financial Strength]] and his willingness to make
"intelligent-but-with-some-chance-of-looking-like-an-idiot" decisions. It also
prompts his Graham citation: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most
businesslike."

## Evolution
- [1984] ~$139M purchased (Projects 1–3) at distressed prices, earning ~16.3%
  after tax; presented as a bond-as-business / concentration case study, with
  candid risk disclosure.
- [1986] Position now carried at **amortized cost $218M / market $310M**, paying
  **$31.7M of annual tax-exempt income** — cited as the kind of "very special
  situation" that is the rare exception to Buffett's wariness of long-term bonds.
- [1987] **Added to** during the year; year-end amortized cost **$240M / market $316M**,
  paying **$34M** of annual tax-exempt income — Buffett's example of investing "a moderate
  portion" in long-term bonds when there is "a significant edge in a specific security."
- [1988] Carried at **$247M cost / ~$352M market**, now **rated AA–** by S&P (ratings had
  been suspended when Berkshire bought, with after-tax yields ~17%). Buffett reports the
  bet "worked out about as expected" — and actually beat "the general run of business
  acquisitions made in 1983" on an unleveraged after-tax basis. Still Berkshire's only
  long-term bonds; it only makes him "hope that we run into some other large stigmatized
  issue… significantly misappraised by the market."
- [1989] Berkshire **sold its low-coupon WPPSS issues** (bought at deep discounts, many
  had roughly doubled in price while paying 15–17% tax-free), at prices only slightly
  below high-grade tax-exempts. It **kept all high-coupon issues**, expecting them to be
  called for redemption in 1991–92 and the early-to-mid 1990s — the position's planned
  wind-down after a textbook contrarian success.
