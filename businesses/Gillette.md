# Gillette

The Gillette Company — the dominant global razor-and-blade franchise — became one of
Berkshire's flagship long-term holdings, entered in [[1989]] via a **$600 million
convertible preferred** and later converted to a large common-stock stake. Of the four
preferreds Berkshire bought in 1987–89, Gillette is the one whose underlying business
Buffett genuinely loves.

The 1989 preferred carried an **8.75% dividend**, mandatory redemption in ten years,
and the right to convert into common at **$50 per share**. Buffett joined Gillette's
board and made clear the difference from the [[USAir]] and [[Champion International]]
preferreds bought the same year: "Gillette's business is very much the kind we like.
Charlie and I think we understand the company's economics and therefore believe we can
make a reasonably intelligent guess about its future. (If you haven't tried Gillette's
new **Sensor** razor, go right out and get one.)" The bet was as much on CEO
**[[Colman Mockler]]** as on the razor franchise — a low-cost, repeat-purchase consumer
staple with the pricing power and durable [[Economic Moat]] Buffett prizes. Berkshire's
preferred carried full voting rights on a converted basis; Buffett would convert to
common in 1991, and the stake rode Gillette's growth (and the eventual 2005 merger into
Procter & Gamble).

## Evolution
- [1989] $600M 8.75% convertible preferred (convert at $50; 10-yr redemption). The one
  preferred whose economics Buffett understands and admires; he joins the board. CEO
  [[Colman Mockler]]; the Sensor razor launch. Voting rights on a fully-converted basis.
- [1990] The preferred is **called**, converting April 1, 1991 into **12 million common
  shares** — turning the holding into a major equity stake (and reducing reported earnings
  ~$35M annually). Buffett values it "as worth somewhat more" than cost. A *Forbes* cover
  story praised Gillette's quality-driven culture; "in so depicting Gillette, *Forbes* in
  fact painted a portrait of [[Colman Mockler]]" — who **died in January 1991**, mourned
  in a moving eulogy ("no description better fitted Colman than 'gentleman'").
- [1991] The conversion (April 1) plus a Gillette **2:1 stock split** leaves Berkshire
  holding **24,000,000 common shares** (cost $600M / market **$1,347,000,000**). Gillette is
  now a flagship equity; together with [[Coca-Cola]], its sharp **P/E re-rating** accounted
  for ~$1.6B of Berkshire's $2.1B net-worth gain — the one-time **"double-dip"** Buffett
  cautions will not recur. The flip side: converting from preferred to common **cut
  look-through earnings** (the old ~$45M after-tax preferred dividend exceeded the
  partial-year common look-through), a foreseen drag on the 1991 total.
- [1992] 24,000,000 shares held unchanged; market **$1,365M**; look-through share $38M.
  Buffett notes the Gillette preferred (converted to common in 1991) was the standout of the
  negotiated-preferred program — better than the others, though even it underperformed the
  open-market purchases he prefers.
- [1993] 24,000,000 shares; market **$1,431M**; look-through share $44M. From 1991–93 Gillette
  grew operating EPS 37% while its stock rose only 6% — the business outran the stock. Its
  **>60% share (by value) of the world blade market** is cited beside [[Coca-Cola]] as a moat
  so obvious "to even the casual observer" that the similarity of its beta to ordinary
  companies' only proves beta meaningless (see [[Risk and Volatility]], [[Economic Moat]]).
- [1994] 24,000,000 shares; market **$1,797M** (a strong year for the stock). Buffett quantifies
  the franchise: via its Gillette stock Berkshire has "a 7% share of the world's razor and blade
  market (measured by revenues, not by units)," ~$250M of 1994 sales — one of the "rare gems" he
  prefers owning a piece of "rather than 100% of a rhinestone." Look-through share $51M.
- [1995] **48,000,000 shares** (a 2:1 split of the 24M); market **$2,502M** — "our best holding"
  among the convertible preferreds, the only one whose business Buffett "told you from the start
  was a superior business." Yet in the same breath he calls the 1989 preferred his **"biggest
  mistake"** (one "never recognized on financial statements"): had he negotiated for **common**
  (~60M shares at ~$10.50) instead of the $600M preferred (48M converted shares), Berkshire would
  have been ~**$625M** better off (less ~$70M of excess preferred dividends). See [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [1996] 48,000,000 shares held; market **$3,732M** (cost $600M); look-through share **$73M**
  (8.6% owned). Named, with [[Coca-Cola]], one of **"[[The Inevitables]]"** — "no sensible
  observer … questions that Coke and Gillette will dominate their fields worldwide for an
  investment lifetime," with dominance likely to "strengthen." Buffett's one warning is the
  past danger of **loss of focus** (Gillette once explored for oil) — now guarded against by
  its current management.
- [1997] 48,000,000 shares; market **$4,821M** (cost $600M); look-through share **$82M** (8.6%).
  In the convertible-preferred retrospective, Gillette is the triumph — "the $600 million we put
  into Gillette in 1989 had appreciated to **$4.8 billion**" — alongside [[First Empire State]]
  ($40M → $236M), both long since converted to common (contrast the once-troubled [[USAir]] and
  [[Salomon]]).
- [1998] **96,000,000 shares** (a 2:1 split of the 48M); market **$4,590M** (cost $600M). Held
  unchanged — one of the two top holdings (with [[Coca-Cola]]) Buffett left alone while trimming
  smaller positions.
- [1999] 96,000,000 shares held; market **$3,954M** (down from $4,590M) on "disappointing
  operating results" — one of the lagging investees in Buffett's worst year. Look-through share
  **$53M** (9.0% owned). Held unchanged; "we still like these businesses."
- [2000] 96,000,000 shares held; market **$3,468M** (down again from $3,954M) — the stock's
  multi-year drift continued. Look-through share **$51M** (9.1% owned). Still held untouched. *(The
  position would be cashed out via the 2005 Gillette–Procter & Gamble merger.)*
- [2005] **Merged into [[Procter & Gamble]]** (closing Q4 2005) — Berkshire's 96M Gillette shares (a
  9.7% stake) became **100M P&G shares** (market $5,788M), forcing a **$5.0B GAAP capital gain**
  Buffett called "**meaningless… ignore it**" (no tax paid; he meant to keep the P&G shares). Buffett
  credits CEO **[[Jim Kilts]]** with reviving Gillette before the merger — and recalls the earlier
  **Duracell** acquisition as a case where "what Gillette received in business value… was not
  equivalent to what it gave up," destroying billions (see [[Executive Compensation]]). The Gillette
  preferred-vs-common decision remains his self-judged "biggest mistake" of the four 1989 preferreds.
  *(The franchise bet now lives on inside [[Procter & Gamble]].)*
