# GEICO

GEICO (the Government Employees Insurance Company) is the low-cost auto insurer
that runs as one of the great recurring stories of the letters — first a stock
holding, eventually a wholly owned Berkshire subsidiary. Its low-cost edge comes
from direct marketing to carefully selected drivers, bypassing the agency system.

In the [[1977]] letter GEICO appears as a marketable-securities holding of the
insurance group: 1,986,953 shares of convertible preferred (cost $19.4M, market
$33.0M) and 1,294,308 shares of common (cost $4.1M, market $10.5M). Buffett had
bought into GEICO during its 1976 near-bankruptcy, when the company had badly
under-reserved and over-expanded. The position reflects his **[[Acquisition Criteria]]**
applied to a troubled but fundamentally low-cost franchise he understood well —
GEICO had been a formative early investment for Buffett since the 1950s.

## Evolution
- [1977] Held as preferred + common stock by the insurance group, bought during
  GEICO's 1976 crisis. Combined cost ~$23.5M, market ~$43.5M.
- [1979] Now Berkshire's **largest equity holding**: 5,730,114 common shares, cost
  $28.3M, **market $68.0M**. Buffett groups GEICO with [[SAFECO]] as Berkshire's
  "very large indirect interest in two truly outstanding management groups."
- [1980] Largest non-controlled holding: 7.2M shares (~33%), cost $47M, **market
  $105.3M**; est. share of earning power ~$20M vs ~$3M dividends. Voting rights held
  by an independent party (per DC/NY insurance regulators), so it's *not* equity-
  method "investee" despite >20%. Buffett's definitive framing: GEICO was never a
  true turnaround but "an extraordinary business franchise with a localized excisable
  cancer," a designed **low-cost** auto insurer whose advantage survived the mid-70s
  crisis; [[Jack Byrne]] was the surgeon. Buybacks cut GEICO's shares from 34.2M to
  21.6M. "GEICO represents the best of all investment worlds."
- [1981] Market value leapt to **$199.8M** (cost still $47M). GEICO's market move
  produced over half of Berkshire's $124M (~31%) net-worth gain for the year. Buffett
  judges GEICO, "by virtue of its extreme and improving operating efficiency, in a
  considerably more protected position than almost any other major insurer" — "a
  brilliantly run implementation of a very important business idea."
- [1982] Market value **$309.6M** ($79M of the year's $208M gain); the ~35% stake
  represents ~$250M of premium volume (more than all of Berkshire's own direct
  volume). Held as the prime example of the commodity-business exception — "a wide
  and sustainable cost advantage." [[Lou Simpson]] introduced as GEICO's investment
  manager; Bill Snyder joins Jack Byrne in management. "Let Jack Do It" the corporate
  creed.
- [1983] Market value **$398M**. While Berkshire's own insurance posted a 121
  combined ratio, GEICO "shot the lights out," writing at **96 vs. the industry's
  111**. A $21M **special distribution** (a proportional tender that kept Berkshire's
  ~1/3 stake constant, taxed as a dividend) flowed through earnings. The ~1/3 interest
  represents ~$270M of premium volume (~80% larger than Berkshire's own). [[Lou Simpson]]
  "the class of the field"; Byrne, Snyder, Simpson "some team."
- [1984] Stake 36%; market value $397M (held roughly flat as the stock, having
  overshot in prior years, "under-performed the business" — exactly as Buffett had
  warned could happen). Another proportional redemption. Buffett's lesson: "we look
  to business performance, not market performance." Names GEICO's **major, sustainable
  competitive advantage** in low-cost auto/home insurance — but notes profitability
  "has eluded GEICO in all endeavors other than its core business."
- [1985] 38%-owned; market value soared to **$595.95M**. Excellent premium growth and
  investment results; underwriting slipped (industry-wide auto/home deterioration) but
  still beat nearly all rivals. **[[Jack Byrne]] left** mid-year to head
  [[Fireman's Fund]], leaving Bill Snyder as Chairman and [[Lou Simpson]] as Vice
  Chairman — Buffett praises Byrne for finding successors "as valuable as his own."
- [1986] **41%-owned**; an outstanding year. Combined ratio **96.9** (personal lines,
  beating an industry that improved far less than commercial lines), premium volume
  **+16%**, share count down 5.5% via buybacks. Berkshire's share of GEICO premium
  now **>$500M** — nearly double three years earlier. Market value of 6.85M shares
  **$674.7M** (cost $45.7M). Introduces the **[[Economic Moat]]** metaphor: GEICO's
  rock-bottom cost (expense + loss-adjustment = 23.5% of premiums, ~15 pts below many
  rivals) is "a kind of moat that protects a valuable... business castle," which
  Chairman **[[Bill Snyder]]** keeps widening. Prints [[Lou Simpson]]'s 1980–86 equity
  record vs. the S&P 500 ("a little embarrassing for me... to chronicle Lou's
  performance"). One of three **permanent** holdings (see [[Permanent Ownership]]).
- [1987] 6,850,000 shares, cost $45.7M, **market $756.9M**; a permanent holding under
  Bill Snyder and [[Lou Simpson]]. The compounding made vivid: GEICO bought 1976–80 at
  ~**$6.67**/share now earns **$9.01**/share after tax. The position contributes little
  reported income (dividends only) but large [[Look-Through Earnings]].
- [1988] **44%-owned**; market value of the unchanged 6.85M shares **$849.4M**. The
  year's worry is regulatory: California's **Proposition 103** (a voter initiative
  forcing auto rates down below cost) threatens GEICO, which draws ~10% of premium from
  the state — and, if copied elsewhere, could socialize auto insurance. Buffett's point:
  unlike mediocre insurers, GEICO has "a huge amount of **[[Economic Goodwill]]** at
  risk" because it earns high returns as a low-cost producer — "in turn, so do we."
- [1990] **48%-owned**; market value **$1,110.6M** (one of five holdings left untouched
  amid "lethargy bordering on sloth"). The star example of the new **cost-of-float**
  framework (see [[Insurance Float]]): GEICO "customarily operates at an underwriting
  profit," so its float has "an effective cost of considerably less than zero" —
  "policyholders, in aggregate, pay the company interest on the float rather than the
  other way around," from "extraordinary operating efficiency and… careful classification
  of risks."
- [1991] **48.2%-owned**; 6,850,000 shares (cost $45.7M) now worth **$1,363.2M** — past
  the $1B mark. Look-through share of earnings $69M. Held unchanged among the permanent
  holdings.
- [1992] A **5:1 stock split** lifts the holding to **34,250,000 shares** (48.1%; cost
  $45.7M / market **$2,226.3M**). **Hurricane Andrew** hit GEICO with a net loss of ~$50M
  (Berkshire's look-through share ~$25M), pulling its look-through contribution down to $34M
  (excluding realized capital gains, "which have been both recurring and significant").
  Buffett reiterates GEICO as the model low-cost operation he admires (alongside Wal-Mart,
  Nucor, Dover, Golden West, and Price Co.).
- [1993] 34,250,000 shares (**48.4%**); market **$1,759.6M**; look-through share rebounded to
  **$76M** (excluding the recurring, significant realized capital gains). Held unchanged.
- [1994] 34,250,000 shares; ownership **crosses 50% (50.2%)** as GEICO's own buybacks shrink the
  share count; market **$1,678.3M**; look-through share $63M. *(The move past 50% foreshadows the
  1995–96 purchase of the whole company.)*
- [1995] **Berkshire buys the whole company.** It agreed to pay **$2.3 billion** for the ~50% it
  didn't own (closing right after year-end), making GEICO — the **7th-largest U.S. auto insurer,
  3.7M cars** — a wholly owned subsidiary. "That is a steep price. But it gives us full ownership
  of a growing enterprise whose business remains exceptional for precisely the same reasons that
  prevailed in 1951." Buffett recaps the **45-year arc** (Ben Graham's chairmanship → the 1951
  [[Lorimer Davidson]] tutorial → his 350-share stake sold for a lesson → the 1976 [[Jack Byrne]] rescue → ~33% in 1980 → ~50% via buybacks). Two managers come with it: **[[Tony Nicely]]** (insurance) and **[[Lou Simpson]]** (investments, 22.8%/yr vs. 15.7% S&P over
  1980–95, and a ready Berkshire successor-investor). The expense ratio fell to **23.6%** —
  "**GEICO's moat widened in 1995.**" The deal **adds ~$3B of float** (the first float Berkshire
  *paid* for, unlike its internally generated float). At year-end the still-separately-listed 34.25M
  shares were marked at **$2,393.2M**.
- [1996] **First full year as a wholly-owned subsidiary** (GEICO consolidated January 2,
  1996), and "all [our high expectations] are being exceeded." An accounting oddity: the
  "step acquisition" to 100% forced Berkshire to **write down** its original 51% by
  **$478.4M** (rules required marking it below both its prior market value and the value
  of the newly-bought 49%) — even though the stake's *economic* value rose with the tax
  efficiencies of full ownership. Operationally a banner year: **voluntary auto policies
  grew 10%** (vs. an 8% best ever in the prior 20 years), accelerating through the year
  and led by the underdeveloped nonstandard market; underwriting targets were beaten. The
  **virtuous circle** is stated cleanly — low cost → low price → retention → **1M+
  referrals/yr ≈ half of new business** → lower acquisition cost → still-lower cost — and
  the aim is "not to widen our profit margin but rather to **enlarge the price advantage
  we offer customers**." A sharply-focused **[[Executive Compensation|incentive plan]]**
  (installed right after purchase) ties bonuses for [[Tony Nicely]] and dozens of
  executives to just **two variables** — growth in voluntary auto policies and
  underwriting profit on "seasoned" (>1-year) business — shunning "lottery-ticket"
  Berkshire options; the 9,000 associates' profit-sharing hit a record **16.9% ($40M)**.
  [[Lou Simpson]] beat the S&P by **6.2 points** (paid on four-year investment results,
  separately from underwriting). Buffett recalls GEICO was valued at **$7M** when its
  cost advantage first attracted him in 1951; Berkshire paid **$2.3B** for the last 49% in
  1995. "The economies of scale … should allow us to maintain or even **widen the
  protective moat**" (see [[Economic Moat]]).
- [1997] **"GEICO is flying."** [[Tony Nicely]]'s "best year": voluntary auto policies
  in-force grew **16%** (up from 1996's 10%), and **new** voluntary business nearly tripled
  over five years (354,882 in 1993 → **913,176** in 1997; in-force 2.0M → 2.95M). Underwriting
  profit was **8.1% of premiums** — *above* the deliberate **~4% target**, so Buffett **cut
  rates** to "pass on most of the benefits of our low-cost operation to our customers." The
  profit-sharing payout, off the chart again, hit a record **26.9% ($71M)** for 10,500
  associates (up from 16.9% / $40M) — "merit badges, not lottery tickets." For 1998 GEICO
  will "step on the gas": **marketing spend >$100M (+50%)** against just a **3% market share**
  in a **$115B** industry — underwriting margin will fall but growth should accelerate. Buffett
  again honors **[[Lorimer Davidson]]** (now 95), who taught him insurance in 1951 — "the huge
  rewards … would not have materialized had it not been for his generosity and wisdom."
- [1998] "**GEICO … simply shot the lights out**." Marketing spend reached **$143M** (from $33M
  in 1995; counselors 652 → 2,162) — and Buffett pledges "**no limit**" (≥$190M in 1999), since
  the only test is creating ">$1 of value per dollar spent." New auto policies hit **1,317,761**
  and in-force **3,562,644**. The 1998 underwriting margin of **6.7%** was "better than we wished,"
  so rates were cut 3.3% (toward the 4% target). Profit-sharing reached **$103M / 32.3% of salary**
  for 9,313 associates — "may well [be] the highest percentage payment at any large company"
  (comp is **50% seasoned-book earnings, 50% policyholder growth**, deliberately excluding loss-
  making first-year business so associates chase new business). Market share rose only **3% →
  3.5%** — "for every policyholder we now have, there are another ten." Buffett frames GEICO as the
  proof of Berkshire's **environment**: [[Tony Nicely]] need never think about "financing, credit
  ratings or 'Street' expectations for earnings per share" (see [[Decentralization]]).
- [1999] "**Exceptional progress.**" Market share in personal auto rose **2.7% → 4.1%**; new
  voluntary policies hit **1,648,095** and in-force **4,328,900** (the customer base grew
  766,256, throwing off **$590M of cash** — each household sends ~**$1,100/year**). Marketing
  reached **$242M** (vs. $33M in 1995; 2,631 counselors) and will climb to **$300–350M** in 2000
  — "we would happily commit **$1 billion annually** … if the last dollar … produce[d] new
  business at an attractive cost." Acquisition costs are rising (media rates up; "diminishing
  returns" as everyone advertises) but a rising **closure ratio** offsets it, and GEICO's
  **renewal cost is the lowest among national auto insurers** — "Others may copy our model, but
  they will be unable to replicate our economics." As predicted, the **underwriting margin fell**
  (auto-industry loss costs rising; underwriting profit just **$24M** vs. $269M in 1998); the comp
  plan's two variables still drove a **$113.3M / 28.4%** profit-sharing payout. Apparent
  retention "declines" are an artifact of a mix shift toward standard/non-standard policies and a
  surge of new customers. Buffett: GEICO advertising is "the **best investment Berkshire can
  make**."
- [2000] **A stumble.** New auto policies *fell* to **1,472,853** (from 1,648,095) — though in-force
  still grew to **4,696,842** — and Buffett admits error: "I told you last year that we would step
  up … advertising … and that the added dollars were the best investment GEICO could make. **I was
  wrong**" (the extra spend "did not produce a commensurate increase in inquiries," and the
  conversion rate fell, spiking acquisition cost). Four causes: ad-frequency overreach, "low-hanging
  fruit" picked, stricter underwriting + rate increases, and — most important — **State Farm** (19%
  share) holding prices down despite an **18%-of-premium auto underwriting loss (~23% float cost)**
  vs. GEICO's 4% (a 6.1% float cost). Buffett salutes State Farm as "one of America's greatest
  business stories" (*The Farmer from Merna*) but insists GEICO's **low-cost model** means it "will
  inevitably increase its market share." Profit-sharing fell to **8.8% / $40.7M**. [[Tony Nicely]]
  cut ineffective advertising and pushed rate increases — "we are in good hands with Tony."
- [2001] **A profit rebound amid a flat policy count.** Premium volume grew **6.6%**, float grew
  **$308M** to **$4.25B**, and GEICO earned a **$221M underwriting profit** — "we were actually
  paid that amount… to hold the float." The lone disappointment was the inability to add
  policyholders: preferred customers (81% of the book) grew 1.6% but standard/non-standard fell
  **10.1%**, so total policies in-force slipped **0.8%**. Marketing spend was held to **$219M**
  ("Tony and I are eager to commit much more… but at the moment we cannot see how to do so
  effectively"). [[Tony Nicely]] is "an owner's dream" — GEICO was the one Berkshire insurer
  comfortably clearing the post-9/11 bar that "each of our insurance operations, save one, must
  deliver an underwriting profit."
- [2002] "**Everything went so well… we should pinch ourselves.**" Growth was substantial, profits
  outstanding, retention up, and sales productivity jumped — an **underwriting profit of $416M**.
  Premiums reached **$6.9B** (from $2.9B at the 1996 full acquisition), saving policyholders ~$1B/yr
  vs. average competitors; **internet new business +75%**; profit-sharing **19%** for 19,162
  associates. **[[Tony Nicely]]** "has been in love with GEICO for 41 years — ever since he went to
  work for the company at 18." A pointed footnote on doing business only with strong reinsurers: a
  1981–83 GEICO umbrella/product-liability venture took just **$72,000** of net premium yet produced
  **$94.1M** of losses (≈130,000%), **$90.3M** of it uncollectible from "deadbeat reinsurers."
- [2003] "A fine insurance company when [[Tony Nicely]] took over in 1992; now it is a **great
  one**." Premium volume grew **$2.2B → $8.1B** over his tenure and personal-auto market share
  **2.1% → 5.0%**, paired with "outstanding underwriting" (**$452M** underwriting profit). Both
  inquiries and the closure rate rose in 2003: **preferred policies +8.2%, standard/non-standard
  +21.4%**. Growth drove a new customer-service center in **Buffalo** (2,500 jobs), brokered by Gov.
  Pataki and Buffalo News publisher **[[Stan Lipsey]]**.
- [2004] **The low-cost model, in full historical sweep.** Buffett recounts GEICO as Berkshire's
  answer to commodity insurance via the **low-cost route** (the complement to NICO's ebb-and-flow
  discipline): Leo Goodwin's **$200,000** 1936 start (eliminate the agent), the growth to a 4%
  share by 1975, the 1976 near-failure, **[[Jack Byrne]]**'s rescue, a diversification detour, and
  **[[Tony Nicely]]**'s ascent — projecting a **~6% market share in 2005**. GEICO **re-entered New
  Jersey** after a 2003 reform (commissioner Holly Bakke), reaching **140,000 policyholders** fast.
  2004: customers saved ~$1B, profit-sharing **$191M / 24.3%**, underwriting profit **$970M**.
- [2005] **The productivity engine — and the rescue act of a hurricane year.** In a disaster-ridden
  year, GEICO's **$1,221M underwriting profit** carried Berkshire's insurance to costless float
  despite $3.4B of hurricane losses. "One statistic stands out: in just two years, GEICO improved
  its productivity by **32%**" — headcount **−4%** while policy count **+26%**. Auto policies in
  force grew **12.1%**, lifting market share **5.6% → 6.1%** (each point ≈ $1.6B of sales);
  advertising rose to **$502M** (from $31M in 1996) at GEICO's highest-ever conversion rate. New
  Jersey (entered 2004) had the highest new-policyholder retention of any state. "If you have a new
  son or grandson in 2006, name him **Tony**" ([[Tony Nicely]]).
- [2006] **"Simply extraordinary."** Policies grew **5.7M → 8.1M (+42%)** over 2003–06 while
  full-time-equivalent employees *fell* 3.5% — a **47% productivity gain** — preserving GEICO's
  low-cost position even as advertising rose to **$631M** (from $238M in 2003). Underwriting profit
  **$1,314M**; float $7,171M. Don Keough's follow-up to the "name him Tony" joke: shareholders
  should "**change the names of their present children to Tony or Antoinette**."
- [2007] "**The widest moat of any of our insurers.**" Market share reached **7.2%** (from 2.5% at
  the 1995 control purchase); advertising up to **$751M** (from $31M); sales $12B; underwriting
  profit **$1,113M** (float $7,768M). New growth fronts: **motorcycle** (share 2.1% → 6% in three
  years), ATVs, RVs, and the **first commercial-auto policy** (GEICO + National Indemnity). Tony
  Nicely, 64, "feels GEICO is just getting started. So do I."
- [2008] **Recession tailwind.** As Americans focused "on saving money as never before," GEICO
  lifted market share **7.2% → 7.7%** and rose to the **#3** U.S. auto insurer (behind only State
  Farm and Allstate; it was #7 at the 1995 control purchase). Tony Nicely, in his **48th year**,
  pushed productivity from **299 to 439 policies per employee** (over five years). "Tony and I feel
  like **two hungry mosquitoes in a nudist camp** — juicy targets are everywhere": **January 2009
  set a monthly policyholder-growth record** (to be beaten in February). Motorcycle policies +23.4%
  (share ~6% → 7%); RV/ATV growing; commercial auto entered. Underwriting profit **$916M** (float
  $8,454M). Ajit Jain's crew also runs the new muni-bond insurer [[Berkshire Hathaway Assurance|BHAC]].
- [2009] Share reached **8.1%** (from 2.5% at the 1996 control purchase — a net +7M policyholders);
  float $2.7B → **$9.6B**; profitable in **13 of 14** Berkshire years; ad budget ~$800M (≈2× the
  runner-up). Tony Nicely, 66, "still tap-dances to the office." Underwriting profit **$649M** (float
  $9,613M). Buffett warns 2010 growth may slow (vehicle registrations down; more uninsured drivers).
  **The credit-card confession:** Buffett's own idea to issue a GEICO credit card was "**a very
  expensive business fiasco entirely of my own making**" — ~$6.3M of operating losses plus a $44M loss
  selling the $98M receivables book at 55¢. GEICO's managers had warned him: "I subtly indicated that
  I was older and wiser. **I was just older.**" (See [[Mistakes Ledger]].)
- [2010] **The book-vs-intrinsic-value case study — "the gift that keeps giving."** Buffett uses GEICO
  to teach how intrinsic value can dwarf book: the 1996 purchase of the second half ($2.3B) implied
  $4.6B for 100% on $1.9B of tangible net worth, so **~$2.7B was goodwill** — ≈97% of GEICO's then-$2.8B
  premiums. Premiums are now **$14.3B**, but accounting keeps the goodwill frozen at **$1.4B** while its
  real economic value is **~$14B** (and rising). Share **8.8%** (from 2.0% when [[Tony Nicely]] took over
  in 1993); underwriting profit **$1,117M** (float $10,272M); ad spend ~$900M; a personal-lines agency
  sold 769,898 new policies (+34%). The 60-year arc retold (Ben Graham → the janitor → [[Lorimer Davidson|Davy]]). (See [[Economic Goodwill]], [[Intrinsic Business Value]].)
- [2011] **Share 9.3%; Tony Nicely's 50th year.** Over Nicely's 18-year tenure as CEO, share grew
  **2.0% → 9.3%** — had it stayed static, premium would be ~$3.3B rather than the **$15.4B** attained
  in 2011 (underwriting profit $576M; float $11,169M). "The extra value created by Tony and his
  associates is a major element in Berkshire's excess of intrinsic value over book value." With "**more
  than 90% of the auto-insurance market left**," Buffett bets on continued gains: "every day more
  Americans discover that the Gecko is doing them a favor."
- [2012] **Share 9.7%; "I count GEICO twice."** Share grew **2.5% → 9.7%** since the 1995 control
  purchase (premium $2.8B → **$16.7B**); Tony Nicely (51 years of service) and 27,000 associates. GAAP
  underwriting profit **$680M** — but understated by a one-time **$410M accounting charge** (no cash
  effect), which "simply widened the already huge difference between GEICO's intrinsic value and… book."
  GEICO also absorbed its **largest single loss ever — Hurricane Sandy** (46,906 vehicles, 3× its
  Katrina loss, reflecting its NY-metro market share) and still profited. Gains in **persistency** and
  **closures**: "a sustained gain in persistency of a bare one percentage point increases intrinsic
  value by more than $1 billion."
- [2013] **Now the #2 U.S. auto insurer** (having "recently passed Allstate"; it was #7 in 1996).
  Underwriting profit **$1,127M** (float $12,566M); Tony Nicely, 52 years of service. Buffett frames
  GEICO's **low-cost moat** — "an enduring one — that competitors are unable to cross" — and updates the
  goodwill: the $1.4B paid above tangible net worth in 1995 stays frozen on the books, but **true
  economic goodwill is "approaching $20 billion."** "With our latest reduction in operating costs,
  [the gecko's] story has become even more compelling."
- [2014] **Market share 10.8%** (from 2.5% at the 1995 control purchase); underwriting profit
  **$1,159M** (float $13,569M); Tony Nicely, 53 years of service — "There is no better manager than
  Tony." Buffett re-states the **low-cost moat**: "an enduring one — that competitors are unable to
  cross." In Munger's anniversary essay, GEICO's quadrupled market share since 1995 is named a pillar
  of [[The Berkshire System]]'s insurance success.
- [2015] **Market share 11.4%** (from 2.5% in 1995); float $15,148M. Underwriting profit fell to
  **$460M** (from $1,159M) as **accident frequency and severity rose** industry-wide — a margin dip, not
  a moat problem; GEICO kept gaining share via its low-cost advantage ("an enduring [moat]… competitors
  are unable to cross"). Tony Nicely, 54 years of service — "There is no better manager than Tony… in
  the 40 years that I've known him, his every action has made great sense." The gecko's line: "15
  minutes could save you 15% or more on car insurance."
- [[2016]] **Market share ~12%** (from 2.5% at the 1995 control purchase); employment grew 8,575 →
  **36,085**; underwriting profit **$462M** (float $17,148M). [[Tony Nicely]] completed **55 years** of
  service (joined at 18; CEO since 1993) — "There is no better manager than Tony." GEICO's **growth
  accelerated dramatically in H2 2016**: as industry-wide auto loss costs rose unexpectedly and some
  rivals lost their appetite for new customers, GEICO instead *accelerated* its new-business push — "**We
  like to make hay while the sun sets, knowing that it will surely rise again.**" The low-cost
  **[[Economic Moat]]** restated: "GEICO's low costs create a moat — an enduring one — that competitors
  are unable to cross."
- [[2018]] **Tony Nicely retires — the "GEICO and Tony Nicely" tribute.** **[[Tony Nicely]] stepped down
  as CEO on June 30, 2018** (after 57 years at GEICO, 25 as CEO), handing the role to long-time partner
  **[[Bill Roberts]]**; Nicely stayed Chairman. Buffett's career scorecard: GEICO is now **America's #2
  auto insurer**, sales up **1,200%** since 1995, **$15.5 billion of pre-tax underwriting profit since the
  1996 buyout**, and float grown **$2.5B → $22.1B** — Nicely's stewardship "increased Berkshire's
  intrinsic value by **more than $50 billion**." The half-interest bought in 1976 had cost just **$47
  million** ("about what you might pay today for a trophy apartment in New York"). 40,000 associates.
- [[2020]] **The founding story, in "A Tale of Two Cities."** Buffett retells GEICO's birth as a parable
  of American entrepreneurship: **Leo and Lillian Goodwin** founded it in **1936** in Washington, D.C.,
  with **$100,000**, convinced auto insurance could be sold **directly** at a far lower price — "tak[ing]
  on giant insurers possessing 1,000 times… their capital." He was "exposed to the company's potential a
  full 70 years ago… It instantly became my first love (of an investment sort)." The scale of compounding:
  GEICO did **$238,288** of business in 1937, its first full year — and **$35 billion** in 2020. At 84, it
  is "constantly fine-tuning — but not changing — the vision of Leo and Lillian."
- [[2024]] **"Reshaped in a major way" by [[Todd Combs]].** GEICO led a record year for Berkshire
  insurance. Buffett: "**In five years, Todd Combs has reshaped GEICO in a major way, increasing efficiency
