# USAir

USAir Group, Inc. was one of the three convertible preferreds Berkshire bought in
[[1989]] — and the one Buffett would later hold up as his cautionary tale about the
airline industry's miserable economics. Berkshire bought **$358 million** of USAir
preferred with a **9.25% dividend**, mandatory redemption in ten years, and conversion
into common at **$60 per share**.

Buffett was explicit that this was *not* a business he understood the way he understood
[[Gillette]]: "we have no ability to forecast… the airline industry… we're agnostics,
not atheists." The investment's structure as a preferred was precisely a hedge against
that uncertainty — designed to "return us our money plus dividends" even if industry
economics proved harsh, while sharing upside if the common did well. The bet rested
heavily on CEO **Ed Colodny**, whom Buffett liked, admired, and trusted, and on USAir's
unrestricted voting rights for Berkshire. In the event, the airline industry's
structural unprofitability nearly turned the investment into a loss (USAir suspended the
preferred dividend in the early 1990s) — a chastening episode Buffett revisits with
self-deprecating candor in later letters before the position was ultimately salvaged.

## Evolution
- [1989] $358M 9.25% convertible preferred (convert at $60; 10-yr redemption). CEO Ed
  Colodny. Bought as a preferred *because* Buffett can't forecast airline economics
  ("agnostics, not atheists") — the structure hedges an industry he doesn't love.
- [1990] Marked down to "**substantially less**" than cost — the worst of the four
  preferreds. Buffett's mea culpa: "your Chairman displayed exquisite timing: I plunged
  into the business at almost the exact moment that it ran into severe problems… an
  'unforced error.'" Trouble came from the Piedmont-merger integration (now fixed by Ed
  Colodny and Seth Schofield) and, worse, "**kamikaze pricing**" across the industry: "in
  a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than
  your dumbest competitor." He still expects it to "work out all right… unless the
  industry is decimated." See [[Mistakes Ledger]].
- [1991] **The industry was, in fact, decimated.** Buffett's 1990 hedge — "unless the
  industry is decimated during the next few years" — came true: Midway, Pan Am, and America
  West all entered bankruptcy in 1991 (Continental and TWA within 14 months). USAir is given a
  **low valuation** ($232.7M vs. $358M cost) reflecting "the risk that the industry will
  remain unprofitable for virtually all participants." Bankrupt carriers wage a
  "burn-the-furniture-to-provide-firewood" fare war (charging below cost, funded by asset
  sales, encouraged by courts) that topples marginal carriers in a domino effect. New CEO
  **[[Seth Schofield]]** (2/1991) fights for survival; Buffett notes the industry "has posted
  a net loss since its birth after Kitty Hawk."
- [1992] Marked at **$268.5M** (vs. $358M cost). "Industry economics had soured before the ink
  dried on our check"; airlines behaved like "a competitive tontine… they wish to bring to
  its conclusion as rapidly as possible." Buffett praises [[Seth Schofield]]'s "truly
  extraordinary" repositioning — especially his courage in **accepting a fall-1992 strike**
  (settled in days) rather than locking in uncompetitive wage costs that doomed any high-cost
  carrier. In early 1993 USAir took **British Airways'** substantial minority investment, and
  Buffett and Munger **joined the USAir board** (his fifth — "more than I believe advisable for
  an active CEO," but warranted when a large owner "should do their bit"). The standing lesson:
  "a competitively-beset business such as USAir requires far more managerial skill than does a
  business with fine economics… [yet] the near-term reward for skill in the airline business is
  simply survival, not prosperity."
- [1994] **The "gold medal" mistake fully ripens** (see [[Mistakes Ledger]]). USAir **suspended
  the preferred dividend in September 1994**, and Berkshire wrote the $358M investment down to
  **$89.5M (25¢ on the dollar)** — a $269M charge routed through the income statement (net worth
  already reflected the markdown). Buffett owns it as "**sloppy analysis**… caused by the fact
  that we were buying a senior security or by hubris": he'd failed to grasp that in "an
  unregulated commodity business, a company must lower its costs to competitive levels or face
  extinction." [[Seth Schofield]] "has worked diligently" on costs against a moving target
  (rivals' labor concessions and bankruptcy "fresh-start" costs — Herb Kelleher: "Bankruptcy
  court for airlines has become a health spa"). The writedown reflects both a chance of full
  recovery and of going "worthless." Buffett and Munger **leave the USAir board**. Redemptive
  maxim: "You don't have to make it back the way that you lost it."
- [1995] A cautious upgrade. "The company's results improved in 1995, but it still faces
  significant problems." The preferred proves "**structurally well-designed**": though no dividends
  have been paid since June 1994, the arrears **compound at 5% over prime**. Carried at **60% of
  par** (no public market), though a junior USAir preferred — inferior in every respect but
  conversion price — was trading higher (later up to 97% of par): "Let's hope the market is right."
  Buffett: "we feel much better about our USAir preferred than we did a year ago, but your guess
  is as good as mine as to its ultimate value." *(The position was ultimately salvaged.)*
- [1996] **The reprieve arrives.** Under CEO Stephen Wolf, USAir turned profitable in the second
  half of 1996 and resumed paying — **$47.9M** of arrears, the unpaid 9.25% dividend having
  compounded at the contract's **prime+5% "penalty dividend"** (13.25–14%), "one thing I got
  right." The preferred is now worth roughly its **$358M par**; lifetime dividends collected total
  **$240.5M** (incl. $30M received in 1997). Buffett still indicts his original analysis as
  "**superficial and wrong**" — he was "beguiled by the company's long history of profitable
  operations … [and] overlooked" that deregulated revenues would meet a regulation-era cost
  structure ("If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of
  librarians"). Twice (1995 at 50% of par, early 1996 at ~$335M) he tried and failed to sell —
  "You're lucky: I again failed in my attempt to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory." Richard
  Branson on how to become a millionaire: "Start as a billionaire and then buy an airline."
- [1997] **"Borders on the miraculous."** Survival "is no longer an issue": US Airways made up all
  dividend arrearages during 1997 "adding extra payments to compensate us for the delay," and the
  common rocketed from a low of **$4 to a recent high of $73**. The preferred was **called for
  redemption on March 15, 1998** — but the surging stock made the **conversion rights**, "which we
  thought worthless not long ago," valuable: "It is now almost certain that our US Airways shares
  will produce a decent profit … and the gain could even prove indecent." Buffett credits two
  changes — he and Munger leaving the board, and **Stephen Wolf** becoming CEO ("the second event
  was the key … phenomenal"). His self-mocking verdict: "I have compiled a record that is
  unblemished by success" (wrong to buy, wrong to try selling at 50¢). "Next time I make a big,
  dumb decision … **Phone Mr. Wolf**."
