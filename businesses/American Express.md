# American Express

American Express enters the letters in [[1980]] as a historical analogy rather than
a current holding: Buffett likens [[GEICO]]'s mid-1970s crisis to "that of American
Express in 1964 following the salad oil scandal." Both were "one-of-a-kind
companies, temporarily reeling from the effects of a fiscal blow that did not
destroy their exceptional underlying economics" — a franchise with "a localized
excisable cancer," to be distinguished from a true turnaround.

The reference reaches back to one of Buffett's most celebrated early investments:
in the 1960s, through the Buffett Partnership, he bought heavily into American
Express when the salad-oil scandal crushed its stock, betting the payments and
travelers-cheque franchise was undamaged. Amex returns much later as one of
Berkshire's largest permanent equity holdings (built up in the 1990s), making this
1980 mention an early thread to a major future position.

## Evolution
- [1980] Cited as the historical parallel to GEICO — a great franchise hit by a
  one-time scandal (1964 salad oil), not a broken business.
- [1991] Berkshire re-enters Amex — not yet as common stock but via a **$300M "PERC"**
  (Preferred Equity Redemption Cumulative stock), an unusual fixed-income security paying
  **8.85%** that *must* convert ~three years out into a capped maximum of 12,244,898 common
  shares (value ceiling $414M, no floor; conversion extendable one year if the common is below
  $24.50). This is the toehold that grows into one of Berkshire's largest **permanent equity
  holdings** later in the decade — the major future position the 1980 entry foreshadowed.
- [1994] **Amex becomes a major equity holding.** The 1991 PERC converted and Berkshire bought
  more, lifting the stake to **27,759,941 shares (~10%; cost $1.36B / market $818.9M)** — one of
  only two positions enlarged in the year (with [[Coca-Cola]]). Buffett recounts the full arc:
  in the mid-1960s he put **~40% of the Buffett Partnership** into Amex amid the **salad-oil
  scandal** (over 5% of the company for $13M) — "the largest investment the partnership had ever
  made." The compounding frame: Amex earned **$12.5M in 1964 and $1.4 billion in 1994**. He also
  notes the **IDS** unit (renamed **American Express Financial Advisors**, ~⅓ of earnings),
  which he first bought in 1953 at a P/E of 3 — "a long-term familiarity with a company and its
  products is often helpful in evaluating it." First look-through contribution: $25M.
- [1995] **The one top holding Berkshire increased** in an otherwise "Rip Van Winkle" year — to
  **49,456,900 shares (~10%; cost $1,392.7M / market $2,046.3M)**. A long-held conviction position
  with decades of personal history behind it.
- [1996] 49,456,900 shares (**10.5%**) held; market **$2,794.3M** (cost $1,392.7M); look-through
  share of earnings **$132M** — among the larger contributors. Held untouched.
- [1997] 49,456,900 shares (**10.7%**); market **$4,414.0M** (cost $1,392.7M); look-through share
  **$161M**. Buffett tells the **Percs back-story**: the 1991 $300M Amex Percs were due to convert
  in August 1994, and he was "lean[ing] toward sale" until a round of golf at Prouts Neck with
  **Frank Olson** (Hertz CEO, "intimate knowledge of the card business") convinced him on the
  second green that "Amex's corporate card was a terrific franchise" — "on the back nine I turned
  buyer," building to 10%. The stake now shows a **$3 billion gain** ("110% luck, the balance
  skill"), and Buffett praises CEO **Harvey Golub**.
- [1998] **Slightly increased** to **50,536,900 shares** — "one of our three largest commitments"
  (the other two left unchanged); market **$5,180M** (cost $1,470M). Amex was the only top holding
  Buffett added to in a year when his portfolio actions (notably the McDonald's sale) actually
  *reduced* the gain.
- [1999] The standout among the holdings in a poor year: 50,536,900 shares (**11.3%**); market
  surged to **$8,402M** (cost $1,470M) — second only to Coca-Cola. Look-through share **$228M**,
  the largest of any investee. Held unchanged.
- [2000] **151,610,700 shares** (a 3:1 split of the 50.5M; ~11.4%); market **$8,329M** (cost
  $1,470M); look-through share **$265M** — again the largest of any investee. Held untouched.
- [[2018]] **The buyback-compounding case study.** Buffett uses Amex to show how investee repurchases work
  for a patient owner: Berkshire's Amex holding had been **unchanged for eight years** (still 151,610,700
  shares; market $14,452M), yet its **ownership rose from 12.6% to 17.9%** purely because Amex repurchased
  its own (underpriced) stock. As a result, Berkshire's share of Amex's $6.9B of 2018 earnings was **$1.2
  billion — about 96% of the $1.3 billion originally paid** for the entire stake. "When earnings increase
  and shares outstanding decrease, owners — over time — usually do well." In the retained-earnings table,
  Berkshire's 2018 share: **$237M dividends vs. $997M retained**.
- [[2021]] Held unchanged at 151,610,700 shares, but ownership rose to **19.9%** on Amex's continued
  buybacks; market value **$24,804M** (cost still $1,287M) — a position whose ownership percentage keeps
  compounding without Berkshire lifting a finger (see [[The Power of Retained Earnings]]).
- [[2022]] **"The Secret Sauce," part two.** Berkshire's Amex purchases were "**essentially completed in
  1995 and, coincidentally, also cost $1.3 billion**"; the annual dividend grew from **$41 million to $302
  million**, and the stake was worth **$22 billion** at year-end (~5% of Berkshire's net worth). Paired
  with [[Coca-Cola]] as proof that "it takes just a few winners to work wonders" — and that, by contrast, a
  flat $1.3B bond would now be a trivial 0.3% of net worth (see [[Concentration]]).
- [[2023]] **Untouched — and now earning back its whole cost.** Held unchanged again; Berkshire's **share
  of Amex's 2023 earnings considerably exceeded the $1.3 billion cost** of the long-ago purchase. Amex's
  dividend was set to rise ~16% in 2024. Paired with [[Coca-Cola]] as Buffett's example of sticking with a
  "truly wonderful business" (~4–5% of net worth each). "Could I create a better worldwide business than
  these two enjoy? As Bertie will tell you: 'No way.'"
