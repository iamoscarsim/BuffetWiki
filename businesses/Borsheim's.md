# Borsheim's

Borsheim's is the Omaha jewelry store Berkshire bought an **80% interest** in early
1989 (announced in the [[1988]] letter) — a near-perfect sibling to the
[[Nebraska Furniture Mart]] in both economics and family. It is run by the **Friedman
family**, headed by [[Ike Friedman]], who are literally Mrs. B's relatives: Borsheim's
matriarch Rebecca Friedman was [[Rose Blumkin]]'s sister, having made her own perilous
escape from Russia (through Latvia) in 1922.

Buffett frames the deal as correcting a 1983 oversight — when buying NFM he "blundered…
by neglecting to ask Mrs. B… 'Are there any more at home like you?'" Louis Friedman
bought the small store in 1948; his son Ike joined in 1950, followed by Ike's son Alan
and sons-in-law Marvin Cohn and Donald Yale. The business runs on the identical
formula Buffett prizes: **a single store with huge inventory and enormous selection,
daily top-management attention, rapid turnover, shrewd buying, and incredibly low
expenses** — letting it offer "everyday prices that no one in the country comes close
to matching." His maxim for the customer who "can judge neither quality nor price":
"If you don't know jewelry, know the jeweler."

True to the Berkshire pattern, the purchase took no audit, no inventory count, no
verification — "Ike simply told us what was so… and on that basis we drew up a one-page
contract and wrote a large check." As Borsheim's reputation spread, customers came from
across the country.

## Evolution
- [1988] 80% interest acquired (closed early 1989). The Friedman family (Ike, Alan,
  Marvin Cohn, Donald Yale) — Mrs. B's relatives — run it on the NFM formula; bought on
  a one-page contract with no audit. "If you don't know jewelry, know the jeweler."
- [1990] Sales **+18%** in a poor retail year — likely the **largest U.S. jewelry volume
  after Tiffany's NY flagship**, from a single Omaha store serving a metro of only ~600,000.
  Growth comes from out-of-state customers, many via a national **mail-order "honor
  system"** (~1,500 assortments sent in 1990, up to hundreds of thousands of dollars each;
  "we have yet to experience a loss from customer dishonesty"). The engine: operating costs
  ~**18% of sales vs. ~40%** at typical competitors — "Just as Wal-Mart… sells at prices
  that high-cost competitors can't touch… so does Borsheim's. What works with diapers works
  with diamonds." Ike's mother **Rebecca Friedman (89)** is "in the store most afternoons,
  Wall Street Journal in hand."
- [1993] **Leadership transition:** in early 1994 **[[Susan Jacques]]** (34) was named
  President and CEO, having risen "in 11 years from a $4-an-hour job that she took at the store
  when she was 23" — a Berkshire meritocracy story. (The [[Ike Friedman]] era gives way to
  Jacques's long stewardship.)
- [1995] An excellent year — **sales up 11.7%** under [[Susan Jacques]] (36), "a terrific
  leader." Buffett distinguishes Borsheim's from the newly acquired [[Helzberg's Diamond Shops]]:
  the two are "an entirely different sort of operation" and "will operate independently of each
  other."
- [1989] A stellar first year with Berkshire — sales rose to "considerably better than
  twice" their level four years earlier. The single-location flywheel: huge volume → vast
  selection + an **expense ratio ~⅓** that of comparable jewelers → far lower prices →
  more volume; up to **4,000 visitors a day** in season. [[Ike Friedman]] ("managing
  genius… has only one speed: fast-forward"); the Santa Fe story — he brought $20M of
  jewels to a presentation and reassured Buffett, "the jewels aren't in the safe." Joins
  the **[[Sainted Seven]] Plus One**.
