# Nebraska Furniture Mart

The Nebraska Furniture Mart (NFM) is the Omaha home-furnishings store Berkshire
bought 90% of in [[1983]] — Buffett's archetype of a low-cost retail franchise and
the start of his celebrated partnership with **[[Rose Blumkin]]** ("Mrs. B") and her
family. Founded by Mrs. B in 1937 with $500 of savings, by 1983 NFM generated over
**$100 million of sales annually from a single 200,000-square-foot store** — more
furniture, carpet and appliances than all Omaha competitors combined, and the
highest-volume home-furnishings store in the country.

NFM's edge is a self-reinforcing low-cost loop: "they buy brilliantly, they operate
at expense ratios competitors don't even dream about, and they then pass on to their
customers much of the savings." Buffett calls it "the ideal business — one built
upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional
economics for its owners," and says he'd "rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with
Mrs. B and her progeny." Berkshire bought 90%, leaving 10% with family managers and
optioning 10% to key younger family members — Mrs. B's son [[Louie Blumkin]]
(president, "the shrewdest buyer of furniture and appliances in the country") and his
three sons. The deal, like later Berkshire acquisitions, was done on a handshake of
trust, with minimal due diligence.

## Evolution
- [1983] 90% acquired. >$100M sales from one store; the model low-cost retail
  franchise. Run by the Blumkin family.
- [1984] Sales **$115M** from the single Omaha store. Buffett quantifies the
  low-cost edge vs. Levitz: operating expenses **16.5% of sales vs. 35.6%**, saving
  customers ~$30M/year. The famous handshake recalled: Berkshire wrote Mrs. B a
  **$55M check** with no audit, no inventory, no title check — "she gave us her
  word." Mrs. B's grandsons Ron, Irv, and Steve now contribute. Her creed: "sell
  cheap and tell the truth."
- [1986] Net sales **+10.2% to $132M** (vs. $44M a decade earlier), the one-store
  territory still widening on its low-price reputation; warehouse capacity being
  expanded by a third. [[Rose Blumkin]], Chairman at **93**, "continues to outsell and
  out-hustle any manager I've ever seen," at the store seven days a week — "competing
  with her represents a triumph of courage over judgment." Buffett's quip: file away
  that Omaha board chairmen haven't peaked at 93 "before you mark your ballot at the
  2024 annual meeting."
- [1987] Net sales **$142.6M (+8%)**. [[Rose Blumkin]] at **94**, "gathering speed" —
  Buffett "persuaded the Board to scrap our mandatory retirement-at-100 policy." Customers
  saved ~$30M by buying from NFM; an anonymous "A Competitor" letter begs NFM to raise
  prices ("leaving 10% to 20% on the table"). Louie and his three sons (Ron, Irv, Steve)
  praised as a team where "the whole is far greater than the sum of the parts." Maxim:
  "Sell cheap and tell the truth." One of the **[[Sainted Seven]]**.
- [1988] [[Rose Blumkin]] at **95** ("the boss for 51 years… old age will always be ten
  years away"). A new detached 20,000-sq-ft **Clearance Center** opened. When Dillard's
  entered Omaha it pointedly declined to sell furniture: "We don't want to compete with
  them… they are about the best there is." NFM is the Omaha World-Herald's largest ROP
  advertiser — Buffett's proof that "it truly does pay to advertise." The 1989 purchase
  of sister-family business [[Borsheim's]] grew directly out of NFM. Pre-tax $18.4M.
- [1989] Record sales and excellent earnings, but a "sad note": **[[Rose Blumkin]] (96)
  quit in May** after disagreeing with the family over the carpet department, and opened
  a competing carpet/furniture store across the street. Buffett backs the family on the
  merits — carpet sales had lagged the store's other departments — and the result vindicates
  them: the **carpet department's Omaha market share hit 75.3%** (up from 67.7%), "over
  six times that of its nearest competitor," run by Louie, Ron, and Irv Blumkin. NFM
  again the local paper's largest ROP advertiser (linage +20% off a record 1988).
- [1990] Sales **$159M (+4%)** from the one store — "close to double the volume of any
  other home furnishings store in the country." Operating costs **15%** vs. ~40% at Levitz
  (the largest U.S. furniture retailer) and 25% at Circuit City; major chains "steer clear
  of Omaha." A Des Moines survey ranked NFM #3 of 20 furniture retailers there — though
  NFM's store is **130 miles away** (customers driving the Washington-to-Philadelphia
  distance past local alternatives). Buffett's "**counterrevelation**" on *synergy* (long
  "the last refuge of scoundrels"): a See's candy cart placed in the Mart outsold some
  full See's stores in California. Run by Louie, Irv, and Ron Blumkin.
- [1992] Pre-tax earnings **$17.1M**. **[[Rose Blumkin]] reconciles with Berkshire**: at
  year-end she sold the building and land of her competing operation to NFM and agreed that
  NFM would set up shop alongside her — "a major addition to its furniture business" — while
  she keeps running her carpet section at the same location. "I am delighted that Mrs. B has
  again linked up with us… whether she was a partner or a competitor… partner is better."
  This time Buffett secured a **non-compete** (which he'd neglected when she was 89).
- [1993] Sales reached **$200 million** — "a larger amount by far than that recorded by any
  other home furnishings store in the United States" — as Buffett marks the tenth anniversary of
  the handshake deal ("her word was good enough for us"). [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] turned **100**
  on December 3, 1993.
- [1994] NFM opened the **Mega Mart** — "a true retailing marvel" selling electronics,
  appliances, computers, cameras and audio gear, adjacent to the main store on the 64-acre site;
  "sales have been sensational since the opening." [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]], now **101**, works
  daily in "Mrs. B's Warehouse" ("She never misses a day at the store — or… an hour"). Pre-tax
  earnings $17.4M.
- [1995] NFM's **Irv Blumkin** played matchmaker for Berkshire's acquisition of **[[R.C. Willey Home Furnishings]]** (Utah's leader, comparable in volume to NFM) — telling CEO [[Bill Child]]
  how pleased the Blumkin family had been with Berkshire. The deal showcases the compounding value
  of Berkshire's manager network. Pre-tax earnings $29.7M (with R.C. Willey from 6/29/95 in the
  Home Furnishings segment).
- [1996] A **record $265 million** of sales from the single Omaha store. The Home Furnishings
  segment (with R.C. Willey) earned **$43.8M pre-tax** (up from $29.7M). [[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]] is
  **103**, still on the floor with an oxygen tank on her cart.
- [1998] NFM's "multi-stored complex" did **$300 million** of business, "an unrivaled breadth of
  merchandise — furniture, electronics, appliances, carpets and computers — all at can't-be-beat
  prices." The Home Furnishings segment (with R.C. Willey and Star) earned $72M pre-tax.
- [1999] Again **>$300 million** from the single 72nd Street location — "in a metropolitan area of
  675,000 … an absolute miracle." The shareholder-weekend "**Berkshire Weekend**" did $7.98M (+26%
  over 1998). With Jordan's added, the Home Furnishings segment earned $79M pre-tax; Berkshire is
  now #1 in furniture in NE, UT, ID, TX, MA and NH. Buffett thanks Louie, Ron and Irv Blumkin "for
  getting me started in the furniture business and for unerringly guiding" the four-chain rollup.
- [[2020]] **The founding parable retold — and a COVID record.** In "A Tale of Two Cities," Buffett
  recounts **[[Rose Blumkin|Mrs. B]]**'s arc: a Russian émigré who reached Seattle in 1915 unable to read
  or speak English, saved **$2,500** to start the store in 1936, and — though by year-end 1946 NFM's net
  worth was only **$72,264** with **$50** of cash — built "a retailing miracle" once son **[[Louie Blumkin| Louie]]** returned from the war (Omaha Beach after D-Day; a Purple Heart at the Battle of the Bulge).
  Berkshire bought 80% in **1983**, "again without an audit"; the third and fourth Blumkin generations run
  it today (Mrs. B "worked daily until she was 103"). NFM now owns the **three largest home-furnishings
  stores in the U.S.**, **each setting a sales record in 2020** despite a six-week COVID closure. Mrs. B's
  holiday song never varied: Irving Berlin's "**God Bless America**."
