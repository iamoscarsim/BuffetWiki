# IBM

International Business Machines, the technology and IT-services giant — a landmark **2011** purchase
that broke Buffett's long aversion to technology stocks. Berkshire bought **63.9 million shares
(5.5%) for $10.9 billion**, making IBM one of the **"Big Four"** equity holdings (with [[American Express]], [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Wells Fargo]]) ([[2011]]). "As was the case with Coca-Cola in 1988
and the railroads in 2006, I was late to the IBM party. I have been reading the company's annual
report for more than 50 years, but it wasn't until a Saturday in March… that my thinking
crystallized" — Thoreau: "It's not what you look at that matters, it's what you see." Buffett
praised CEOs **Lou Gerstner and Sam Palmisano** for moving IBM "from near-bankruptcy twenty years
ago to its prominence today," and especially for **brilliant financial management** — wise use of
debt, cash acquisitions, and aggressive **share repurchases**.

IBM became the running illustration of the **net-buyer/buyback logic** (see [[Evolving Views — Buybacks]]): a long-term holder of a company that is retiring stock should *root for a low share
price*, since IBM's planned ~$50B of buybacks would then retire more shares and lift Berkshire's
ownership. "If repurchases ever reduce the IBM shares outstanding to 63.9 million, I will abandon my
famed frugality and give Berkshire employees a paid holiday."

The investment ultimately disappointed: IBM's operating results stalled, and Berkshire wrote down
and then sold out of the position later in the decade — a rare technology foray that did not pan out.

## Evolution
- [2011] 63.9M shares (5.5%) bought for $10.9B; a "Big Four" holding; the net-buyer/buyback case
  study; Gerstner/Palmisano praised for financial management. Buffett "late to the party," as with
  Coca-Cola (1988) and the railroads (2006).
- [2012]–[2014] Position held (the buyback thesis playing out), but IBM's operating performance
  began to lag; Berkshire later exited at a loss.
