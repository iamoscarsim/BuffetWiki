# See's Candies

See's Candies is the California boxed-chocolate maker that became Buffett's
archetype of a wonderful business: a beloved consumer brand that earns high
returns on very little incremental capital. It was purchased at the beginning of
**1972** by [[Blue Chip Stamps]] (then a Berkshire affiliate), and is run by
[[Chuck Huggins]], who Buffett repeatedly singles out for praise.

In its first appearance in the [[1977]] letter, the economics are already
remarkable: pre-tax operating earnings had grown from **$4.2 million** at the 1972
purchase to **$12.6 million** in 1977 — "with little additional capital
investment," and achieved "while operating in an industry experiencing
practically no unit growth." This combination — rising earnings without rising
capital, in a flat industry — is the essence of pricing power and a durable
franchise, and See's becomes the recurring teaching example for those ideas in
later letters.

## Evolution
- [1972] Purchased by Blue Chip Stamps; pre-tax earnings ~$4.2M.
- [1977] First mention in the letters: pre-tax earnings $12.6M with little added
  capital, in a no-unit-growth industry. Run by [[Chuck Huggins]].
- [1978–80] Steady pre-tax earnings (~$12.5M–$15.5M) flowing through the Sources of
  Earnings table.
- [1981] Pre-tax earnings jump to **$21.9M** (from $15.5M) — the pricing power of a
  durable franchise on display, still on minimal incremental capital.
- [1983] Record after-tax profit **$13.7M** on revenues of $133.5M (207 stores,
  24.65M lbs). Buffett flags two problems: rising non-raw-material costs (now being
  controlled by [[Chuck Huggins]]) and **flat poundage** (industry-wide). Source of
  the famous maxim — "**price is what you give, value is what you get**" — and the
  central example in the [[Economic Goodwill]] appendix (25% after-tax on tangible
  assets).
- [1986] Pre-tax earnings **$30.3M**; total poundage **+2%** (over 12,000 tons sold),
  with same-store poundage roughly flat after six years of decline — a strong
  Christmas "stemmed the decline." Margins held on minimal price increases, credited
  to [[Chuck Huggins]]'s cost control and customer-first culture. Cited in the
  **[[Owner Earnings]]** Appendix as the rare business where maintenance capex exceeds
  depreciation by only $0.5–1M, so reported earnings sit close to owner earnings.
- [1987] **Record volume ~25M lbs**; same-store poundage flat for a second year (an
  improvement after six years of decline). The **seasonality** is extreme — ~**85% of
  profit earned in December**. The parable of [[Chuck Huggins]] in action: culling 14
  pieces triggered "many hundreds" of angry customer letters; he reinstated the two most
  missed, answered every writer personally, and enclosed a gift certificate. One of the
  **[[Sainted Seven]]**; Huggins now 16 years at the helm.
- [1988] Record **25.1M lbs** sold; a weak October rescued by Christmas volume well above
  1987's record. Seasonality even sharper — a record **90% of full-year profit earned in
  December** ($29M of $32.5M pre-tax; "enough to make you believe in Santa Claus"). On
  putting [[Chuck Huggins]] in charge "about five minutes after we bought the company":
  "Upon reviewing his record, you may wonder what took us so long."
- [1989] Poundage **+8%** (over 27M lbs) with "excellent same-store gains — our first in
  many years." Buffett credits **newspaper advertising**: total ad spend raised $4M → $5M
  with on-target copy from agency Hal Riney & Partners, more than tripling newspaper
  spend. A neat reflexive point — at the Buffalo News Berkshire *sells* advertising; at
  See's it *buys* it, "practic[ing] exactly what we preach." [[Chuck Huggins]]'s daily,
  quality-first involvement across 225 shops makes routine excellence "look easy."
- [1990] Physical volume set a record "but only barely" — the Kuwait invasion cut Western
  mall traffic, and Christmas poundage slipped (dollar sales up on a 5% price increase),
  with margins improving on cost control. The year's set-piece: the **Albuquerque store**,
  threatened by a bad lease renewal, was saved when manager Ann Filkins rallied **263
  customers** to write and call (some threatening a mall boycott); the landlord relented,
  [[Chuck Huggins]] thanked every loyalist by name in a newspaper ad, and Christmas sales
  rose — a vivid proof of the See's-customer bond. Also Berkshire's first "**synergy**":
  a See's cart inside [[Nebraska Furniture Mart]] outsold full See's stores. Pre-tax $39.6M.
- [1991] **"Twenty Years in a Candy Store"** — the 20th-anniversary retrospective and one
  of the most important See's set-pieces. Buffett recounts the purchase math: a $40M ask
  less $10M excess cash = a **$30M** true price, on just **$7M of tangible net worth**; he
  and [[Charlie Munger]], "not yet fully appreciative of the value of an economic
  franchise," nearly killed the deal by capping their bid at **$25M** ("and we meant it").
  The payoff: 1972→1991, pre-tax profit grew from **$4.2M to $42.4M** while net worth rose
  only $7M→$25M, so just ~**$18M of reinvested earnings** funded the growth and ~**$410M**
  of pre-tax profit was distributed upstream to deploy elsewhere — the purest illustration
  of a franchise that compounds without consuming capital. 1991 sales matched 1990 in
  dollars (poundage −4%, all in the last two months) amid a California recession and a new
  "snack food" sales tax, yet profits rose 7% to a **record 21.6% pre-tax margin**. Buffett:
  "ownership of See's has taught us much about the evaluation of franchises. We've made
  significant money in certain common stocks because of the lessons we learned at See's."
- [1995] A record: pre-tax earnings rose to **$50.2M** (from $47.5M) — quietly crossing $50M for
  the first time, the steady franchise compounding on minimal capital while flashier Berkshire
  units (the Shoe Group, World Book) stumbled.
- [1999] A **record 24% operating margin** ($74M pre-tax). The cumulative scorecard since the
  **$25M 1972 purchase**: See's has earned **$857 million pre-tax** "despite … very little
  additional capital" — the canonical asset-light franchise. Buffett credits [[Chuck Huggins]]
  ("**Huggins' Law**": See's pre-tax profit in $ millions ≈ Huggins' age as a percent — ~10% at 46,
  now **100% at 74**). See's is also the lead example in the year's [[Economic Goodwill]] argument:
  its economic goodwill "has grown … for 78 years" and "probably [will] continue for at least
  another 78."
- [2005] **[[Chuck Huggins]] retires** after a **34-year** tenure that produced a "**more-than-tenfold
  increase in profits**" in a flat industry — the manager Buffett and Munger had installed "about
  five minutes after we bought the company" and "hit a home run with." **[[Brad Kinstler]]** (who had
  run Cypress Insurance and Fechheimer) takes the reins — a rare instance of Berkshire moving a
  manager between businesses.
- [2007] **The prototype "great business"** in the [[The Great the Good and the Gruesome|great/good/ gruesome]] essay. The canonical math: bought for **$25M** when sales were $30M and pre-tax earnings
  <$5M on **$8M of capital** (~**60% pre-tax**); by 2007, sales **$383M** and pre-tax profit **$82M**
  on only **$40M** of capital — so just **$32M of reinvested capital** since 1972 funded growth while
  **$1.35 billion** of cumulative pre-tax profit flowed out to buy other businesses ("See's has given
  birth to multiple new streams of cash"). Sales 16M → 31M lbs (~2%/yr) — proof a great business
  needn't grow fast. Profits **+50%** under [[Brad Kinstler]] over two years.
