# Clayton Homes

The leading U.S. **manufactured-housing** company — builder, retailer, and **financer** of
factory-built homes — acquired by Berkshire in 2003 ([[2003]]) in what Buffett called the deal with
"a more unusual genesis" than any other. **Dr. Al Auxier**'s University of Tennessee finance
students, on their annual Omaha visit, gave Buffett the autobiography of founder **[[Jim Clayton]]**;
already aware (from a painful **Oakwood Homes** junk-debt loss) of how "atrocious" the industry's
consumer financing had become, Buffett admired Clayton's relatively disciplined conduct, called CEO
**[[Kevin Clayton]]** (Jim's son), and made an offer "based solely on Jim's book, my evaluation of
Kevin, the public financials… and what I had learned from the Oakwood experience." Clayton's board
was receptive because the **securitization markets it depended on had dried up** — Berkshire's
balance sheet solved that.

**The finance model.** Manufactured-housing makers had "front-ended" income by securitizing loans;
Clayton, under Berkshire, **retains its consumer paper**, funded by Berkshire borrowing and
re-lending at **cost + 1 percentage point** ("every tub on its own bottom"). The loan book was
expected to grow to **$5B+**. Clayton agreed to buy Oakwood's assets, broadening its reach (and
turning a small profit on Berkshire's distressed Oakwood debt).

## Evolution
- [2003] Acquired (closed Aug 7) via the Tennessee-students/Jim Clayton-book story; Kevin Clayton
  CEO; the retain-the-loans, cost-plus-1% finance model; agreed to buy bankrupt Oakwood's assets.
- [2004] Clayton "prospered" while the industry shrank; its lending grew as Berkshire's AAA balance
  sheet let it fund and **buy distressed loan portfolios** from exiting lenders — a counter-cyclical
  edge in a depressed sector.
- [2005] **"The star of our finance sector"** (Kevin Clayton), the only manufactured-housing player
  earning significant money in a sector at 40-year-low sales. Clayton now **services $17B of loans**
  (from $5.4B at purchase) and **owns a $9.6B servicing portfolio** built almost entirely under
  Berkshire — funded by Berkshire borrowing publicly and re-lending at cost +1% ($83M markup in
  2005). It added Karsten (a four-plant West Coast maker) to the Oakwood-expanded manufacturing base.
- [2006] Earned **$513M pre-tax** (+ an $86M financing fee) in an industry at its **lowest unit
  volume since 1962** ("Outside of Clayton, I doubt if the industry, overall, made any money in
  2006"). Its receivables portfolio reached **$10B**; employees 14,787 (from 6,661 at purchase).
  "The driving force… is **Kevin Clayton**."
- [2007] **Record 31% market share** in an industry shrinking to 96,000 unit sales (from 131,000 in
  2003). Most of Clayton's $526M pre-tax earnings come from its **$11B loan portfolio (300,000
  borrowers)**, "performing well… delinquencies, foreclosures and losses… similar to" prior years —
  a pointed contrast with the imploding subprime mortgage market. ("Some commentators criticized its
  directors for selling at a cyclical bottom" in 2003 — they look foolish now.)
- [2008]–[2009] **Vindication in the crisis.** Clayton's borrowers kept paying far better than the
  doom-laden headlines implied, because Clayton had financed homes to people who could actually
  afford them — Buffett uses it as the rebuttal to subprime-style lending (see [[Margin of Safety]]).
- [2011] Clayton remained profitable through the housing depression even as manufactured-housing
  volumes hit historic lows — a low-cost, conservatively-financed survivor.
- [2010] **47% of a shrunken industry; the mortgage data laid bare.** Clayton built 23,343 homes — 47%
  of the industry's 50,046 (vs. an 8% share when the industry made 372,843 in 1998). Buffett publishes
  the loan book as a lesson for housing-policy reformers: **200,804 originated mortgages**, average FICO
  **648** (47% ≤640 — "questionable credits"), yet annual net losses of only **1.17%–1.86%** through the
  crisis, because Clayton took meaningful down-payments, geared fixed payments to verified income, and
  **kept the loans on its own books** ("if we were stupid in our lending, we were going to pay the
  price — that concentrates the mind"). "If home buyers throughout the country had behaved like our
  buyers, America would not have had the crisis." He keeps pressing the FHA/Freddie/Fannie financing
  bias against factory-built homes.
- [[2016]] **America's largest home builder.** Clayton delivered **42,075 units — ~5% of all new U.S.
  homes** (it builds close to half of all manufactured homes, which are ~70% of new homes costing under
  $150,000). It branched into **site-built** housing (first builder bought 2015, two more in 2016;
  ~3% of unit sales / ~14% of dollar volume expected in 2017). Pre-tax earnings **$744M**; employees up
  to 14,677 (from 6,731 at the 2003 purchase). The lend-long/borrow-short model again benefited from low
  rates. Buffett discloses the social-cost side candidly: Clayton **foreclosed on 8,304 mortgages (2.5%
  of the portfolio)** and lost **$150M** on foreclosures, while extending loss-mitigation aid (11,000 loan
  extensions; $3.4M of payments forgiven for 3,800 borrowers; 93% of helped borrowers kept their homes).
  Average monthly payment just **$587** (incl. insurance and taxes). "Clayton and Berkshire have been a
  wonderful partnership" — an unplanned synergy that surfaced after purchase ([[Kevin Clayton]]).
- [[2017]] **Doubling down on site-built homes.** Clayton bought **two conventional-home builders** —
  **Oakwood Homes** (Colorado) and **Harris Doyle** (Birmingham) — "more than doubl[ing] our presence in a
  field we entered only three years ago"; 2018 site-built volume was expected to exceed **$1 billion**.
  Manufactured homes remained the core: Clayton sold 19,168 units at retail and wholesaled 26,706 more,
  for **49% of the U.S. manufactured-home market** (~3× its nearest competitor, vs. 13% when it joined
  Berkshire in 2003). The Knoxville-neighbor friendship between the Clayton and Haslam families helped
  cement Berkshire's **[[Pilot Flying J]]** deal the same year.
