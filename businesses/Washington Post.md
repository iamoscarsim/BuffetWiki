# Washington Post

The Washington Post Company was one of Berkshire's signature long-term equity
investments, bought in 1973 at a price Buffett considered a fraction of the
business's intrinsic value. In the [[1977]] letter the insurance group held
934,300 Class B shares at a cost of **$10.6 million** against a market value of
**$33.4 million** — already a triple, and the largest gain in the disclosed equity
table.

The Post exemplifies Buffett's [[Stocks as Fractional Ownership]] thesis: an
outstanding business (a dominant newspaper plus television properties) bought
through the stock market "at very large discounts from the prices they would
command in negotiated transactions involving entire companies." It also begins
Buffett's long association with Katharine Graham and his admiration for media
franchises with local dominance.

## Evolution
- [1977] Held by insurance group: cost $10.6M, market $33.4M (bought 1973).
- [1978] Same 934,300 shares now worth $43.4M against the $10.6M cost — the
  standout gain in the equity table as the Dow went sideways.
- [1979] 1,868,000 shares (post-split), cost $10.6M, market $39.2M.
- [1980] Market $42.3M. One of four holdings named as major sources of
  [[Look-Through Earnings]].
- [1981] Market **$58.2M** on the same ~$10.6M cost — a ~5.5x gain.
- [1982–84] Continued to compound; market value ~$103M (1982) to ~$150M (1984).
- [1985] The **"triple dip"** celebrated: bought 1973 at ~¼ of business value, the
  Post's value soared, per-share value rose faster via [[Katharine Graham]]'s
  bargain-priced buybacks, and the discount narrowed — total proceeds + value reached
  **$221M** on the $10.6M cost. Buffett credits the extra ~$160M (vs. peer media
  firms) to Kay Graham's managerial skill. He left the board in 1986 (Cap Cities FCC
  conflict) but intends to hold indefinitely; warns the company's $1.8B valuation
  caps future returns.
- [1986] Held as one of three **permanent** holdings (with Cap Cities/ABC and GEICO);
  1,727,765 shares, cost $9.7M, **market $269.5M**. Buffett would not sell "even if
  these securities were to appear significantly overpriced" (see [[Permanent Ownership]]).
  Like the other two, it pays Berkshire little current income (~$9M total across the
  three), retaining earnings Buffett expects to surface later as capital gains.
- [1987] 1,727,765 shares, cost $9.7M, **market $323.1M**; a permanent holding under
  [[Katharine Graham]] and Dick Simmons. The textbook compounding example: bought 1973 at
  **$5.63**/share, the Post earned **$10.30**/share after tax in 1987 — "Mr. Market has
  proven to be a mighty good friend."
- [1988] 1,727,765 shares, **market $364.1M**; permanent, unchanged, under [[Katharine Graham]]
  and Dick Simmons whom Buffett admires "unqualified[ly]." Grouped with the other two
  permanents as facing somewhat "less favorable" industry conditions than years earlier.
- [1991] 1,727,765 shares (14.6% owned), cost $9.7M, **market $336.1M** (down from $364.1M);
  look-through share of earnings **$10M** (from $18M). Caught in the **media franchise →
  business** reclassification (see [[Evolving Views — Newspapers]]), so its intrinsic value
  declined materially — but, with [[Capital Cities]], it is the media holding Buffett is "more
  comfortable" with: superbly run by [[Katharine Graham]], nearly **debt-free**, and absent
  from the late-1980s acquisition frenzy. A permanent holding, retained despite the shift.
- [1992] 1,727,765 shares (14.6%), market **$396.95M**; look-through share $11M. Held
  unchanged.
- [1993] 1,727,765 shares (14.8%), market **$440.1M**; ~$7M annual dividends on the $10M
  cost. Twin honors for the manager: **[[Katharine Graham]] retired as chairman** and was
  elected to *Fortune's* Business Hall of Fame. The Post is also the **beta counter-example**
  of the year — a stock the academics deemed *riskier* after its 1973 price collapse, when it
  was in fact cheaper and safer (see [[Risk and Volatility]]).
- [1994] 1,727,765 shares (15.2%), market **$419.0M**; look-through share $18M. Held unchanged.
  Buffett's boyhood paper route (delivering the *Post*) supplied "about half of my initial
  investment funds" — one of the "past associations" he says feed current decisions.
- [1996] 1,727,765 shares (**15.8%**), market **$579.0M** (cost $10.6M) — back among the itemized
  over-$500M holdings; look-through share **$27M**. Held unchanged, still the highest-percentage
  ownership of any Berkshire investee.
- [1997] 1,727,765 shares (**16.5%**), market **$840.6M**; look-through share **$30M**. Cited (with
  [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Wells Fargo]]) as an investee whose **share repurchases at past low prices**
  benefited Berkshire "far more than do today's repurchases, made at loftier prices" (see [[Mr. Market]]).
- [1998] 1,727,765 shares, market **$999M** (cost $10.6M) — nearing $1B, the same share block held
  since the 1970s. Among the itemized over-$750M holdings.
- [1999] 1,727,765 shares (**18.3%**), market **$960M** (cost $10.6M); look-through share **$30M**.
  The longest-held of all Berkshire's stocks, still untouched.
- [2000] 1,727,765 shares (18.3%), market **$1,066M** (cost $10.6M) — **crossing $1 billion** for
  the first time on the same 1973-vintage share block; look-through share **$18M**. Buffett singles
  out **The Washington Post Company** for an admirable governance habit: it "**unfailingly and
  objectively reviews its acquisitions three years after they are made**" — a candid post-mortem
  almost unheard of in corporate boardrooms (where "CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth"
  for blunders).
