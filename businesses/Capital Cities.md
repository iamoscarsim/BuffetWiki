# Capital Cities

Capital Cities Communications was a broadcasting and publishing company run by
[[Tom Murphy]], whom Buffett regarded as one of the finest managers and capital
allocators in American business. In the [[1977]] letter Berkshire's insurance
group held 220,000 shares (cost $10.9M, market $13.2M) acquired that year.

Buffett uses Cap Cities to make an unorthodox argument about **non-control
ownership**: the company "possesses both extraordinary properties and
extraordinary management," its managers excel at both operations and capital
allocation, and to buy such properties directly would cost roughly twice the
stock-market price while offering no advantage. His conclusion — "we can obtain a
better management result through non-control than control" — captures his
willingness to be a passive minority owner when the people in charge are superb.

Berkshire later backed Cap Cities' 1985 acquisition of ABC with a large
investment, deepening the relationship.

## Evolution
- [1977] First buy: 220,000 shares, cost $10.9M. Praised for management quality;
  used to argue non-control can beat control when managers are excellent.
- [1978] The Cap Cities position no longer appears in the equity table; instead
  Berkshire holds 246,450 shares of American Broadcasting Companies (ABC), cost
  $6.1M / market $8.6M — an early ABC stake foreshadowing the later Cap Cities/ABC
  merger Berkshire would help finance in 1985.
- [1984] **Subsequent event (March 1985):** Berkshire agreed to buy **3 million
  shares of Capital Cities at $172.50**, contingent on Cap Cities' acquisition of
  ABC — a deal Berkshire was, in effect, helping finance. Buffett's praise of Tom
  [[Tom Murphy]] and Dan Burke ("tops in both ability and integrity") frames the investment;
  full discussion promised for 1985.
- [1985] **Purchase completed: 3 million shares at $172.50 (~$517.5M)**, financing
  Cap Cities' $3.5B acquisition of ABC. Buffett calls Murphy and Burke the best
  management of any U.S. public company ("the sort of fellows you'd want your daughter
  to marry"). An unusual **long-term voting agreement** lets Murphy (or Burke) vote
  Berkshire's block, with sale restrictions — to give the managers stability against
  "revolving-door capitalists." Famously confesses he had *sold* the same stock at
  **$43 in 1978–80**: "A little more time, please." ABC's near-term economics will be
  "unexciting"; "you can't produce a baby in one month by getting nine women
  pregnant."
- [1986] Now Berkshire's largest equity holding: 2,990,000 shares, cost $515.8M,
  **market $801.7M** — and one of the three **permanent** holdings Buffett vows never
  to sell (see [[Permanent Ownership]]). The corporate-rate cut (46%→34%) is flagged as
  a positive for Cap Cities/ABC. Buffett left the Washington Post board to avoid the
  FCC/media conflict.
- [1987] 3,000,000 shares, cost $517.5M, **market $1,035M** — Berkshire's largest
  holding. A permanent holding whose managers, Tom Murphy and Dan Burke, leave Buffett
  feeling he "couldn't be in better hands."
- [1988] 3,000,000 shares, **market $1,086.8M**; permanent holding, unchanged. Buffett
  flags that industry conditions for Cap Cities/ABC (with GEICO and WaPo) are now "range
  from slightly to materially less favorable" than five to ten years earlier, capping
  upside despite "superb management and strong properties."
- [1991] 3,000,000 shares (18.1% owned), cost $517.5M, **market $1,300.5M**; Berkshire's
  look-through share of its earnings slipped to **$61M** (from $85M). Reclassified, with the
  rest of media, as drifting from *franchise* toward *business* (see [[Evolving Views — Newspapers]], [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]]) — so its **intrinsic value declined
  materially**. But Buffett is "more comfortable" with Cap Cities and [[Washington Post]] than
  any other media company: both are exceptionally managed, "essentially free of debt" (unlike
  rivals carrying 5×+ income), and stayed out of the late-1980s bidding frenzy. Held under the
  no-sell rule for permanent holdings.
- [1992] 3,000,000 shares (18.2%), market **$1,523.5M**; look-through share $70M. Held
  unchanged.
- [1993] **Berkshire sells one-third of the position** — 1,000,000 shares at **$630** (vs. the
  1986 cost of $172.50) — leaving 2,000,000 shares (13.0%; carried cost $345M / market
  **$1,239M**). After 35% capital-gains tax the sale produced a **$297M profit**, which Buffett
  uses to show look-through earnings are *conservative*: the realized gain far exceeded the
  $152M of (14%-taxed) retained earnings the shares had been credited with over eight years. A
  rare trim of a near-permanent holding (see [[Permanent Ownership]]).
- [1994] After a **10:1 split**, the remaining stake is **20,000,000 shares** (13.0%; cost
  $345M / market **$1,705M**). The 1993 trim becomes the year's **"silver medal" mistake** (see
  [[Mistakes Ledger]]): the shares sold (10M post-split at $63) were worth $85.25 by year-end —
  $222.5M of forgone value — making Buffett "a repeat offender" after also selling at $4.30 in
  1978–80. CEO **[[Dan Burke]] retired**, thanked for steering Cap Cities/ABC to record results
  through hard times. *(Cap Cities/ABC would be acquired by Disney in 1995.)*
- [1995] **The Cap Cities chapter closes.** The 20,000,000 shares are marked at **$2,467.5M** at
  year-end; in early 1996 Berkshire **exchanged them for cash + [[Disney]] stock** (electing
  stock, expecting >20M Disney shares). Buffett reveals he **brokered the Disney/Cap Cities
  merger** at Sun Valley (a chance meeting with Michael Eisner and [[Tom Murphy]]), and delivers a
  glowing Murphy tribute — pinned to his own regret that the third he sold in 1993 for $635M would
  fetch **$1.27 billion** in the merger. A near-19-year holding ends as a large [[Disney]] stake.
