# Salomon

Salomon Inc — parent of the Wall Street investment bank Salomon Brothers — was the
target of Berkshire's largest and most-publicized 1987 investment: a **$700 million
purchase of 9% convertible preferred stock**, convertible after three years into
common at **$38 per share** and otherwise redeemable ratably over five years from
October 31, 1995. It is the first of the great convertible-preferred commitments
([[1989]] would bring Gillette, USAir and Champion) and, eventually, the source of one
of Buffett's most dramatic episodes — the 1991 Treasury-auction scandal that made him
interim chairman.

In [[1987]] Buffett frames the preferred as essentially a **medium-term fixed-income
holding with an attached conversion option**. He is candid that Berkshire has "no
special insights regarding the direction or future profitability of investment
banking" — an industry "far less predictable" than its usual commitments, which is
precisely why the participation took the form of a preferred rather than common. What
he *does* feel strongly about is the "ability and integrity of [[John Gutfreund]],"
Salomon's CEO, whom he had known since Gutfreund "played a key role in GEICO's escape
from near-bankruptcy" in 1976, and whose "service-above-self" advice to clients (steering
them from unwise but fee-generating deals) impressed him. Salomon was also Berkshire's
underwriter for its 1988 debenture issues. At year-end 1987 the preferred was carried
at 98% of par ($14M below cost).

## Evolution
- [1987] $700M 9% convertible preferred bought (convertible at $38; redeemable from
  1995). Framed as medium-term fixed income plus a conversion option; a bet on
  [[John Gutfreund]]'s integrity, not on investment-banking economics.
- [1988] Still held at cost; estimated market value improved from "moderately under" to
  "moderately over" cost. Close association with [[John Gutfreund]] "reinforced our
  admiration," though Buffett still claims "no great insights" about investment-banking
  economics — "the overwhelming portion of the preferred's value resides in its
  fixed-income characteristics, not its equity characteristics." Berkshire's RJR Nabisco
  [[Arbitrage|arbitrage]] was constrained because Buffett and Munger, as Salomon
  directors, were walled off (and then fully informed) during Salomon's RJR bid.
- [1989] Now grouped as one of Berkshire's **"four preferreds"** alongside the new
  [[Gillette]], [[USAir]], and [[Champion International]] convertibles. Salomon Brothers
  also underwrote Berkshire's $902.6M [[Zero-Coupon Bonds|zero-coupon debenture]] issue
  "in superb fashion" — and Buffett singles out Salomon as the (well-led) creator of the
  benign Treasury-"strips" market, distinct from the junk-zero abuses he condemns.
- [1990] Held (one of four preferreds); valued at year-end "about what we paid." Buffett
  corrects a press error that priced the convertibles purely on conversion value, stressing
  "most of the value… is derived from their **fixed-income characteristics**" — a floor a
  non-convertible preferred would share. (The Salomon relationship faces its crisis the
  following year.)
- [1991] **The crisis.** Following the Treasury-auction bidding scandal, [[John Gutfreund]]
  was forced out and Buffett was elected **Interim Chairman of Salomon on August 18, 1991** —
  "the events that led to my appointment" he assumes shareholders have read about. He casts
  taking the job as the inverse of buying Coke: there he was "putting our money where my
  mouth was"; at Salomon he "put my mouth where our money was." The episode doubles as a
  proof of Berkshire's [[Decentralization]] — that he could divert months to a New York
  rescue "confident that [Berkshire's] economic progress would not skip a beat." Despite "the
  company's travails," Buffett and Munger judged the **preferred rose slightly in value**
  during 1991 (lower rates plus a higher Salomon common price); year-end fair value ~$714M on
  $700M cost. Buffett notes he was simultaneously writing a letter for Salomon's own annual
  report and refers shareholders there for a company update.
- [1995] **A candid post-mortem** in the convertible-preferred review. The conversion option
  Buffett prized "must be graded as **very poor**" — the Dow and the brokerage group both doubled
  while the option stayed worthless. Yet "under some trying conditions" the preferred kept
  delivering as fixed income (the 9% dividend "currently quite attractive"); carried at $700M /
  market $728M (including a **$140M scheduled redemption** in 1995 — "a senior security that
  matures is not 'sold'"). 20% redeems each Oct 31 through 1999, leaving "four more bites at the
  conversion apple." Buffett's verdict on his original thesis: "I had 'no special insights
  regarding the direction or future profitability of investment banking.' Even the most
  charitable commentator would conclude that I have since proved my point." (See [[Mistakes Ledger]].)
- [1992] **The interlude ends.** Buffett stepped down as Interim Chairman in **June 1992**
  after ten months: "You can tell from Berkshire's 1991-92 results that the company didn't
  miss me while I was gone. But the reverse isn't true." The rescue worked — Salomon ranked
  **second of 311 firms** in *Fortune's* most-improved-reputation survey, and Salomon Brothers
  posted **record pre-tax earnings (+34%)**. He credits executives **Deryck Maughan, [[Bob Denham]], Don Howard, and John Macfarlane** ("without [their] combined efforts… the firm
  very probably would not have survived") and lead lawyer **[[Ron Olson]]** of Munger, Tolles
  & Olson. The convertible preferred is carried at $700M / fair value **$756M**.
- [1996] Salomon (now led by CEO **[[Bob Denham]]**) did "absolutely first-class" work on
  **four 1996 transactions** for Berkshire — Buffett's rebuttal to the press notion that he
  resents investment-banking fees ("dead wrong … we are delighted to make payments that are
  commensurate with performance"). The four: initiating banker on the **[[FlightSafety International]]** purchase; a small finance-sub debt placement; the **May [[Class B Shares|Class B]] offering** (517,500 shares, $565M, a record-low 1.5% commission — Salomon
  "would have made far more money … if our offering had been standard," but tailored it to
  Berkshire's goals; **Terry Fitzgerald** captained both equity deals); and a **$500M note
  issue exchangeable into Berkshire's Salomon shares** ($447.1M proceeds; each $1,000 note
  exchangeable into 17.65 Salomon shares; 3% yield to maturity, ~1.1% if exchanged). Denham
  also relayed the shareholder tip that began the FlightSafety deal.
- [1997] **The redemptive close: Salomon merges into [[Travelers Group]].** "In a transaction that
  finally rewarded its long-suffering shareholders, Salomon recently merged into Travelers Group,"
  converting Berkshire's stake into 23.7M Travelers shares (market $1,278.6M). Buffett owes "a huge
  debt to **Deryck Maughan and [[Bob Denham]]**" for saving the firm after the 1991 scandal and
  rebuilding it into "an attractive acquisition for Travelers" — "No two fit [my 'like, trust and
  admire' test] better." Final results "will be far better than I anticipated two years ago,"
  though he recalls feeling in 1991–92 "like the drama critic who wrote: 'I would have enjoyed the
  play except that I had an unfortunate seat. It faced the stage.'"
