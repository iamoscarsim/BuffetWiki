# FlightSafety International

FlightSafety International is the world's leading trainer of pilots, acquired by
Berkshire in late 1996 for about **$1.5 billion** — Berkshire's first foray into a
**capital-intensive** operating business and its largest acquisition since the GEICO
buyout. Founded in 1951 by **[[Al Ueltschi]]** while he still worked for Pan Am, it
trains pilots of single-engine planes, helicopters, jets and marine craft, and builds
the simulators they use. By 1996 it operated **41 locations** with **175 simulators**
(of aircraft from Cessna 210s to Boeing 747s, some costing as much as $19M each); about
half of revenues came from training **corporate** pilots, the balance from airlines and
the military.

The deal's origin was typically serendipitous (see [[Acquisition Criteria]]): long-time
Berkshire shareholder Richard Sercer, a Tucson aviation consultant, recognized the fit
and wrote to **[[Salomon]]** CEO Bob Denham; Buffett met Ueltschi on September 18, 1996
and "in about 60 seconds … knew that Al was exactly our kind of manager." To minimize
share issuance (see [[Issuing Shares for Acquisitions]]), the structure gave
FlightSafety holders a choice of cash or stock with terms nudging the tax-indifferent
toward cash — the result: **51% cash, 41% Berkshire A shares, 8% B shares**. Ueltschi,
79 at closing, "looks and acts about 55" and stayed on to run it: "We never fool with
success." Buffett joked that since Berkshire won't split its stock, "we will split his
age 2-for-1 when he hits 100." Charlie Munger, long mock-critical of Buffett's corporate
jet *The Indefensible*, "now rhapsodizes at the mere mention of jets."

## Evolution
- [1996] Acquired (~$1.5B), structured to limit Berkshire share issuance (51% cash / 41%
  A / 8% B). The **first capital-intensive business** in the Berkshire stable. Run by
  founder [[Al Ueltschi]]; sourced by a shareholder's tip to [[Salomon]].
- [1997] First full year in the fold; earned $139.5M pre-tax / $84.4M to Berkshire.
- [1998] Gains a sister operation: with the **[[Executive Jet]]** (NetJets) acquisition,
  FlightSafety trains EJA's ~650 pilots (at least twice a year), and the two report together
  in the new **Flight Services** segment ($181M pre-tax). "The world leader in pilot training."
- [1999] Paired with [[Executive Jet]] under "**Aviation Services**" — both "runaway leaders," FSI
  "five times or so the size of its nearest competitor," still run by founder [[Al Ueltschi]]. Its
  defining trait is how **capital-intensive** it is: "A single flight simulator can cost as much as
  **$15 million — and we have 222**," and only one person trains at a time, so capital per dollar of
  revenue is exceptionally high and margins must be too. 1999 capex was **$215M** (at FSI and its
  50%-owned FlightSafety Boeing affiliate). Flight Services segment earned $225M pre-tax.
- [2000] [[Al Ueltschi]] is **83 and "continues to operate at full throttle"** (Buffett plans to
  "split Al's age 2-for-1 when he hits 100"). Buffett uses FSI to make a **depreciation** point
  against the EBITDA crowd: it spent **$272M on flight simulators** in 2000 (a similar amount due in
  2001), and "anyone who thinks that the annual charges for depreciation don't reflect a real cost …
  should get an internship at a simulator company" — Berkshire spends amounts equal to depreciation
  "**simply to stay in the same economic place**," then more to grow. "Growth is in prospect for FSI
  as far as the eye can see."
- [2002] A **$60M pre-tax gain** from selling Berkshire's 50% interest in **FlightSafety Boeing**
  flattered "Flight Services" earnings; absent it, training earnings would have slipped with the
  post-9/11 corporate-aviation slowdown. Still "the gold standard for the industry."
- [2003] "Normal" operating earnings fell **$183M → $150M** on the corporate-aviation slump, plus a
  **$37M loss** from premature simulator obsolescence. Simulators carried at $1.2B original cost
  (3× the nearest competitor).
- [2004] Earnings rebounded as corporate aviation recovered; **283 simulators** ($1.2B cost), return
  on tangible equity up to **15.1%** (from 8.4%). Founder **[[Al Ueltschi]]** handed the CEO role to
  43-year veteran **[[Bruce Whitman]]** ("But Al's not going anywhere; I won't let him"). The
  capital intensity is stark: "as much as **$3.50 of capital investment** is required to produce $1
  of annual revenue," so utilization is "all-important." Its #1 customer is NetJets ([[Executive Jet]]).
- [2005] Earnings improved as corporate aviation kept rebounding; FlightSafety expanded to **42
  training centers**, opening a major facility at **Farnborough, England** (>$100M and 15 simulators
  when built out in 2007). **[[Bruce Whitman]]** "makes sure that no competitor comes close."
- [2007] **The textbook "good" (not great) business** in the [[The Great the Good and the Gruesome|great/good/gruesome]] essay: since the 1996 purchase, depreciation totaled $923M but
  **capex $1.635B** (273 simulators, >$12M each), so a **$509M incremental investment** lifted pre-tax
  earnings $111M → **$270M** — "an excellent but not extraordinary business… put-up-more-to-earn-more."
  Revenues +14%, pre-tax +20%; trains ~**58% of U.S. corporate pilots** (Bruce Whitman). Flight
  services overall set a record (+49% to $547M).
