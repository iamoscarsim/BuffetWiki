# Berkshire Hathaway Textiles

The textile operation is Berkshire's original business and its enduring cautionary
tale. Berkshire Hathaway Inc. was formed in 1955 by merging Berkshire Fine
Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing. As Buffett recounts in [[1977]],
in 1948 the two companies had combined after-tax earnings of nearly $18 million
and employed 10,000 people across a dozen New England mills — "an economic
powerhouse." Yet in the decade after the 1955 merger, $595 million of aggregate
sales produced an aggregate *loss* of $10 million, and net worth shrank from $53
million to $22 million by 1964.

Buffett took control in 1965 (the change that brought in [[Ken Chace]] to run the
mills) and used the textile division's cash to finance the [[1977|insurance]]
acquisitions that became Berkshire's true engine. The textile business "again had
a very poor year" in 1977 after two years of mistakenly optimistic forecasts.
Buffett defends staying in — citing the mills as major local employers, the
cooperation of workers and unions, Chace's straightforward management, and a hope
of "at least modest profits." But he frames the deeper lesson plainly: it is far
better to be in a business with **tailwinds** than one where "even very good
management probably can average only modest results." The contrast with insurance
(where mistakes can be survived) versus textiles (where good management yields
little) is the seed of his later franchise-vs-commodity thinking.

## Evolution
- [1977] Poor year again; defended on loyalty/employment grounds but acknowledged
  as a structurally weak business. Source of the "tailwinds vs. headwinds" lesson
  and the "single-year snapshot" digression on the 1955–64 decline.
- [1978] Earns $1.3M on $17M capital. Buffett gives the textbook
  [[Commodity vs Franchise Economics]] explanation: undifferentiated goods +
  capital intensity + excess capacity = inadequate returns on capital.
- [1979] Confesses the **Waumbec Mills** (Manchester, NH) purchase was a mistake
  despite being a statistical bargain bought below working capital — the basis of
  "turnarounds seldom turn" and [[Good Business at a Fair Price]]. Some Waumbec
  capacity feeds the New Bedford decorator line (the "strongest franchise").
- [1985] **Shut down** (July 1985) after 21 years. Buffett's post-mortem: the
  industry is a commodity business with global overcapacity; capital investment to
  cut costs was "illusory" because competitors did the same (the "everyone stands on
  tiptoe at the parade" trap). The **Burlington Industries** comparison ($3B capex
  over 21 years, shareholders worse off in real terms) shows that allocating capital
  brilliantly *within* a doomed industry still fails: "a good managerial record... is
  far more a function of what business boat you get into than... how effectively you
  row." Praises managers Ken Chace and Garry Morrison and the cooperative union. The
  **1986 auction** is the coda: equipment costing $13M (replaceable for $30–50M)
  fetched $163,122 gross — net below zero; looms sold for $26 scrap. Admits he should
  have "quit sooner" (faulting his heart over his intellect; Comte).
- [1980] **Waumbec operations terminated** (equipment moved to New Bedford or sold);
  Buffett: "your Chairman made a costly mistake in not facing the realities of this
  situation sooner." New Bedford looms cut ~one-third, abandoning undifferentiated
  high-volume lines; remaining operation split into independent manufacturing and
  sales divisions, with less capital employed.
