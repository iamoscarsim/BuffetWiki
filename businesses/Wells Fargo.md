# Wells Fargo

Wells Fargo & Company is the California bank Berkshire began buying in 1989–90 — its
first major banking investment and a defining example of buying a **well-managed
business amid panic**. By year-end 1990 Berkshire owned just under **10%** (5,000,000
shares for **$290 million**, the most allowed without Federal Reserve approval).

Buffett is candid that "the banking business is no favorite of ours": with assets
"twenty times equity," small mistakes "can destroy a major portion of equity," and most
bank troubles stem from the [[Institutional Imperative]] — bankers playing
"follow-the-leader with lemming-like zeal." His rule: not to buy a "poorly-managed bank
at a 'cheap' price" but "well-managed banks at fair prices." Wells Fargo qualified
because of its managers, **[[Carl Reichardt]]** and Paul Hazen — likened to [[Tom Murphy]]
and Dan Burke at [[Capital Cities]]: each pair "stronger than the sum of its parts,"
paying able people well while abhorring excess head count, attacking costs in good times
and bad, and sticking to what they understand (Thomas J. Watson Sr.: "I'm smart in spots
— but I stay around those spots").

The purchase exploited a "chaotic market in bank stocks" — fear of a California
real-estate collapse (Wells being a leading real-estate lender) drove the stock down
~50% in months, letting Berkshire buy at **under 5× after-tax (and under 3× pre-tax)
earnings** of a bank earning >20% on equity and 1.25% on assets. Buffett lays out the
downside math (a 10%-of-loans hit at 30% severity would roughly break Wells even for a
year) and welcomes it: "at Berkshire we would love to acquire businesses… that produced
no return for a year, but… could then be expected to earn 20% on growing equity." The
investment is the canonical illustration of his **net-buyer** philosophy and of buying
when "pessimism… produces" low prices.

## Evolution
- [1990] ~10% bought for **$290M** (most without Fed approval; ~⅙ in 1989, rest in
  1990) during a California-real-estate panic — under 5× after-tax earnings. Managers
  [[Carl Reichardt]] and Paul Hazen (compared to Murphy & Burke). "Buy well-managed
  banks at fair prices"; the net-buyer logic in action.
- [1991] Holding steady at **5,000,000 shares** (~9.6%; cost $289.4M / market $290.0M). The
  feared California real-estate stress showed up in the numbers: Wells delivered a 1991
  **look-through break-even** for Berkshire — dividends received were offset by the bank's
  *negative* retained earnings (heavy loan-loss provisioning) — an outcome Buffett had called
  in 1990 "a low-level possibility, not a likelihood." It was one of the unforeseen drags on
  Berkshire's 14% look-through decline. Buffett does not waver on the position.
- [1992] Berkshire **added moderately**, to **6,358,418 shares** (~11.5%; cost $381.0M /
  market $485.6M). With the worst of the California real-estate fear passing, Wells's
  look-through contribution swung back to a positive **$16M** (from −$17M). The patience
  validates the 1990 net-buyer thesis.
- [1993] Added again, to **6,791,218 shares** (~12.2%; cost $423.7M / market **$878.6M** — the
  market value nearly doubling as the California recovery took hold). Look-through contribution
  jumped to **$53M**, among the largest of any investee — the bank-panic buy of 1990–91 now
  paying off handsomely.
- [1994] 6,791,218 shares (**13.3%**), market **$984.7M** (cost $423.7M); look-through share
  **$73M**. Buffett frames the stake as a "$7 billion **Berkshire Bank**" (13% of a $53B bank)
  earning ~$100M. CEO **[[Carl Reichardt]] retired**, thanked (with [[Dan Burke]]) for bringing
  his company through tough conditions "with record earnings… and bright prospects."
- [1995] 6,791,218 shares held untouched; market **$1,466.9M** (cost $423.7M) — one of the five
  top positions left alone in "Rip Van Winkle mode."
- [1996] **7,291,418 shares** (8.0%; cost $497.8M / market **$1,966.9M**) — the holding rose as
  Wells's own buybacks shrank the share count. Look-through share **$84M**. Flagged among the
  businesses (with See's and Freddie Mac) that a California mega-quake could "hit hard" alongside
  super-cat losses.
- [1997] **Trimmed** to **6,690,218 shares** (7.8%; cost $412.6M / market **$2,270.9M**) — among
  the larger positions "modestly trimmed" in the year's ~5% net portfolio sales / bond-stock
  realignment. Look-through share **$103M**. Cited (with [[Coca-Cola]] and [[Washington Post]]) as
  an investee whose **past low-price repurchases** benefited Berkshire more than today's would
  (see [[Mr. Market]]).
- [1998] Shown at **63,595,180 shares** (cost $392M / market **$2,540M**) — the share count
  reflecting a stock split. Held among the itemized over-$750M holdings. *(Wells Fargo merged with
  Norwest in late 1998, taking the Norwest management and the Wells Fargo name.)*
- [1999] 59,136,680 shares; market **$2,391M** (cost $349M); ownership now **3.6%** (diluted by
  the larger post-Norwest share base). Look-through share **$108M**. Held among the itemized
  over-$750M positions.
- [2000] 55,071,380 shares; market **$3,067M** (cost $319M); ownership **3.2%**; look-through share
  **$117M**. One of just five itemized over-$1B holdings; held essentially unchanged.
