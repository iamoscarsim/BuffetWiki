# Executive Jet

Executive Jet Aviation (EJA) — whose **NetJets®** program Berkshire acquired in 1998 (for
**$725 million**, equal cash and stock; closed **August 7**) — sells **fractional shares
of jets** and operates the fleet for its owners. CEO **[[Rich Santulli]]** "created the
fractional ownership industry in 1986." A customer buys a portion (say 1/8th) of a jet,
which entitles it to 100 annual flight hours, then "on a few hours notice" can summon that
plane (or another at least as good) at any of 5,500 U.S. airports — "like phoning for a
taxi" — paying a monthly management fee plus an hourly rate.

At purchase EJA was "by far the largest operator in its industry": **>1,000 customers, 163
aircraft** (including 23 "core" planes EJA owns or leases to guarantee peak-demand service)
and ~**650 pilots**, all trained at least twice a year by fellow Berkshire subsidiary
**[[FlightSafety International]]**. Two structural advantages: scale (an "armada" positioned
nationwide cuts dead-head costs and blankets the country with service) and **manufacturer
neutrality** — NetJets offers Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna and Raytheon, "like a
physician who can recommend whatever medicine best fits," whereas its two competitors are
owned by manufacturers pushing a single "house" brand.

Buffett was a customer first: introduced to NetJets by H.H. Brown's **[[Frank Rooney]]**
~four years earlier, his family flew 900 hours on 300 trips and "love this service" — he
even did a testimonial ad before any thought of buying the company. After the deal he
**sold the Berkshire corporate plane** and moved all his flying to NetJets crews. On the
buy-now logic: Aunt Alice's fur coat — "*You* aren't buying it; your heirs are." In 1997
EJA "accounted for 31% of all corporate jets ordered in the world," and Buffett thought
"the potential of fractional ownership has barely been scratched." It reports in the
**Flight Services** segment (with FlightSafety), which earned $181M pre-tax in 1998.

## Evolution
- [1998] Acquired ($725M, equal cash/stock; closed 8/7/98) — the fractional-jet (NetJets)
  leader, created by [[Rich Santulli]] in 1986. >1,000 customers, 163 aircraft; pilots trained
  by [[FlightSafety International]]; manufacturer-neutral. Buffett sold the Berkshire plane.
- [1999] "Larger than its next two competitors combined." By contrast with capital-heavy
  FlightSafety, EJA's customers own the planes — so "modest pre-tax margins … can produce good
  returns on equity." Customers own **>$2B** of planes, with **$4.2B on order** (the limiting
  factor is *plane availability*); EJA takes ~**8% of all business jets** made; recurring revenue
  (management + hourly fees) rose **46%**. It is **building critical mass in Europe** — "expensive
  — very expensive — but we will spend what it takes," since "the company with the most planes in
  the air worldwide will be able to offer … the best service" ("**Buy a fraction, get a fleet**").
  Manufacturer-neutrality (five suppliers) remains the edge over single-make rivals. Buffett added a
  1/16 of a Cessna V Ultra; "**Charlie signed up** … No other endorsement could speak more
  eloquently." EJA follows a "most favored nations" pricing policy (no special deals, even for
  directors).
- [2000] Recurring revenue (management + hourly fees) grew **49%** (after 46% in 1999), though
  margins were "slim," partly from the cost of building out **Europe**. Buffett stresses **safety**
  given that he, his family, most directors and at least nine Berkshire managers fly NetJets — he
  applauds [[Rich Santulli]]'s "unusually high amounts of pilot training (an average of 23 days a
  year)," ~800 flight hours per pilot, and single-model assignment. Still **constrained by aircraft
  availability**, EJA will take delivery of **>50 new jets in 2001 (7% of world output)** and
  remains "the world leader in fractional ownership."
- [2001]–[2004] **A persistent money-loser** despite ~70–75% industry share. NetJets sold record
  planes but ran small losses (2001), then lost money outright — a small U.S. profit "more than
  offset by losses in **Europe**" (and a $32M used-aircraft inventory write-down in 2003). U.S.
  contracts grew 3,877 → 4,967 by 2004 (Marquis Jet's 25-hour card a factor); European
  management-and-flying revenue grew 88%/61%/77% across 2001–03. Rich Santulli's gold-standard
  safety/training (NetJets pilots ~18 days/yr, single aircraft type) is the reason FlightSafety's #1
  customer is NetJets and why Buffett flies it.
- [2005] **"I said this business would earn money in 2005 — and I was dead wrong."** The U.S.
  operation "dipped far into the red" (efficiency fell, costs soared), more than offsetting an
  improved European result (contracts +37%; NetJets the only large fractional operator in Europe).
  Buffett insists [[Rich Santulli]] "will solve our revenue/expense problem" without impairing
  service, security or safety. (NetJets losses become a recurring thread, peaking in the 2009 crisis.)
- [2006] **The turnaround.** Worldwide pre-tax earnings of **$143M** — **Europe finally profitable**
  under **[[Mark Booth]]** (a net 589 customers added 2005–06 after a cumulative $212M European loss),
  and a good U.S. year. Revenue from flight operations is up **596%** since the 1998 purchase. Credit
  **[[Rich Santulli]]** (and Booth); the managed fleet is worth more than its three largest
  competitors' combined.
- [2007] **A record year** — part of flight services' +49% jump to $547M pre-tax. NetJets operates
  **487 planes in the U.S. and 135 in Europe** (more than twice its three major competitors'
  combined), with **~90% of the large-cabin market**. European earnings **tripled** (Mark Booth).
  Buffett on **[[Rich Santulli]]**: "If you were to pick someone to join you in a foxhole, you
  couldn't do better than Rich… he just doesn't stop."
- [2009] **"The major problem for Berkshire last year" — and the rescue.** The recurring losses
  culminated in catastrophe: an **11-year aggregate pre-tax loss of $157M** and debt ballooning from
  **$102M at purchase to $1.9B** (a staggering **$711M loss in 2009** alone); "without Berkshire's
  guarantee of this debt, NetJets would have been out of business. It's clear that **I failed you in
  letting NetJets descend into this condition**" (see [[Mistakes Ledger]]). **[[David Sokol]]** became
  CEO in August, cut debt to $1.4B, and made the company "solidly profitable" — without undercutting
  founder [[Rich Santulli]]'s safety/service standards. "We eat our own cooking" (Buffett, his family,
  directors and managers all fly NetJets at standard prices). Dominance in the field "remains
  unchallenged."
- [2010] **The turnaround complete.** "A huge story in this sector's year-to-year improvement
  occurred at NetJets." Now charged an appropriate fee for Berkshire's credit guarantee ($38M in
  2010), NetJets still earned **$207M pre-tax — a $918M swing from 2009.** [[David Sokol]]'s "quick
  restructuring of management and… rationalization of… purchasing and spending… turned what was
  Berkshire's only major business problem into a solidly profitable operation," without undercutting
  safety/service (2010 market share 5× its nearest competitor; record customer-satisfaction). Buffett:
  he and his family have flown >5,000 hours on NetJets, "always… with the best-trained pilots in
  private aviation."
